Faced with a shortage of child psychiatrists and an increase in demand for care, mental health organizations must reinvent themselves. ### A "Question of Competency": Child Guidance, Pediatrics, and the Origins of Child Psychiatry in America
**כתב עת:** 📄 Bull Hist Med | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Ilerbaig J, Vicedo M | **תאריך:** 2026

During the 1920s and early 1930s, American psychiatrists and other experts in the field of child guidance engaged in debate about which discipline had professional competency over children's mental health. Psychiatrists contested psychologists' claims to expertise, arguing that children's behavioral disturbances were medical problems of an emotional nature. As the child guidance movement gained steam through the proliferation of child guidance clinics, pediatricians protested their exclusion from it. Some hospital-based psychiatrists echoed their claim that the adoption of psychoanalysis at child guidance clinics without a hospital connection isolated psychiatry from scientific medicine. The authors examine the early work of some of these psychiatrists, who sought to collaborate with pediatricians in a hospital framework. They developed a medical model for the nascent discipline of child psychiatry, alternative to the model practiced at child guidance clinics. Eschewing psychoanalytic interpretations, they emphasized the interaction between physical and mental health problems from early childhood.

### Instruments and scales for assessment, screening, and psychoeducational diagnosis of children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder: a scoping review
**כתב עת:** 📄 Rev Paul Pediatr | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Oliveira KS et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

ABSTRACT Objective: To investigate instruments and scales for psychoeducational assessment, screening, and diagnosis of children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder. Data source: This review followed the methodological procedures recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses - Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). Its research question was, “What are the main instruments and scales for psychoeducational assessment, screening, and diagnosis of children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder in school?”. Data synthesis: The study included 16 articles that investigated instruments for psychoeducational assessment, screening, and diagnosis of children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder. The instruments found were the Psychoeducational Profile - Revised (PEP-R), Psychoeducational Profile 3 (PEP-3), and the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC-IV). This indicates a scarcity of instruments and scales for the psychoeducational assessment, screening, and diagnosis of children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder. Conclusions: The most used instruments to assess the psychoeducational profile of schoolchildren with autism spectrum disorder include the Revised Psychoeducational Profile (PEP-R) and the Psychoeducational Profile, 3 rd Edition (PEP-3), according to scientific literature. There is a scarcity of instruments and scales for the psychoeducational assessment, screening, and diagnosis of children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder, suggesting the need for further research on the subject.

INTRODUCTION Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder with a neurobiological basis, characterized by persistent impairments in communication and social interaction, associated with restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior. 1 Initial signs can be observed between 12 and 24 months of a child’s life, manifesting as significant developmental delays or regression of previously acquired skills. 2 The etiology of ASD is multifactorial and, since it does not present biological markers, the diagnosis is predominantly clinical, based on systematic observation and information provided by caregivers. It is also recommended that standardized instruments be used to qualify the diagnosis. 3 , 4 , 5 The assessment of a child with ASD is not limited to diagnostic conclusions, but is essential for defining their psychoeducational profile and guiding individualized therapeutic and educational interventions and strategies. 1 , 3 The assessment of the student’s psychoeducational profile aims to understand their learning, cognition, motor skills, behavior, and social relationships at school and in the ecological environment, integrating educational and psychological knowledge to obtain a complete overview of child development and offer the necessary references to assist in the development of an inclusive educational curriculum. 3 According to the literature, instruments such as the Psychoeducational Profile - Revised (PEP-R) and the Psychoeducational Profile - 3 (PEP-3) prioritize the measurement of development in multiple domains, including expressive and receptive communication, cognition, motor skills, social interaction, and characteristic motor and verbal behavior, identifying strengths and weaknesses to guide educational interventions. 6 , 7 Historically, children with ASD have sometimes been treated as “untestable” due to low adherence to testing situations, bonding difficulties, and expressive and receptive communication limitations, which hinder the applicability of conventional instruments. 7 Thus, the PEP-R and later the PEP-3 were conceived as benchmarks for understanding the repertoire of skills and needs of children with ASD, supporting the definition of the level of support and the development of an individualized educational and clinical plan, as well as the possibility of short, medium, and long-term follow-up. 7 The timely and thorough identification of ASD is a structural basis for implementing interventions capable of enhancing development and improving the quality of life of children and adolescents. However, the psychoeducational assessment of this population requires duly validated instruments and scales, sensitive to the specificities of the disorder and able to reliably screen, support diagnosis, and monitor outcomes with adequate adaptation to different cultural and educational contexts. Nevertheless, the literature still has a limited collection of validated measures for psychoeducational screening, diagnosis, and characterization, especially in specific settings. This weakens the standardization and reliability of practices and limits the conduct of interventions rigorously anchored in scientific evidence. This scoping review aimed to identify instruments and scales for screening, assessment, and psychoeducational diagnosis of children and adolescents with ASD at school and to provide an updated overview to support education and health professionals in choosing appropriate instruments, contributing to the development of contextualized and effective practices. ### Automated screening of autism via a pretrained vision-language model in naturalistic caregiver-child interactions
**כתב עת:** 📄 Nat Commun | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Deng R et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 16

Early autism screening is essential for timely intervention but remains constrained by specialist dependence, costly protocols, and limited accessibility. We present Autism-CLIP, a vision-language model that automates screening from 3-min naturalistic caregiver-child interaction videos recorded in toddlers aged 15–24 months. By integrating contrastive learning, large language model–generated supervision, domain-specific architecture, and hybrid training, Autism-CLIP aligns visual behavioral cues with autism-related assessment outcomes, enabling annotation-free inference. In multicenter evaluation, Autism-CLIP achieved an AUC of 0.903 in the internal cohort ( n = 103) and 0.873 in the external cohort ( n = 102), outperforming conventional machine learning methods. Attention mapping highlighted clinically relevant markers, including gaze aversion and limited social reciprocity. Requiring only smartphone-captured videos without manual coding or controlled settings, Autism-CLIP offers a scalable, low-cost approach to broaden access to early autism screening and support prioritization of clinical resources.

Here, the authors develop Autism-CLIP, a vision–language model for automated early autism screening using 3-min home videos of toddlers aged 15–24 months.

Introduction Autism, affecting 1 in 31 individuals in the United States 1 , and with a pooled prevalence of 0.70% among 6–12-year-old children in China, is characterized by social communication challenges and restricted/repetitive behaviors that impair daily functioning 2 . Early screening of autism is critical: neurodevelopmental trajectories in the first two years of life are marked by heightened neural plasticity 3 , 4 , offering a pivotal window for interventions that can substantially improve long-term outcomes 5 , 6 . During this period, subtle behavioral markers of autism—such as reduced joint attention, diminished eye contact, and delayed responsiveness to social cues—often emerge 7 , 8 . Yet, a significant proportion of children with autism remain undiagnosed before the age of 2, with only 49% having received their first evaluation by age 3 1 , underscoring systemic barriers in current screening paradigms. Conventional autism screening relies on clinician-administered tools like the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) 9 , 10 , which demand specialized training, costly resources, and controlled clinical environments. While machine learning (ML) has advanced automated analysis of structured assessments (e.g., shorten ADOS assessment 11 and pose estimation in ADOS videos 12 ), these methods remain tethered to standardized settings and clinician oversight, limiting scalability in low-resource communities. Recent innovations in mobile health platforms, such as gamified apps capturing gaze patterns or vocal prosody 13 – 15 , demonstrate improved ecological validity but retain operational complexity. For instance, tools requiring specialized-sensor setups, or user training and systematic implementation by primary providers, to balance accuracy with real-world feasibility 16 , 17 . Critically, none address the urgent need for scalable, caregiver-friendly screening in children under 2 years—a gap perpetuating diagnostic delays. Naturalistic approaches, which analyze unstructured caregiver-child interactions in familiar environments, offer a promising alternative. Prior work using the Systematic Observation of Red Flags (SORF) has shown that manual coding of home or brief parent-child interaction videos can distinguish autistic from non-autistic toddlers, including in 10-min interaction samples from children aged 15–24 months 18 , 19 . However, these approaches still depend on trained human coders and manual behavioral scoring, which limits scalability. Early machine learning models, trained on handcrafted behavioral features (e.g., eye contact, social smiles 20 , 21 ), still required manual labeling by raters. A clinically viable solution must therefore overcome four barriers: (1) compatibility with ubiquitous mobile devices, (2) usability without prior training, (3) elimination of manual annotation, and (4) robust performance in children under 2 years. Advances in computer vision provide a pathway. While traditional convolutional/recurrent neural networks (CNNs/RNNs) excel at temporal video analysis, vision-language models (VLMs) like CLIP 22 —pretrained on vast image-text pairs—enable zero-shot classification by aligning visual features with semantic concepts. However, autism screening poses unique challenges: complex semantic information, prolonged video durations, and small domain dataset sizes demand domain-specific adaptation of these frameworks. In this study, we present Autism-CLIP, a pretrained VLM fine-tuned on 3-min naturalistic videos of toddlers (15–24 months) interacting with caregivers (Fig. 1 ). By integrating diagnoses and clinical assessment scores (e.g., ADOS-T) as supervisory signals using Large Language Model (LLM) and CLIP architectures, designing domain-specific predictive networks, and employing hybrid supervision strategies, Autism-CLIP bridges the gap between generic action recognition and autism-specific phenotypic analysis, automating screening of diagnostically salient behaviors (e.g. gaze aversion) without manual coding. In multicenter validation (internal n = 103; external n = 102), Autism-CLIP achieved an AUC of 0.873 (95% CI: 0.796–0.937) and sensitivity of 0.857, outperforming state-of-the-art ML models. Attention mapping further localized autism-related behaviors (e.g., atypical social engagement), enhancing interpretability for clinical translation. This work advances the field by (1) demonstrating the first pretrained VLM adapted for autism screening in naturalistic settings, (2) validating performance in children under 2 years, i.e., a cohort underrepresented in prior ML studies, and (3) eliminating dependencies on structured tasks or manual annotation. By enabling analysis of brief, smartphone-captured videos, Autism-CLIP democratizes access to early screening, aligning with global priorities for equitable neurodevelopmental care (Supplementary Table 1 ), offering a unique approach compared to existing methods. ### Online gaming for autistic youth: Exploring the impact on participation from the perspectives of autistic adolescents and their caregivers
**כתב עת:** 📄 Aust Occup Ther J | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Hayden-Evans M et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct

Abstract Introduction The rise in the number of adolescents diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder has highlighted the need for strength‐based approaches to support them. The accessibility of online gaming has made it a popular avenue for autistic adolescents to socialise and connect with others while enhancing skill development. This study aimed to explore online gaming from the perspectives of autistic adolescents and their caregivers, including the impact gaming may have on daily participation and functioning. Methods This study employed a mixed methods research design. Both autistic adolescents and their caregivers completed online surveys comprising a combination of Likert‐scale and open‐ended questions. The survey responses were descriptively and thematically analysed. Consumer and Community Involvement Survey questions were reviewed by volunteer staff (including autistic staff members) of a local autistic community coding club to ensure readability and accessibility of the survey. Results In total, 45 participants (23 caregivers and 22 autistic adolescents) responded to the survey. Themes relating to online gaming were identified, including social communication, emotion regulation, and skill development while also highlighting caregiver concerns of online safety and excessive screen use. Most autistic adolescents reported feeling happy when playing online games and most caregivers agreed online gaming supported their child to build better relationships with others. Over half of autistic adolescents agreed that online gaming helped them feel more confident in social situations with others. Conclusion Allied health professionals, including occupational therapists, may utilise online gaming, incorporating strength‐based learning and skill development, when working alongside autistic adolescents and their caregivers to facilitate social participation and promote online safety.

PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY Online gaming is becoming more common, giving autistic young people a way to make friends and learn new skills. This study explored how online gaming affects autistic teenagers, from both their own perspective and their caregivers'. The researchers collected information through online surveys from 22 autistic adolescents and 23 caregivers. The study found that online gaming helped many autistic teenagers feel happier, more confident, and better at talking to others. Many caregivers noticed that gaming helped their child build relationships. However, some caregivers were worried about online safety and too much screen time. Occupational therapists could use online gaming to help autistic youths develop social skills.

Key Points for Occupational Therapy Online gaming utilises the unique preferences and interests of autistic adolescents which promotes strength‐based skill development. Autistic adolescents' development of social communication, fine motor, and problem‐solving skills can be supported by online gaming. Concerns of online safety, emotional regulation, and excessive screen use should be considered by allied health professionals. 1 INTRODUCTION Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition characterised by differences in reciprocal social communication, interaction and response to sensory input, and the presence of restricted or repetitive behaviours, interests, and activities (American Psychiatric Association, 2022 ; Hodges et al., 2020 ). It is estimated that 1 in 100 children are diagnosed with autism globally (Zeidan et al., 2022 ). Expanding awareness and understanding of autism has contributed to an increase in the rate of diagnosis, emphasising the need for deeper exploration into alternative mediums to provide strength‐based support to autistic adolescents (American Psychiatric Association, 2022 ; Hodges et al., 2020 ). Living in a predominately neurotypical society, there is increased pressure for autistic individuals to adapt their communication skills to participate in society (Mitchell et al., 2021 ). Adolescents may experience increased social pressure in different social contexts, where there is often a lack of understanding in communication between neurotypical and autistic individuals (Chen et al., 2015 ; Deckers et al., 2014 ). Stigmatisation and negative reception of behaviours misinterpreted by neurotypical peers may contribute to higher levels of social anxiety and reduced social participation of autistic adolescents (Briot et al., 2020 ). Expression of autistic characteristics has traditionally been discouraged and autistic adolescents may consequently ‘mask’ their true selves, negatively impacting their mental wellbeing (Cage & Troxell‐Whitman, 2019 ; Hull et al., 2017 ). In comparison with their neurotypical peers, autistic individuals may experience higher incidences of anxiety, depression, and suicidality, attributing to discrimination, social isolation, and reduced likelihood of receiving appropriate health care and support services (Croen et al., 2015 ; Shattuck et al., 2011 ). Strength‐based approaches focus on harnessing an individual's strengths and interests to build skills and positive experiences, diverging from a deficit‐focussed mindset (Lee et al., 2024 ). They challenge traditional notions of autistic traits by re‐evaluating them as potential strengths. Embedded in the social model of disability, a strength‐based approach takes a holistic view, emphasising that societal structures, not individual abilities, create barriers (Lee et al., 2020 ). Interventions can be adapted to suit autistic adolescents by leveraging their unique strengths and interests, including circumscribed interests, to increase engagement and participation (Dunn, 2017 ). The positive outcomes of this approach are credited to creating safe social environments and keeping activities interest‐based (Jones et al., 2021 ). Utilising the interests of autistic adolescents promotes a sense of accomplishment and builds confidence, empowering them to engage in different activities or challenges (Koegel et al., 2012 ). One example of strength‐based interventions is online gaming. Video games, online gaming, and technology interventions have become increasingly popular through the rise of the internet and more accessible technologies in clinical, educational, and community settings (Fam, 2018 ; Kaplan et al., 2022 ; Odom et al., 2015 ). Online games can provide social skills training, cognitive and executive function training, treatment of mental health conditions, and an alternative mode of presenting educational material to adolescents with processing difficulties (Beaumont et al., 2019 ; Bocci et al., 2023 ; Crouch et al., 2023 ; Rashedi et al., 2022 ; Zayeni et al., 2020 ). Studies show adolescents are attracted to video and online games as they are motivationally fun, social, immersive, and provide a sense of achievement (Snodgrass et al., 2017 ). However, some autistic adolescents use online games to escape from dysphoric moods and problematic relationships within the real world (Snodgrass et al., 2017 ). This can lead to negative repercussions within their daily life if not adequately addressed, including behavioural and lifestyle changes (Granic et al., 2014 ; Kaplan et al., 2022 ). Traditionally, research into online gaming interventions focussed exclusively on the perspectives of caregivers or autistic adolescents (Mazurek et al., 2012 ; Rashedi et al., 2022 ; Willis et al., 2022 ). However, there is frequently a discrepancy between these two views, with many caregivers voicing concerns about screen time and its effects on their children (Fam, 2018 ; Kaplan et al., 2022 ; Rashedi et al., 2022 ; Snodgrass et al., 2017 ).

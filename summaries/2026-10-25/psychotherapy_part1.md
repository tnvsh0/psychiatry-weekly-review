### Emotion-Focused Behavioral Weight Loss Treatment for Adolescents: A Randomized Clinical Trial
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Netw Open *(IF: 120.7)* | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Boutelle KN et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 1

IMPORTANCE: Family-based behavioral treatment (FBT) efficacy decreases in adolescence, a developmental period of gradual maturation and increasing autonomy during which emotion regulation skills develop.

OBJECTIVE: To evaluate the feasibility and efficacy of FBT alone compared with FBT plus emotion regulation across 6 months of treatment and 12 months of follow-up on adolescent weight change, emotion regulation, emotional eating, and parent weight change.

DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS: This parallel-group randomized clinical trial of adolescents 13 to 16 years of age with body mass index (BMI) in the 85th percentile or higher but less than the 99.9th percentile was conducted from August 29, 2019, to January 4, 2024, at 2 university clinics. Central randomization was completed by a statistician, and outcome assessors were blinded to treatment condition. The majority of treatments and assessments were delivered remotely via video conference due to the COVID-19 pandemic.

INTERVENTIONS: FBT plus emotion regulation vs traditional FBT skills alone for weight loss. FBT included nutrition and physical activity education, behavior change strategies, and parenting skills. Both treatments had 6 months of adolescent and parent groups and behavior coaching sessions. The combined group included FBT skills and added dialectical behavior therapy and emotion-focused therapy.

MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES: The main outcomes were adolescent weight (body mass index [BMI] z score (BMIz) and BMI as a percentage of the 95th BMI percentile [%BMIp95]), emotion regulation skills (measured by Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale [DERS] scores), emotional eating behaviors (measured by Emotional Eating Scale [EES] scores), parent weight, and feasibility and acceptability of treatment. Primary outcome analyses used an intention-to-treat approach.

RESULTS: A total of 166 (FBT plus emotion regulation = 84; FBT = 82) dyads (adolescents: mean [SD] age, 14.3 [1.2] years; 95 [57%] female; mean [SD] BMIz, 2.2 [0.6]; mean [SD] %BMIp95, 120.4 [21.4]; parents: mean [SD] age, 45.3 [6.5] years; 141 [85%] female; mean [SD] BMI, 32.6 [7.0]) were assessed at baseline, midtreatment, posttreatment, and 6- and 12-month follow-up visits. There were no statistically significant differences in posttreatment weight loss among adolescents receiving FBT plus emotion regulation compared with FBT (difference in BMIz, 0.03 [95% CI, -0.05 to 0.10]). However, adolescents in FBT plus emotion regulation did not regain as much weight at the 12-month follow-up (difference in BMIz, -0.09 [95% CI, -0.17 to -0.02]). At the 12-month follow-up, 4% (95% CI, 1%-12%) of adolescents in FBT and 22% (95% CI, 8%-48%) of adolescents in FBT plus emotion regulation lost a clinically significant amount of weight (≥0.20 BMIz). At 12 months' follow-up, parent weight (difference in BMI, 0.51 [95% CI, -0.03 to 1.05]) and adolescent emotion regulation (difference in DERS scores, -0.06 [95% CI, -0.25 to 0.50]) did not differ by treatment. By contrast, the FBT plus emotion regulation group had lower scores than the FBT group on emotional eating (difference in EES scores, -0.39 [95% CI, -0.63 to -0.15]).

CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE: In this randomized clinical trial of 166 parent-adolescent dyads, adding emotion regulation skills to FBT did not improve weight loss during treatment but was associated with less weight regain and lower emotional eating through the 12-month follow-up. These findings suggest that adding emotion regulation skills to an FBT program may attenuate weight regain and decrease emotional eating among adolescents.

TRIAL REGISTRATION: ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT03674944.

### The impact of state- and county-level anti-sexual and gender minority structural stigma on psychotherapy outcomes among a national sample of college students
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology (J Consult Clin Psychol) *(IF: 5.2)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Keefe JR et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep

OBJECTIVE: While structural stigma against sexual and gender minorities (SGMs) has been well-established to negatively impact SGM mental health, its influence on therapy outcomes for SGM living in more stigmatizing environments remains unclear.

METHOD: Using the Center for Collegiate Mental Health Standardized Data Set (student n = 416,339; institution n = 216), we examined the impact of stigma on baseline symptoms, treatment attendance, and psychotherapy outcomes among SGM compared with non-SGM students. The self-report Counseling Center Assessment of Psychological Symptoms Distress Index, a measure of general psychiatric symptoms, was our primary outcome. We examined outcomes and attendance across eight sessions of psychotherapy, using multilevel modeling. Time-varying (years 2012-2022) sexual and gender-based structural stigma composites for the institutions' states and counties were calculated using data on both public opinion and laws and policies impacting SGM individuals.

RESULTS: SGM students entered treatment with more severe Counseling Center Assessment of Psychological Symptoms Distress Index symptoms compared to non-SGM students (ds = 0.36-0.41). Higher county-level gender-related stigma predicted more severe symptoms among gender-diverse students (ps = .025-.010). SGM students attended relatively more psychotherapy sessions in counties with greater sexual structural stigma (p < .001). However, SGM students did not demonstrate meaningfully different symptom change trajectories relative to non-SGM compared to non-SGM students, and structural stigma was not associated with changes in Counseling Center Assessment of Psychological Symptoms Distress Index scores when accounting for SGM identity (all ps > .250).

CONCLUSION: Structural stigma was not associated with poorer treatment outcomes for SGM students receiving naturalistic psychotherapy at collegiate counseling centers. Rather, these results highlight the potential resilience-building effects of psychotherapy, even within stigmatizing contexts. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

### Effects of an internet-delivered behavioral self-monitoring intervention on depressive symptoms in older widowed adults: A randomized controlled trial
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology (J Consult Clin Psychol) *(IF: 5.2)* | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Stahl ST et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep

OBJECTIVE: Late-life bereavement triggers substantial change to surviving spouses' routine health behaviors. Behavioral self-monitoring interventions are promising tools for promoting change across multiple behavioral domains, yet little is known about their efficacy in the spousal bereavement period. This trial evaluated the efficacy of an internet-delivered behavioral self-monitoring intervention for reducing depressive symptoms in older widowed adults.

METHOD: We conducted an assessor-blind randomized controlled trial at a single site in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, from January 2020 to August 2025. We randomly assigned adults aged 60+ years who experienced the recent death of a spouse or life partner and had subsyndromal depressive symptoms (defined as a Hamilton Depression Rating Scale score of ≥9) to either behavioral self-monitoring or enhanced usual care. We measured change in Hamilton Depression Rating Scale at 3 months as our primary outcomes using intent-to-treat analyses. Secondary outcomes included changes in anxiety, complicated grief, and posttraumatic stress at 3 months.

RESULTS: We randomized 151 protocol-eligible patients to either our intervention or control arms. Their M (SD) age was 70.3 (6.9) years, 121 (81%) were female, and 124 (82%) completed their 3-month follow-up assessment. The intervention produced significant effect size improvements in depression, Cohen's d = 0.48; 95% confidence interval (CI) [0.1, 0.8]; p < .001, anxiety (0.57; 95% CI [0.2, 0.9]; p < .001), but not in complicated (prolonged) grief or posttraumatic stress.

CONCLUSIONS: Internet-delivered behavioral self-monitoring for older widowed adults is more effective than enhanced usual care at reducing depression and anxiety symptoms during the spousal bereavement period. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

### Basic-level category processing advantage in fear generalization and its modulation by threat-conditioned stimulus diversity: Behavioral and event-related potential evidence
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Behaviour Research and Therapy (Behav Res Ther) *(IF: 5.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Zhang P et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 16

Category-based fear generalization refers to the extension of acquired fear responses to novel stimuli sharing categorical cues with a threat stimulus. Categories are organized into superordinate, basic-level, and subordinate levels, which differ in cognitive processing advantages; however, whether this categorical advantage influences fear generalization remains unclear. Experiment 1 used a discriminative fear conditioning paradigm, employing subordinate exemplars of birds and measuring tools as the threat-conditioned stimulus (CS+) and the safety-conditioned stimulus (CS-), respectively. During the generalization test, we included generalization stimuli sharing either the same basic-level category (GS Basic ) or the same superordinate category (but different basic-level category) as the CS+. Fear generalization was assessed via shock expectancy ratings and skin conductance responses. Experiment 2 manipulated CS+ diversity (high vs. low) in the same task while recording electroencephalography to examine its moderating role. Behaviorally, acquired fear generalized more robustly to GS Basic  than to GS sharing the same superordinate category (GS Superordinate ). Participants in the high-diversity group showed higher shock expectancy ratings for both the CS+ and GS Basic  than those in the low-diversity group. Event-related potential results revealed that GS Basic  elicited P2 and N400 amplitudes comparable to those of the CS+, and exhibited more positive P2 and less negative N400 amplitudes than GS Superordinate  and CS-. Moreover, only the CS+ and GS Basic  elicited more positive LPP amplitudes than CS-. These findings suggest that early attentional allocation, late semantic processing, and motivational attention collectively constitute the electrophysiological basis of the basic-level category processing advantage in fear generalization, offering implications for the intervention of anxiety disorders.

### Incorporating Data From Text Messages and Email Into Psychotherapy: Randomized Controlled Trial
**כתב עת:** 📄 JMIR Ment Health | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Vahia IV et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 19

Abstract Background As digital communication becomes central to daily life, psychotherapy increasingly has access to patients’ electronic media data. Although digital phenotyping has been widely studied, less is known about whether incorporating personal communication data, such as text messages, improves clinical outcomes in psychotherapy. Objective This study aimed to determine whether integrating personalized text message data into psychotherapy improves depression, anxiety, health-related quality of life, and therapeutic alliance and to examine whether increased access to collateral information influences clinical decision-making. Methods The Electronic Media and its Impact on Psychotherapy (EMAP) study was a randomized controlled trial conducted in adult and geriatric outpatient and partial hospitalization settings at an academic psychiatric hospital. Participants receiving psychotherapy for primary depression or anxiety (n=101) were randomized to electronic media–enhanced therapy (EME; n=52) or treatment as usual (TAU; n=49). In EME, research staff reviewed participants’ text messages prior to sessions using personalized mood-related key terms and presented aggregated findings on a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)–compliant clinician dashboard. TAU participants received standard care without message review. Outcomes included change scores for the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), the 36-item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36), and the Working Alliance Inventory–Short Revised (WAI-SR). Linear regression and nonparametric tests compared groups. Moderation analyses assessed whether the frequency of information access influenced treatment changes. Results No statistically significant between-group differences were observed in anxiety or working alliance. Among participants with SF-36 follow-up data (n=65), EME participants demonstrated greater improvement in the pain subscale. Greater frequency of information access was associated with increased treatment-related changes in the EME group. Access to additional collateral information was associated with more clinical actions but not with improved distal outcomes. Conclusions Integrating personalized text message data into short-term psychotherapy did not significantly improve depression or anxiety, and it neither improved nor worsened therapeutic alliance. However, access to greater amounts of collateral information, including electronic media, influenced clinical decision-making, suggesting nuanced and context-dependent effects.

Increasingly, psychiatry, and particularly the realm of psychotherapy, has begun to recognize the ways in which digital data can be incorporated into care. A vast literature exists in the domain of digital biomarkers and digital phenotyping [ 2 - 5 ], where sensors and devices track behavior and, in turn, generate objective measures of activity, sleep, mood, and parameters such as medication responses and side effects. However, a simpler and more intuitive use of this digital collateral information available through digital media and communication—such as text messages—has been relatively understudied. Psychotherapy relies on patient recall of key information, events, and interactions. There is evidence to indicate that clinicians sporadically incorporate information from digital communications into therapy sessions and that including this information may have a positive impact on the psychotherapeutic process [ 6 - 8 ]. However, emerging evidence also indicates that the effectiveness of these approaches may be situational and that outcomes may vary depending on how data are presented and accessed. For instance, one study [ 9 ] found that the use of a dashboard that presented clinicians with broad metrics, such as time spent on a smartphone during the night, miles walked per week, and top searches on social media, led to no statistically significant changes in patients’ well-being scores. A follow-up study [ 10 ] also found that clinician use of such a digital dashboard did not improve well-being. Notably, both studies found that patients and clinicians alike appreciated the awareness the dashboard brought to patients’ digital use. Patients also reported feeling comfortable and positive about the dashboard experience [ 10 ]. This suggests that the impact of this approach may be subtle and nuanced, a notion that is being increasingly replicated in other studies [ 11 ]. However, neither study investigated whether there was a differential impact from evaluating more personal communication, such as email or text messages. Investigating this is relevant because Liu et al [ 12 ] demonstrated that the language in text messages strongly correlates with the severity of self-reported depressive symptoms. This finding was based on sentiment analysis of existing text messages; however, how it may translate into real-time inclusion of text messaging content into psychotherapy is unknown. There are important implications for gaining a more sophisticated understanding of how access to broader information from digital communication and media impacts outcomes in psychotherapy. Spending time in a session reviewing digital content can take away from time spent engaged directly with patients [ 13 ], and it is not clear how this may impact rapport. It is also not clear whether any benefits are empirical or situational. ### Efficacy of progressive muscle relaxation training in post-traumatic stress disorder after the Kahramanmaraş earthquake: a randomized controlled trial
**כתב עת:** 📄 BMC Psychiatry | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Karadaşlı T, Arslan M | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 19

Background The February 6, 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquake was one of Turkey’s most devastating natural disasters, causing serious psychological issues, especially Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD). Although various treatments exist for PTSD, they can be costly and sometimes ineffective. Progressive Muscle Relaxation Training (PMRT) is a low‑cost, self‑administered technique with no side effects, but randomized controlled trials on its effectiveness for post‑earthquake PTSD remain very limited. This randomized trial examined whether PMRT effectively treats PTSD in individuals after the Kahramanmaraş earthquake. Methods The study included 44 earthquake-affected individuals aged 18–65 who met at least one of the following criteria: formal DSM-5 PTSD diagnosis by a psychiatrist, or a PCL-5 score of 47 or higher (indicating clinically significant PTSD symptoms). Based on power analysis, 22 participants were assigned to each group via block randomization: PMRT and control. All received PTSD and healthy lifestyle information; those on ongoing treatment continued. The PMRT group additionally received 6 weeks (twice/week, 20–30 min/session) of telerehabilitation-based PMRT. Assessments (PCL-5, DASS-21, PSQI, FSS, SF-36) were conducted pre- and post-intervention. Time-group interactions were analyzed with two-way mixed ANOVA ( p < 0.05). Results In the PMRT group, PTSD symptom severity significantly decreased after the intervention ( p < 0.001; η² p = 0.735). In the control group, no significant changes were observed in these parameters, while sleep quality and physical health deteriorated in the control group. Conclusion PMRT was associated with greater improvements in PTSD symptoms, psychological well-being, sleep quality, fatigue, and quality of life compared to an education-only control group. Background Earthquakes are among the most destructive natural disasters, negatively affecting survivors both physically and psychologically [ 1 ]. Major earthquakes can lead to long-lasting psychological problems in exposed individuals, and one of the most common psychiatric conditions resulting from this traumatic process is Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) [ 2 ]. The most recent and striking illustration of this phenomenon is the twin earthquakes that struck near Kahramanmaraş on February 6, 2023, registering magnitudes of 7.7 and 7.6. These seismic events, separated by a nine-hour interval, had a direct impact on 11 provinces. Over 14 million individuals personally experienced the disaster, and more than 50,000 fatalities were reported [ 1 , 3 ]. Beyond the extensive destruction of life and property, the earthquakes also triggered severe psychiatric conditions among survivors, including PTSD, anxiety, and depression [ 2 – 4 ]. In a meta-analysis conducted by Çınaroğlu and colleagues, the prevalence of probable PTSD among adults after the Kahramanmaraş earthquake was reported to be 41% [ 3 ]. The long-term psychological impact of earthquakes has been well documented. Following the 1999 Marmara earthquake, the 36-month prevalence of PTSD was 19.2% [ 5 ], and a systematic review of studies conducted over 10 years after the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake confirmed that PTSD represents a substantial long-term burden among survivors [ 6 ]. The fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-5 (DSM-5) defines PTSD as a psychiatric condition arising from direct traumatic exposure, witnessing a traumatic incident, or repeatedly learning about traumatic details. This disorder encompasses four distinct symptom domains: (i) intrusive re-experiencing (such as distressing memories, flashbacks, or nightmares), (ii) persistent avoidance of trauma-related stimuli, (iii) negative alterations in thoughts and emotions (including self-blame or ongoing negative affect), and (iv) heightened arousal and reactivity (manifesting as hypervigilance or exaggerated startle responses) [ 1 , 7 ]. Current clinical guidelines prioritize trauma-focused psychotherapy for PTSD, with pharmacotherapy (primarily selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) serving as a secondary option. However, these treatments can be costly, do not always provide satisfactory results, and symptoms often follow a persistent course [ 8 ]. Nevertheless, various physiotherapy approaches such as aerobic, breathing, relaxation, stretching, and strengthening exercises can also be used in the management of PTSD [ 4 , 9 , 10 ]. Progressive Muscle Relaxation Training (PMRT) is an active relaxation technique developed by Edmund Jacobson in 1938. This technique is based on the sequential contraction and relaxation of different muscle groups [ 11 , 12 ]. PMRT is a self-administered method with no adverse effects, easy, accessible, and economical [ 13 ]. This technique increases parasympathetic nervous system activity; thus, anxiety and stress levels decrease, sleep quality improves, and physical and mental relaxation is achieved [ 11 ]. In studies conducted with different groups in the literature, PMRT has been reported to be effective in reducing depression, anxiety, and stress, as well as improving sleep and quality of life [ 11 , 13 , 14 ]. However, randomized controlled trials investigating the efficacy of PMRT in individuals who develop PTSD after disasters are quite limited [ 4 , 15 ]. Furthermore, different results have been reported regarding the effect of PMRT on PTSD symptoms in existing studies [ 4 , 15 ]. This situation necessitates further research on this topic. ### Characterizing Behavioral Engagement in Smartphone Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Obsessive Compulsive Disorder: Secondary Analysis of a Randomized Controlled Trial

Abstract Background Digital mental health interventions (DMHIs) are increasingly popular for their ability to disseminate evidence-based treatments in a scalable, accessible way. Engagement with digital treatments is critical for effectiveness but remains poorly defined and measured. Most studies rely on subjective self-reports or 1 or 2 objective metrics, overlooking the multidimensional nature of how users interact with DMHIs. Objective This exploratory secondary analysis applied a road map for comprehensive behavioral engagement measurement, developed for a prior smartphone cognitive behavioral therapy program for body dysmorphic disorder, to a 12-week randomized controlled trial of smartphone cognitive behavioral therapy for obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD; N=60). Aims were to (1) comprehensively characterize behavioral engagement using 21 objective app analytics-based variables, (2) identify distinct behavioral engagement profiles via cluster analysis, (3) assess whether profiles were differentially associated with OCD symptom change, and (4) evaluate associations between profiles and pretreatment credibility, expectancy, posttreatment satisfaction, and perceived app quality. Methods Behavioral engagement was measured via 21 objective variables derived from app analytics, spanning use volume, frequency, feature usage, session duration, and temporal patterns. Hierarchical agglomerative cluster analysis identified engagement profiles. Associations between cluster membership and OCD severity change (Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale) were examined using hierarchical generalized linear mixed models. Objective measures were complemented with subjective self-reports: namely, cluster differences in treatment credibility, expectancy, satisfaction, and perceived app quality were examined using ANOVAs and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Results Cluster analysis indicated a 3-cluster solution representing high (cluster 1: 21/60, 35%), moderate (cluster 2: 31/60, 52%), and low (cluster 3: 8/60, 13%) app users. All 3 clusters experienced significant reductions in OCD severity, with no significant differences in pre- to posttreatment Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale change across clusters. Descriptively, cluster 1 had a higher rate of treatment responders than cluster 3. While clusters 1 and 2 had similar perceptions of treatment credibility and expectancy at baseline and satisfaction at posttreatment, cluster 3 perceived the intervention as significantly less credible, had lower expectations, and reported significantly lower satisfaction than cluster 2. All clusters rated app quality as similarly high. Conclusions Comprehensive behavioral engagement measurement is feasible and informative across different DMHIs and clinical populations. The finding that all engagement profiles showed comparable symptom improvement challenges simple dose-response assumptions and may suggest that multiple paths of engagement can be therapeutically beneficial; nonsignificant differences in improvement across clusters may also reflect limited power to detect differences. Introduction Digital mental health interventions (DMHIs) have the potential to expand access to care, offering scalable and flexible treatment options. However, their effectiveness depends on user engagement, a critical yet commonly overlooked factor in digital tools [ 1 - 3 ]. Engagement is a multidimensional construct that encompasses behavioral indicators of intervention use (eg, frequency, duration, breadth, and depth) and subjective experience (eg, affective response, interest, and attentional focus) during interactions with the intervention [ 3 , 4 ]. Despite its importance, engagement measurement has historically been oversimplified: most studies use self-report measures of satisfaction or acceptability that are vulnerable to demand characteristics [ 5 , 6 ]; those that incorporate objective data typically include only 1 or 2 such variables, such as login counts or total time on the app [ 6 - 8 ]. Considering only 1 or 2 objective measures of engagement overlooks nuances of how users interact with DMHIs. For example, 2 users may log into an app the same number of times, but one may do so consistently over 8 weeks, whereas the other logs in intensively at first and then disengages. Certain intervention content, such as exposure with response prevention (ERP) for obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), may be most effective when practiced consistently over time. Thus, these 2 usage patterns reflect meaningful differences in treatment exposure, yet they appear identical when measured by login counts alone. Similarly, measuring total time on an app does not capture how time is distributed across content areas. Time spent on ERP modules may be more strongly associated with OCD symptom improvement than time spent on less therapeutically active modules. Altogether, there is value in measuring engagement comprehensively to capture quantity, quality, and context of use. We previously developed a road map for comprehensive behavioral engagement measurement in a trial of smartphone-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) [ 9 ]. Using 19 app analytics-derived variables ranging from use volume, frequency, feature usage, session duration, and temporal regularity, cluster analysis identified three engagement profiles: (1) “deep” users, who engaged selectively with content but for extended durations; (2) “samplers,” who interacted briefly with most content; and (3) “light” users, with minimal and sporadic use. Engagement profiles were not significantly associated with differential treatment outcomes, although trends suggested that deep engagement may be linked to greater improvement compared with light use. This work demonstrated a novel approach to comprehensive behavioral engagement measurement. Our initial study in smartphone CBT for BDD was limited to a single clinical condition and intervention. However, each DMHI has unique features, content, and capabilities that will drive different use patterns, and each clinical target may require different patterns of use to achieve adequate treatment response. Thus, a next step is to test whether this measurement approach adapts and generalizes to new populations and interventions. Many who do initiate OCD treatment do not receive guideline-concordant ERP [ 11 , 12 ]. DMHIs may help address these barriers, but their success hinges on user engagement. Second, CBT for OCD presents unique engagement challenges that give an opportunity to test whether our approach can be tailored to the distinctive considerations of a specific condition and intervention. Namely, ERP is aversive and may elicit disengagement [ 13 ], particularly in self-directed DMHIs, where no therapist is present to provide support and accountability.

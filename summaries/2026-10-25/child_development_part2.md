### Maternal adverse childhood experiences and epigenetic aging in pediatric populations: a scoping review
**כתב עת:** 📄 Front Child Adolesc Psychiatry | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Chanda D et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

Introduction Maternal exposure to adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) may influence aging biomarkers in pediatric populations, as estimated using epigenetic clocks or other measures of aging, such as telomere length. This scoping review aimed to identify and map the available evidence on the association between maternal ACEs and biomarkers of epigenetic aging in offspring from birth to 18 years of age. Methods A systematic search of MEDLINE, Embase, APA PsycINFO, CINAHL, Scopus, and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global was conducted on 26 May 2025 and updated on 28 April 2026. The inclusion criteria were epigenetic clock studies that included pediatric populations (0–18 years of age) born to mothers who self-reported ACEs during gestation or within 1 year after childbirth using a standard questionnaire. DNA methylation analysis was conducted using biospecimens, and offspring aging biomarkers were examined. Three reviewers independently screened 1,441 articles, of which 38 underwent full-text review. Results Four studies met our inclusion criteria. The geographic scope of the studies was limited; three studies were conducted in the United States and one in the United Kingdom. Sample sizes ranged from 80 to 883 maternal-child dyads. The age at the time of biological sample collection ranged from birth to 8 years. Measures of epigenetic aging were examined across diverse tissue types, including blood, saliva, and umbilical vein endothelial cells. Some evidence has been found for an association between maternal ACEs and offspring epigenetic aging; however, the findings were often limited to specific conditions or ACE subtypes. Total maternal ACE scores were not significantly associated with newborn gestational epigenetic age acceleration or child symptoms. However, more specific associations emerged for maternal experiences of parental divorce, sexual abuse, and neglect. In addition, sex-specific associations were also noted. Racial disparities were also evident with maternal childhood abuse linked to increased epigenetic aging in Black but not White maternal-child dyads. Some associations emerged only when considering moderators such as maternal restless sleep during pregnancy. Discussion Maternal ACEs may influence offspring epigenetic aging; however, these associations are complex, context-dependent, and supported by a limited body of evidence. Longitudinal studies with greater demographic diversity and methodological consistency, alongside mechanistic investigations of the biological pathways linking maternal adversity and offspring epigenetic aging, are needed.

1 Introduction Early life, particularly the prenatal and early postnatal periods, represents critical windows of development during which environmental exposures can have profound and lasting effects on biological systems. Exposure to adversity during these developmental periods may lead to persistent alterations in body structure, metabolic processes, neurodevelopment, and physiological function ( 1 , 2 ). Early life adversity has been associated with a broad range of adverse health outcomes across the life course, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, certain cancers, osteoporosis, neurodegenerative diseases, reproductive dysfunction, asthma, autoimmune conditions, and mental health disorders, such as depression and schizophrenia ( 3 ). Increasing evidence suggests that some of these long-term associations may arise through biological processes that influence the timing and trajectory of development and aging. 1.1 Biological aging and its measurement Biological aging is a multidimensional progressive accumulation of molecular, cellular, physiological, and functional changes that reduce homeostatic capacity and increase susceptibility to disease and mortality. Unlike chronological age, biological aging captures inter-individual variation in the functioning of biological systems. Consequently, individuals of the same chronological age may differ substantially in terms of their biological age and health risks ( 4 – 6 ). This multidimensional process involves interconnected changes in genomic stability, epigenetic regulation, cellular senescence, mitochondrial function, proteostasis, nutrient sensing, immune regulation, and communication between intercellular cells ( 7 , 8 ). Biological age is, therefore, best conceptualized as a latent construct estimated using biomarkers rather than a directly observable characteristic. Epigenetic aging is one approach for estimating biological aging. Epigenetic clocks use DNA methylation (DNAm) patterns at selected cytosine–phosphate–guanine (CpG) sites to estimate chronological age or predicted age-related phenotypes ( 9 , 10 ). Epigenetic clocks provide DNA methylation-based estimates of age or aging-related phenotypes. Deviations between estimated and expected age have been investigated as biomarkers of biological aging, although their interpretation varies according to the clock, tissue, developmental stage, and outcome. The differences between an individual's DNAm-predicted epigenetic age and chronological age can be described as epigenetic age deviation. When this deviation is operationalized as the residual difference between DNAm-predicted age and chronological age, after accounting for chronological age, it is commonly referred to as epigenetic age acceleration (EAA). Thus, EAA represents an approach to quantify epigenetic age deviation ( 9 , 11 ). Early epigenetic clocks, including the Horvath and Hannum clocks, were primarily developed to estimate chronological age ( 105 , 106 ). Subsequent studies commonly derived measures of EAA from these clocks to quantify deviations between estimated epigenetic age and chronological age or an appropriate reference population ( 9 , 11 ). Later generation clocks were developed to capture additional aging-related phenotypes, including phenotypic age (PhenoAge), mortality risk (GrimAge), and pace of aging (DunedinPACE) ( 12 – 14 ). These measures should not be considered interchangeable; PhenoAge and GrimAge are trained on phenotypic or mortality-related outcomes, whereas DunedinPACE estimates the pace of biological aging rather than the deviation between estimated and chronological age. The Pediatric-Buccal Epigenetic (PedBE) clock was specifically developed to estimate the epigenetic age of children and adolescents using buccal epithelial cells ( 15 ). Accordingly, studies of epigenetic age and epigenetic aging-related measures may use different clocks and age-deviation measures, which are not necessarily interchangeable, and their associations with environmental exposures and health outcomes may not be consistent. A systematic review of 299 publications demonstrated substantial heterogeneity across clocks and outcomes, reinforcing that epigenetic aging is not a uniform construct ( 16 ). In adults, accelerated epigenetic aging has been associated with morbidity, frailty, cognitive and physical functioning, depression, and mortality, although associations vary depending on the epigenetic clock, tissue, population, and outcome examined ( 9 , 13 , 17 , 18 ). Epigenetic age acceleration is also associated with potentially modifiable behavioral, environmental, socioeconomic, and psychosocial exposures, including smoking, adiposity, metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, environmental pollutants, and socioeconomic disadvantage ( 19 – 22 ). Physical activity, along with other favorable lifestyle and socioeconomic factors, has been associated with more favorable epigenetic aging profiles, although findings vary across different populations and clocks ( 23 – 26 ). Early life psychosocial adversity may represent an important pathway linking social exposures to chronic stress. Cumulative adversity may influence biological aging through neuroendocrine, inflammatory, metabolic, behavioral, and epigenetic pathways ( 27 ). ### The interaction between progesterone and glucocorticoid signaling on pregnancy-related mood symptoms
**כתב עת:** 📄 Int Rev Psychiatry | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Regmi P, Lee RS | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 21

Postpartum depression (PPD) is a heterogeneous disorder whose biological basis remains incompletely understood. Although the rapid postpartum decline in progesterone and its neuroactive metabolite allopregnanolone has been implicated in PPD, this hormonal transition occurs in all pregnant individuals, whereas only a subset develop depression. This review proposes a two-hit framework in which early-life adversity establishes persistent, maladaptive stress-system vulnerability and pregnancy-related progesterone exposure subsequently interacts with that altered system to increase depression risk. Adverse childhood experiences may dysregulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis through genetic and epigenetic mechanisms involving stress response genes. Progesterone and glucocorticoid signaling may converge because their receptors, which are structurally similar, recognize related hormone-response elements and regulate overlapping gene networks. In other words, progesterone may contribute to psychiatric symptoms by activating stress response genes. Associations between progesterone exposure and depression, prospective evidence linking progesterone levels with subsequent PPD and the efficacy of the gamma-aminobutyric acid. A receptor modulator allopregnanolone further support investigation of progesterone-related mechanisms. However, current evidence does not yet establish progesterone as a causal second hit. Longitudinal and mechanistic studies integrating early adversity, steroid trajectories, neuroactive-steroid metabolism, HPA-axis function and stress response gene regulation are needed to test this model.

### The role of distress tolerance and adverse childhood experiences in depression and suicidal ideation among Latinos: A moderation analysis
**כתב עת:** 📄 J Psychiatr Res | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Watkins LG et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 19

INTRODUCTION: Rates of mental health challenges are rising among Hispanic communities in the United States, yet research examining factors that shape the relationship between depression and suicidal ideation relationship in this population remains limited. The present study investigated whether adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and distress tolerance, both well-documented factors in mental health risk, moderate the association between depressive symptoms and suicidal thoughts.

METHODS: Data were collected from 800 Latino adults in Texas who completed validated assessments of depression, suicidal ideation, ACEs, and distress tolerance. Using a dual-moderation analysis, we examined interaction effects of ACEs and distress tolerance on the relationship between depression and suicidal ideation, while accounting for age, sex, and acculturative stress.

RESULTS: Depression, ACEs, and distress tolerance each demonstrated significant direct associations with suicidal ideation, with distress tolerance negatively associated. Only distress tolerance significantly moderated the depression-suicidal ideation relationship, and this association was most pronounced among participants with lower distress tolerance relative to those with higher levels. Additionally, acculturative stress was positively associated with suicidal ideation, whereas age was negatively associated, and males reported higher levels of suicidal ideation relative to females.

DISCUSSION: Findings highlight a complex interplay between depression, distress tolerance, and suicidal ideation among Latino adults. The interesting result that lower distress tolerance amplified the depression-suicide association, indicates distress tolerance may function as a protective mechanism. These results emphasize the importance of continued investigation of these dynamics within a population experiencing significant mental health challenges.

### Associations between adverse childhood experiences and breastfeeding initiation and duration: a longitudinal birth cohort study in Pakistan
**כתב עת:** 📄 BMC Public Health | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Egger EE et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Jul 23

Rationale Prior studies have found that a history of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) is associated with breastfeeding initiation and duration, but the evidence is inconsistent. ACEs are associated with poor maternal and infant health outcomes. Despite low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) carrying a disproportionate amount of poor maternal and infant health outcomes and despite evidence that breastfeeding could alleviate some of these outcomes, little research has assessed the relative impact of ACEs on breastfeeding initiation and duration of exclusive breastfeeding in LMICs. Objective The study investigates whether maternal ACEs are associated with breastfeeding initiation and exclusive breastfeeding at 3 and 6 months postpartum among a cohort of women from rural Pakistan. Methods The prospective cohort analysis included 869 women 18–45 years of age from the Bachpan Cohort Study. Multivariable modified log-Poisson regression models were used to assess the relationship between any ACE exposure with breastfeeding initiation ( n = 755) and exclusive breastfeeding at 3 ( n = 758) and 6 months ( n = 809). Models were adjusted for known confounders. Results 58% of participants reported experiencing at least 1 ACE. 17.5% of women with a history of at least one ACE reported initiating breastfeeding within an hour of childbirth. 43.8% of women with at least one ACE reported exclusively breastfeeding at 3 months postpartum and 7.6% of women with at least one ACE reported exclusively breastfeeding within the last 24 h at 6 months postpartum. There was no significant difference between groups for initiating breastfeeding. However, women who had experienced ACEs were less likely to recall exclusively breastfeeding at 3 (RR = 0.8; 95 CI = 0.7, 1.0) and 6 months (RR = 0.7; 95% CI = 0.5, 1.2) postpartum compared to women with no self-reported history of ACEs. Conclusion Our findings indicate that ACEs may contribute to differences in exclusive breastfeeding. Understanding the long-term effects of ACEs on breastfeeding is important to help inform the design of intervention programs to prevent or mediate the negative repercussions of ACEs. Introduction The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends breastfeeding as the ideal form of nutrition for infants [ 1 , 2 ]. WHO recommends initiating breastfeeding within one hour of birth [ 3 ]. Initiating breastfeeding within one hour of childbirth is pivotal because it guarantees that infants receive colostrum— the first breast milk produced for 2–4 days after childbirth [ 4 ]. Compared to other stages of breast milk, colostrum is concentrated with antibodies that provide passive immunity for babies [ 4 , 5 ]. Initiating breastfeeding within one hour of childbirth has also been shown to be associated with the continuation of exclusive breastfeeding practices [ 6 ]. Breastfeeding has been shown to enhance infant health in many ways. In low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) such as Pakistan, exclusive breastfeeding until six months can be particularly protective against malnutrition [ 7 – 10 ]. More broadly, breast milk has been associated with lower rates of a variety of childhood illnesses, such asthma and wheezing [ 11 , 12 ]. Breastfeeding is also associated with mental and emotional health benefits, such as maternal-child attachment and reduced physiological stress, anxiety, and postpartum depression for breastfeeding mothers [ 9 ]. Breastfeeding initiation and duration of exclusive breastfeeding vary widely across LMIC [ 13 ]. The World Health Organization defines exclusive breastfeeding as an infant receiving no liquids besides breastmilk, including water “with the exception of oral rehydration solution, or drops or syrups of vitamins, minerals or medicines” [ 14 ]. Between 2010 and 2018 in LMIC, only 45.1% of infants were breastfed at 6 months. One in five babies born in Pakistan is breastfed within one hour of birth and 48% are exclusively breastfed at six months old (based on 24-hour recall) [ 15 ]. Infant mortality has been declining in Pakistan over the past decade, reaching 62 deaths per 1000 live births in 2018 [ 16 ]. Child malnutrition is the cause of nearly 50% of these deaths [ 17 ]. Exclusive breastfeeding can reduce infant deaths up to 10 percent [ 18 ]. While many birthing people are aware that breastfeeding provides optimal nutrition benefits for infants, qualitative studies have consistently demonstrated that most are unable to identify the specific benefits associated with breastfeeding [ 19 ]. In Pakistan, 52% and 54% of women received antenatal counseling about the initiation of breastfeeding and exclusive breastfeeding, respectively [ 16 ]. Counseling rates were highest in urban areas and among women who had relatively high education status [ 16 ]. Many therefore use cultural practices known to reduce hunger and respiratory illnesses in their infants [ 17 ]. For instance, mothers sometimes discard colostrum in Pakistani villages because they perceive it as not having value for a child’s wellbeing [ 20 , 21 ]. In one study, 14% of mothers in urban and semi-urban Pakistan discarded colostrum [ 22 ]. Many Pakistani mothers complementary feed their infants during the first three days of life with water, ghee, animal milk, and herbal tea, which many Pakistani mothers believe optimizes newborn nourishment and health outcomes [ 20 , 21 , 23 ]. This is referred to as prelacteal feeding [ 24 ]. Among children who were breastfed, 76% received pre-lacteal feeds within the first three hours of life [ 16 ]. Pakistani mothers also face the wide variety of common barriers to breastfeeding such as: time management, physiological changes after delivery, perception of insufficient breast milk, high maternal work load, additional maternal stress from initiating breastfeeding early, cesarean section deliveries, employment, and limited workplace support [ 20 , 25 ]. Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), categorized as abuse, neglect, and household challenges, are important determinants of maternal and infant well-being [ 26 , 27 ]. Several studies suggest that the prevalence of ACEs is consistently highest among LMIC countries [ 28 – 31 ]. ACEs have been found to have an enduring effect on maternal health prior to and during pregnancy [ 28 ]. Evidence suggests that ACEs induce physiological changes that lead to increased risk of poor health and behavioral outcomes [ 32 , 33 ]. An increase in the number and severity of ACEs also appears to be linked to worse health outcomes throughout the life course, indicating the existence of a dose-response relationship [ 34 – 36 ]. Evidence suggests that conditions associated with ACEs, such as substance use and intimate partner violence, can reduce the initiation and continuation of breastfeeding, due to higher rates of chronic health conditions and psychosocial difficulties with pregnancy [ 25 , 37 , 38 ]. Several studies have examined the association between ACEs and breastfeeding outcomes; however, results of these studies vary and few have been conducted in LMIC [ 39 , 40 ]. One study reported that ACEs are associated with an increased likelihood of exclusive breastfeeding, but not breastfeeding initiation, while another found an association between experiencing child sexual abuse and a higher breastfeeding initiation rate [ 26 , 41 ]. Another reported a significant association between ACE count and an increased likelihood of initiating breastfeeding, but not continued breastfeeding at two months postpartum [ 42 ]. To our knowledge, no research has assessed the relationship between ACEs and breastfeeding outcomes in Pakistan. ### Associations between dysfunctional parental discipline and parent-reported mental health outcomes in preschoolers: a cross-sectional study
**כתב עת:** 📄 Front Public Health | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Liu L et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

This study explored the association between parental discipline and mental health in a sample of 21,366 Chinese preschool children (mean age = 4.82 ± 0.89 years; 51.77% boys, 48.23% girls). Parenting practices were measured as overall parenting scores, laxness, overreactivity, and verbosity, and children’s mental health was assessed by the official simplified Chinese version of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ). Both outcomes were dichotomized using validated cut-offs for Chinese preschoolers, and odds ratios are reported as the odds of meeting these cut-off definitions (Total Difficulties Score > 14; adequate Prosocial Behavior Score ≥ 6). The results indicated that higher parenting scores (for total difficulties: AOR = 4.03; 95% CI: 3.64–4.46; for prosocial behavior: AOR = 0.48; 95% CI: 0.45–0.51), particularly in laxness (for total difficulties: AOR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.23–1.34; for prosocial behavior: AOR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.85–0.91) and overreactivity (for total difficulties: AOR = 1.83; 95% CI: 1.76–1.90; for prosocial behavior: AOR = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.64–0.68), were associated with higher odds of a Total Difficulties Score > 14 (i.e., risk of mental health difficulties) and lower odds of an adequate Prosocial Behavior Score (≥ 6), while verbosity (for total difficulties: AOR = 0.80; 95% CI: 0.77–0.84; for prosocial behavior: AOR = 1.23; 95% CI: 1.19–1.27) was associated with lower odds of a Total Difficulties Score > 14 and higher odds of an adequate Prosocial Behavior Score (≥ 6). Subgroup analyses suggested potential effect modification by education level and child age, but most interaction effects were not significant after multiple comparison correction. The findings emphasize the need for balanced parenting and interventions targeting both discipline and parental well-being to enhance children’s mental health.

1 Introduction Mental health problems represent a considerable public health challenge, significantly contributing to the overall global disease burden ( 1 , 2 ). In this article, “mental health problems in children” refer to problems involving emotional, behavioral, and social interaction abnormalities ( 3 , 4 ). An increasing volume of literature indicates that mental health disorders, such as hyperactivity, attention deficits, and emotional or behavioral dysfunctions, impact a substantial proportion of children globally. Studies from different countries have reported varying prevalence rates: approximately 0.7–3% in some European populations ( 5 ), up to 12–39% in rural China ( 6 ). Mental health problems in children may persist into adulthood ( 7 ), leading an increased risk of chronic issues like depression, anxiety ( 8 ), and substance abuse ( 9 ). Over time, they also increase the risk of unemployment, criminal behavior ( 10 ), emphasizing the need for early intervention to prevent long-term negative outcomes ( 11 ). Parenting young children can be highly demanding, requiring parents to regulate their emotions, cognition, and biology in order to manage daily stress and meet the evolving developmental needs of their children ( 12 ). Previous researches showed that dysfunctional parental discipline behavior increased the risk of children developing externalizing and internalizing problems, as well as social problems ( 13–15 ). However, these outcomes are conceptually multifaceted, and the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) assesses five domains of child mental health: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention, peer relationship problems, and prosocial behavior. Feng et al. ( 16 ) found a nonlinear dose–response relationship between maternal depressive symptoms and children’s mental health outcomes among 17,115 mother–child dyads, with turning points at CES-D scores of 17 (total difficulties and internalizing problems), 21 (externalizing problems), and 14 (prosocial behavior problems). According to Arnold et al. ( 17 ), dysfunctional parental discipline behavior can be divided into three categories: laxness, overreactivity, and verbosity. Laxness is marked by permissive discipline, where parents may give in, neglect to enforce rules, or unintentionally reward misbehavior. In contrast, overreactive caregivers tend to manage their children through anger, harshness, and irritability. Verbosity refers to caregivers who rely on lengthy verbal responses and excessive talking, even when it proves ineffective in addressing the child’s behavior ( 17 ). These categories laid the foundation for the development of the Arnold-O’Leary Parenting Scale, a tool designed to evaluate dysfunctional parental discipline across various cultural contexts. Research has demonstrated that different forms of parental discipline to which young children are exposed have different associations with their mental health problems ( 18–21 ). Kiel et al. ( 19 ) found an adverse association between maternal overprotectiveness and children’s inhibition and shyness. Similarly, Lovato et al. ( 18 ) found that elevated levels of parental laxness were related to diminished childhood negative affectivity. Research also showed that uncaring parental behavior was linked to both mental health problems and a higher likelihood of injury in children ( 22 ). Despite the existing literature detailing the connections between parental disciplinary styles and children’s well-being ( 23 , 24 ), previous studies have used relatively small sample sizes, with some being qualitative research, and there is currently a lack of large-sample empirical studies. We aimed to address these limitations by using a large population-based cohort of preschoolers in a city in China. Employing a stratified cluster sampling approach across multiple districts and encompassing a diverse array of child and family characteristics, this research endeavors to deliver a thorough analysis of the relationship between parental discipline and mental health among children aged 3 to 6 years. Theoretical Framework: This study is grounded in Bandura’s Social Learning Theory, which posits that children acquire behavioral patterns, emotional regulation strategies, and social competencies through observational learning and modeling of parental behaviors ( 25 ). Parents’ disciplinary practices serve as behavioral models that children internalize, influencing their subsequent emotional and behavioral development. Additionally, the ecological systems theory ( 26 ) emphasizes that parenting practices operate within multiple contextual systems - including family dynamics, parental stress, and child characteristics - that collectively shape child development outcomes. Dysfunctional disciplinary practices, such as overreactivity and laxness, may disrupt children’s development of self-regulation and social competencies, thereby increasing the risk of mental health difficulties.

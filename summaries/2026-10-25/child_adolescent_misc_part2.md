### [Russian early childhood psychiatry: challenges and prospects]
**כתב עת:** 📄 Zh Nevrol Psikhiatr Im S S Korsakova | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Goryunova AV et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

This article examines the main challenges In Russian early childhood psychiatry. It outlines the developmental stages of Russian "micropsychiatry," which addresses children from the first year of life to age four, and notes its close relationship with pediatrics, neurology, and early childhood psychology. The increasing prevalence of mental disorders among children and adolescents in recent decades underscores the need for early diagnosis, rehabilitation, comprehensive treatment, and prevention within national policies aimed at strengthening the mental health of the younger generation. Establishing a system for early detection and comprehensive intervention for developmental disorders in children within primary healthcare settings has therefore become a priority. Early childhood psychiatry currently faces clinical-diagnostic, classification, terminological, interdepartmental, therapeutic-corrective, and organizational-methodological challenges, which this article examines along with proposed solutions.

В статье обсуждаются основные вопросы и задачи, которые стоят перед психиатрией раннего детства на сегодняшний день. Изложены этапы становления отечественной «микропсихиатрии», которые охватывает возраст с первого года жизни до четырех лет, подчеркивается ее неразрывная связь с педиатрией, неврологией и психологией раннего детства. Рост психической патологии у детей и подростков, отмечаемый в последние десятилетия, свидетельствует о необходимости диагностики и реабилитации этих расстройств в раннем возрасте, их комплексной терапии и профилактики в контексте государственной политики, нацеленной на укрепление психического здоровья подрастающего поколения. В этой связи на повестку дня выходит проблема организации системы раннего выявления и ранней комплексной коррекции нарушений развития ребенка в первичном звене здравоохранения. Перед психиатрией раннего детства стоят актуальные клинико-диагностические, классификационные, терминологические, межведомственные, лечебно-коррекционные, организационно-методические задачи, содержание которых авторы последовательно рассматривают и предлагают пути решения.

### Working memory and language development have a bi-directional longitudinal relationship in autistic preschoolers
**כתב עת:** 📄 J Neurodev Disord | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Sehgal A et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Jul 22

Background Among neurotypical children working memory and language development are closely related, but the longitudinal relationship between these factors is unknown in autism. This study had the goal of evaluating potential bidirectional relationships between these domains. Methods A longitudinal design included language and visuospatial working memory assessments across three time points with 160 autistic preschoolers. Time points were one year apart. A cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) using Full-Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) was used to evaluate longitudinal associations between performance on a working memory task (i.e., the Hide and Seek Task) and language scores (i.e., verbal development quotient). Results Cross-lagged associations were significant for paths from working memory at the first time point to language at the second time point (β = .31, p = .01) and from language at first time point to working memory at the second time point (β = .40, p < .001). Cross-lagged paths from the second to third time point were not statistically significant in either direction. Conclusions This study provides evidence of a bidirectional relationship between working memory and language in autistic toddlers and preschoolers among younger autistic preschoolers. These results highlight working memory as a potential early assessment and intervention domain for improving language outcomes in autism as well as the role of language delays as a factor related to reduced working memory skills later in development.

Introduction Understanding the early neurocognitive development of autistic children can clarify etiology and inform intervention. Working memory is the ability to hold information in mind while updating it to problem-solve [ 1 ]. Language includes speech, gesture, sign, writing, and enables communication, social interaction, and engagement with cultural and environmental contexts [ 2 ]. Both working memory and language underly foundational aspects of child development, including the acquisition of new knowledge, social communication, and adaptive functioning across everyday contexts. Difficulties in either domain can therefore have cascading effects on learning and developmental outcomes. In autism, working memory and language abilities are frequently characterized by persistent delays or differences relative to same-age peers, though the extent and quality of these difficulties varies considerably across individual children and is sensitive to modality and task demands [ 3 – 7 ]. There are several potential theoretical relationships between working memory and language development. Diamond [ 8 ] suggested that working memory supports language development by allowing children to link speech or speech sounds they hear with other information that is not well-synchronized such as gestures or labeled objects. Conversely, language skills including inner speech may allow children to develop cognitive skills including working memory. Specifically, language supports the development of self-regulation and of reflective and abstract thinking [ 9 – 13 ]. Language may also enable children to represent things that are not currently present in their memory. Of course, it is possible that a third factor could underlie the development of both language and working memory, or that the relationship between working memory is bi-directional with mutual influence in early development [ 14 ]. Indeed, there is some evidence for a bi-directional longitudinal relationship between language and executive function batteries that include working memory tasks in young neurotypical children [ 15 – 17 ]. Evidence suggests that working memory is an early developing component of cognitive function [ 18 – 21 ]. For neurotypical children, the capacity to actively hold information in mind typically emerges between 9 to 12 months of age [ 20 ], and working memory abilities increase in their complexity to include manipulating and updating information around 15 months [ 18 ]. Working memory capacity continues to increase during the preschool years [ 19 , 21 ], a period that also coincides with rapid language development. Neurotypical children are also acquiring receptive language within their first year, and expressive language expands through toddlerhood and into their preschool years. Numerous studies have examined the relationship between language and working memory across varying developmental levels and age groups. Among young neurotypical children, early working memory predicts later language skills, particularly vocabulary [ 22 – 26 ]. For example, verbal working memory ability at age four predicted vocabulary a year later [ 25 ], working memory at 30 months predicted vocabulary size at age three [ 22 ], and verbal working memory at age two predicted both receptive and expressive language at age four [ 26 ]. Conversely, early language ability predicts working memory and other aspects of executive functioning among neurotypical children [ 27 – 30 ]. For instance, verbal ability at age three predicted executive function performance on a battery that included working memory tasks at age four [ 30 ], and significant correlations between earlier language and later executive function as well as earlier executive function and later language were reported across two-, three-, and four-year assessments [ 27 ]. However, a second study examining these relationships over the same time period found evidence of a direct relationship for language on later executive function but not for executive function on later language [ 16 ]. These studies document the potential influence of language on later working memory and of working memory on later language early in neurotypical development, but most investigations examined executive function generally rather than isolating working memory and not all studies tested potential bidirectional relationships. No research has specifically investigated the longitudinal relationship between working memory and language in autistic children during the preschool period. Several studies have explored the concurrent relationship (i.e., at cross-sectional time points) between working memory and language in autism. Among school-aged children, working memory and language were not related in an autistic group despite a relation in a neurotypical group [ 31 , 32 ]. In contrast, working memory relates to both structural and pragmatic language in autistic groups [ 33 , 34 ], and a group of autistic children with language impairment had lower verbal working memory ability than a group of autistic children without language impairment [ 35 ]. School-aged autistic children may be less likely than neurotypical children to use verbal strategies to support their working memory performance [ 31 , 32 ]. More recent work found autistic children with structural language impairment were less likely to use verbal mediation during a planning task that involved working memory compared to autistic children with normal language and neurotypical peers suggesting the relation between working memory and language may differ within autism depending on a child’s language profile [ 36 ]. Among preschoolers between the ages of 2–4 years, verbal ability related to working memory performance and this relationship was stronger among autistic children than neurotypical children [ 37 ]. These authors used lab-based tasks to create a working memory composite and measured language using a composite of receptive and expressive language. Similar results were reported for 2- to 8-year-olds using parent report of real-world working memory ability and parent reported communication, pragmatic, and receptive-expressive social communication skills [ 38 ]. ### Autism with social anxiety is associated with acquiescent behaviors and enlarged amygdala volume
**כתב עת:** 📄 Transl Psychiatry | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Hao Y et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Jul 27

Social anxiety (SA) frequently co-occurs with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), but it remains unclear whether SA in ASD reflects greater symptom severity or a distinct subtype. Across two complementary studies, we characterized the behavioral and neural correlates of SA in ASD using a naturalistic social navigation task measuring affiliative and power-related behaviors. In Study 1 (ASD = 575; controls = 357), autistic individuals with SA exhibited significantly reduced task-derived power behaviors (e.g., compliance with others’ requests) compared to those without SA—a pattern not observed in non-autistic individuals. In Study 2 (ASD = 72; controls = 72), individuals with both ASD and SA showed enlarged amygdala volumes relative to both ASD without SA and non-autistic individuals. These behavioral and neural differences found in Study 1 and Study 2 were not observed in ASD with co-occurring depression or generalized anxiety disorder. Moreover, in Study 2, larger amygdala volume in the ASD group was associated with reduced task-derived power behaviors, indicating a neural signature linking social-behavioral differences and anxiety-related features. Together, these findings point to a distinct ASD phenotype marked by social anxiety, altered interpersonal dynamics, and amygdala enlargement, with implications for diagnosis and tailored intervention.

Introduction Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition that affects approximately 1–2% of the population [ 1 , 2 ]. Anxiety was noted as a feature in Kanner’s 1943 description of autism, alongside core symptoms such as social communication and interaction difficulties and restricted, repetitive behaviors [ 3 ]. Research estimates that 30% to 50% of individuals with ASD (mainly cognitively unimpaired) experience social anxiety [ 4 – 6 ]—four times the rate in the general population [ 7 ]. Assessing social anxiety in the context of autism is challenging because certain autistic behaviors—such as reduced social approach—can resemble the behavioral criteria used to diagnose social anxiety, making differentiation difficult [ 5 , 8 – 10 ]. Despite its prevalence and considerable phenotypic overlap [ 11 ], the social-behavioral and neural features that distinguish social anxiety in ASD remain poorly understood, and research in autistic adults is particularly limited. Social anxiety symptoms may limit the benefits of social skills interventions [ 12 , 13 ], which may increase the challenges of employment and social life. Understanding social anxiety in ASD is crucial for improving diagnosis, developing effective treatments, and enhancing support for this population. The hallmark features of social anxiety disorder are fears of negative evaluation and avoidance of anxiety-provoking social situations [ 14 ]. While social fears are associated with social anxiety in both autistic and neurotypical (NT) individuals [ 4 , 15 , 16 ], the nature of these fears may differ. NT individuals with social anxiety often show intact or heightened perspective-taking yet underestimate their own competence [ 17 ]. In autism, however, social fears frequently stem from core social-cognitive difficulties—such as confusion in social situations or intolerance of unpredictability—rather than prototypical fears of rejection or embarrassment [ 9 , 10 , 18 ]. Although autistic individuals with social anxiety display greater social avoidance than those without [ 5 ], this avoidance may arise from mechanisms distinct from those in NT populations. Many autistic individuals value peer approval and desire social connection [ 19 – 21 ], yet difficulties interpreting social cues can make interactions distressing and promote motivational avoidance [ 18 ]. Consequently, social anxiety in autism is often inferred from behavior rather than explicitly reported internal fears [ 11 ], and may present differently than in NT individuals, contributing to limited treatment response [ 22 , 23 ]. These differences highlight the need to identify social-behavioral phenotypes that disentangle social anxiety from autism-related social difficulties and more precisely capture how social anxiety manifests within autism. To quantify the social-behavioral profile of social anxiety in autism, we employed a naturalistic experiment for assessment of implicit social behavior [ 24 ]. Our task was designed to capture social behavior tendencies on the dimensions of power and affiliation [ 25 – 27 ], which define a 2D social space—a cognitive map that supports flexible social behavior across abstract domains [ 28 ]. The affiliation-power model of social space has been used to study social anxiety, social motivation and depression (e.g., [ 29 – 32 ]). Our naturalistic task specifically captured high-level behaviors including social motivation, reciprocity, compliance, and communication—embedded within realistic decision-making scenarios, and the items corresponding to the two social dimensions have been validated and examined across multiple samples [ 24 , 30 , 32 – 36 ]. In our previous work, we found that autistic adults showed lower affiliative behaviors and reduced social distance (a combined measure of affiliation and power in the 2D social space) compared to NT individuals [ 35 , 36 ]. These results indicate that the affiliation-power model could reflect meaningful social-behavioral phenotypes of ASD. Therefore, this model is suitable for examining whether social anxiety further reduces task-derived affiliation and/or power behaviors. Studies estimated that 49% to 80% of autistic adults experience interpersonal victimization [ 37 , 38 ]. Difficulties recognizing manipulation or anticipating consequences may also lead to compliance in some contexts [ 39 ], suggesting that social anxiety in autism may diminish their ability to navigate interpersonal demands rather than increasing assertive or dominant behaviors. Beyond the limited work on social-behavioral features, neural correlates of social anxiety in autistic adults have been scarcely examined. Across species, the amygdala—particularly its medial divisions—acts as a central hub of the social behavior network, transforming diverse social cues into adaptive affiliative, defensive, or competitive responses [ 40 – 42 ]. Rather than merely generating emotional valence, the amygdala assigns social meaning and orchestrates downstream behavioral mechanisms, making it a core integrator of social perception and social action [ 41 , 42 ]. Converging cellular, molecular, and neuroimaging evidence further highlights the amygdala as a biologically vulnerable system in autism [ 43 , 44 ]. Transcriptomic work shows that numerous ASD-susceptibility genes are expressed in the developing amygdala and exhibit cell-type–specific dysregulation [ 45 ]. Neurochemical studies similarly report reduced NAA/Cr ratios—an index of neuronal integrity—in the amygdala–hippocampal region, correlating with autistic symptoms of greater social and emotional impairment [ 46 ]. Postmortem findings reveal atypical developmental trajectories, with an early surplus of neurons followed by decline into adulthood [ 47 ], and longitudinal MRI work identifies enlarged basolateral nuclei and altered subnuclear growth associated with social-communication symptoms [ 48 ]. Furthermore, at the circuit level, specific amygdala microcircuits regulate social evaluation, social-threat learning, and anxiety in social contexts [ 49 – 51 ], suggesting a mechanism through which amygdala abnormalities may contribute to social anxiety–related difficulties in autism. This aligns with the alternative view that amygdala dysfunction in ASD may relate more to heightened fear and anxiety than to social-cognitive deficits per se [ 52 ]. On September 22, 2026, the National Health Commission released the "Guideline for intervention and rehabilitation in children with autism spectrum disorder (2026 edition)". The guideline applies to intervention and rehabilitation services for children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) under 18 years of age. It establishes eight basic principles, namely early intervention, individualized intervention, comprehensive intervention, evidence-based practice, long-term and high-intensity intervention, primary-level care as the mainstay, family participation, and social inclusion and support, and specifies that the optimal treatment window is before 6 years of age and that intervention should be provided for at least 20 hours per week. It covers health education, intervention for developmental deficits, intervention for challenging behaviors, family intervention, digital and intelligent intervention and rehabilitation, and follow-up.

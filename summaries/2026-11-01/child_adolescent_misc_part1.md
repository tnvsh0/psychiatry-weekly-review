### Whole-blood lead-copper association in children with autism spectrum disorder: Implications for toxic-essential metal interactions
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Metab Brain Dis *(IF: 14.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Bjørklund G et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 27

Lead (Pb) is a developmental neurotoxicant that can interfere with essential-metal regulation, whereas copper (Cu) is a tightly regulated redox-active trace element required for normal neurological function. This study investigated whether whole-blood Pb concentrations were associated with whole-blood Cu concentrations in 28 children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), compared with 28 pediatric controls closely matched for age and with the same sex distribution. Mean whole-blood Pb concentration was 70.29 ± 31.34 µg/L in the ASD group and 47.99 ± 13.95 µg/L in controls. Pb concentrations were higher in ASD (geometric mean ratio 1.394, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.146-1.697, p = 0.001). Within the ASD group, Pb was positively associated with Cu (Pearson r = 0.385, 95% CI 0.014-0.663, p = 0.043), with a regression slope of 1.940 µg/L Cu per 1 µg/L Pb (95% CI 0.067-3.813; R² = 0.148). After adjustment for age and sex, the Pb coefficient remained similar in magnitude (β = 1.765, 95% CI - 0.174 to 3.704, p = 0.072). The Pb-Cu correlation was not significant in controls, and there was no evidence that the Pb-Cu association differed between the ASD and control groups. The association remained directionally consistent across additional sensitivity analyses, and adjustment for Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS) score did not materially alter the Pb coefficient. The findings are compatible with experimental evidence on Pb-Cu interactions and Cu homeostasis but do not establish causality or ASD-specific Pb-Cu coupling. Replication in larger, prospectively characterized cohorts is required.

### Longitudinal relationship between sleep and emotional/behavioral problems in autism spectrum disorders: a cross-lagged panel analysis
**כתב עת:** 📄 Eur J Pediatr | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Dong H et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 30

UNLABELLED: Sleep, emotional, and behavioral problems are common comorbidities associated with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Current longitudinal research on the temporal links between sleep problems and emotional-behavioral problems shows inconsistent results among preschool children with ASD. Hence, this longitudinal study aimed to examine the temporal associations between emotional-behavioral and sleep problems among preschool children with ASD across two time points. Among 124 enrolled preschool children with ASD, 70 completed follow-up and constituted the final analytic sample (mean age 36.7 ± 10.2 months at T1). They were evaluated for developmental quotient using the Griffiths Development Scales-Chinese version (GDS-C), ASD symptoms using Autism Behavior Checklist (ABC) and Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS), emotional and behavioral problems using the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL), and sleep problems using the Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ) at time 1 (T1). The evaluations were replicated at a follow-up appointment 18 months after T1 (time 2; T2). The cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) was employed to examine the potential relationship between CBCL and CSHQ scores in the children. The total CBCL score at T1 could predict the CSHQ at T2 (β = 0.348, t = 3.105, p = 0.002), whereas the CSHQ at T1 could not predict the CBCL at T2 (β = 0.117, t = 1.083, p = 0.279). Findings remained robust across five sensitivity subsets after excluding subgroups with distinct rehabilitation exposure, younger age, higher development quotients, girls, or mild ASD symptoms.

CONCLUSION: Emotional-behavioral problems predict sleep problems 18 months later in preschool ASD children. Screening of these symptoms during follow-up supports early sleep-risk monitoring.

WHAT IS KNOWN: • Sleep, emotional, and behavioral problems are common comorbidities associated with ASD. • Thus, researching their temporal correlation over time is necessary.

WHAT IS NEW: • Emotional and behavioral problems indicate a positive temporal correlation at T1, which was also evident for sleep problems at T2, in children with ASD. • Emotional and behavioral problems in preschool children with ASD can forecast sleep problems that develop 18 months later, providing guidance for clinical decision-making.

### Association of Copy Number Variations in PTEN with Autism Spectrum Disorder
**כתב עת:** 📄 Genes (Basel) | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Zheng X et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 24

Background: Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder with a strong genetic basis. While copy number variations (CNVs) significantly contribute to ASD risk, the specific role of individual genes within these variants remains unclear. This study investigates the contribution of CNVs in the PTEN gene to ASD pathophysiology by integrating population-level genetic analysis with multi-dimensional biological evidence. Methods: The biological plausibility of PTEN in ASD was first assessed using public multi-omics data, including tissue expression profiles, protein-protein interaction networks, and published cell-type-specific differential-expression data derived from single-nucleus RNA sequencing. Subsequently, a group-matched case-control study was conducted in a Han Chinese population (121 ASD cases and 110 controls) to evaluate the association between PTEN CNVs and ASD susceptibility. One-way ANOVA was performed to compare the differences in ASD clinical phenotype scores among groups with different CNV types of PTEN . Results: Integrative biological analyses revealed that PTEN is enriched in ASD-relevant brain regions (e.g., cerebellum) and exhibits cell-type-specific expression in neurons and glia. Furthermore, protein network analysis confirmed its central position within the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, a critical regulator of neurodevelopment. In the Han Chinese population, no significant association was detected between PTEN CNVs and ASD risks or clinical phenotype scores. Conclusions: PTEN CNVs may not be independently associated with ASD, but may contribute to susceptibility through broader neurodevelopmental networks and molecular pathways.

1. Introduction Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is an early-onset neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by deficits in social communication and the presence of restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, with a global prevalence of approximately 1% among children [ 1 ]. Family studies consistently demonstrate that ASD exhibits exceptionally high heritability (approximately 80–90%), underscoring the predominant role of genetic factors in its etiology [ 2 ]. Current research is limited by the extreme heterogeneity of ASD, the low individual predictive power of common variants, and the pleiotropic nature of rare variants. Although approximately 10–20% of ASD cases can be attributed to identifiable rare genetic variants, and the heritability of ASD is estimated to be as high as 80%, the high heritability has not yet been effectively translated into clearly actionable clinical findings [ 2 , 3 ]. Therefore, expanding our understanding of ASD-associated genetic variants and their functional implications remains a critical priority for advancing biological insights into this disorder. In recent years, advances in chromosomal microarray analysis and high-throughput sequencing have firmly established CNVs—defined as deletions or duplications of DNA segments larger than 1 kb—as a major source of genetic risk for ASD [ 4 , 5 , 6 ]. Pathogenic CNVs are estimated to account for approximately 5% to 10% of ASD cases, with a notably higher contribution when the affected genes are critical for neurodevelopment or signaling regulation [ 7 ]. Our previous genome-wide CNV analysis in a Northeast Han Chinese population identified multiple rare CNV events and 20 candidate genes associated with ASD risk, including PTEN [ 8 ]. However, CNV regions typically encompass multiple genes [ 9 ]; therefore, the precise contribution of individual genes such as PTEN to ASD pathophysiology cannot be determined from CNV data alone. In addition, common variants in other neurodevelopmental genes, such as RELN , have been associated with ASD in case-control studies, supporting the relevance of single-gene investigations in ASD [ 10 ]. Nevertheless, a substantial body of independent genetic and functional evidence has robustly implicated PTEN in ASD [ 11 ]. The PTEN gene (located at 10q23.3) encodes a tumor suppressor with dual lipid and protein phosphatase activities [ 12 ]. It negatively regulates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway via dephosphorylation of PIP3 to PIP2, a cascade critically involved in neuronal proliferation, synaptic plasticity, and dendritic development [ 13 ]. Loss of PTEN function results in hyperactivation of this pathway, leading to excessive dendritic growth, aberrant synaptic protein synthesis, and excitatory/inhibitory synaptic imbalance—cellular phenotypes that closely recapitulate the neuropathological hallmarks of ASD [ 14 , 15 ]. Moreover, germline PTEN pathogenic sequence variants are detectable in approximately 0.2–1% of ASD patients, and this proportion reaches up to 17% in those with macrocephaly [ 16 ]. PTEN pathogenic variants are typically sequence-level changes, such as missense, nonsense, frameshift, or splice-site mutations. They directly disrupt PTEN protein function and are well established in ASD. Notably, in patients with germline PTEN pathogenic variants, the presence of pathogenic or likely pathogenic CNVs was significantly more common among those with ASD/developmental delay, suggesting that CNVs may act as genomic modifiers. ### Sex-Related Specificities of Sensory Profiles in Autism Spectrum Disorder: Impact on Clinical Functioning
**כתב עת:** 📄 Medicina (Kaunas) | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Pezone M et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 2

Background and Objectives: Sensory anomalies are a distinctive feature of Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) individuals and appear to differ in intensity, frequency, and subtype between male and female subjects. According to the diagnostic framework of the DSM-5-TR, these sensory patterns are key clinical indicators. The objectives of this study were to investigate sensoriality in ASD, to compare sensory profiles in ASD subjects of both sexes and evaluate potential correlations with clinical profiles. Materials and Methods: A total of 94 ASD participants were enrolled at the Children and Adolescents Neuropsychiatry Department of the University of Naples Federico II. The population was assessed through psychometric (cognitive and adaptive levels, and intensity of autism symptoms) and sensory characterization (using the Short Sensory Profile questionnaire). Subsequently, a Mann–Whitney U test was performed to evaluate differences in sensory profiles between males and females. Spearman’s correlation was used to assess significant associations between the sensory profile and cognitive level, adaptive level, and ASD symptom intensity. Subsequently, for the correlations that emerged as significant, an analysis was performed using a General Linear Model (GLM), including age and Intelligence Quotient (IQ) as covariates in the model. Results: No statistically significant differences in the sensory profile emerged between male and female subjects. Sensory anomalies do not appear to be correlated with cognitive level. Although some statistically significant correlations emerged between the sensory profile, the AQ, and the ADOS-2 comparison score, these results were not confirmed after a GLM analysis was performed using the participants’ age and IQ as covariates. Conclusions: The observed results appear partially in line with the existing literature. Following the GLM analysis, it was further observed that variables such as age and IQ play a crucial role in interpreting the functional profile of individuals with ASD. At present, the study has several limitations (the exclusive use of parent-report instruments, an observational design, the lack of a control group) which could affect the robustness of the observed findings. Therefore, further studies are deemed necessary to better define the sex-specific sensory characteristics of ASD individuals.

1. Introduction Atypical sensory profiles are a highly prevalent feature in Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) individuals. In fact, one of the diagnostic criteria currently set forth by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR)—categorized under the B criteria, including repetitive and stereotyped behaviors—describes symptoms as: “Hyper- or hypo-reactivity to sensory input or unusual interest in sensory aspects of the environment” [ 1 ]. Globally, many studies focused on sensory anomalies in ASD individuals to contribute to a clearer neurobiological and phenotypic definition. Sensory anomalies can affect any sensory channel (sight, hearing, touch, etc.) and may be associated with an individual’s unique behaviors and response thresholds. For instance, referring to the theoretical framework proposed by Dunn W., we can identify four sensory processing patterns (derived from the combination of response thresholds, behavioral self-regulation strategies, and the interaction between these two factors): sensory seeking, sensory avoiding, sensory sensitivity, and low registration [ 2 ]. These aspects impact the functioning of ASD individuals, contributing to the impairment of their ability to adapt to different contexts and exacerbating any concomitant dysfunctional behaviors [ 3 ]. Indeed, it is well known that sensory abnormalities can also affect the social skills of ASD individuals, leading for example to difficulties in integrating social messages provided by other people; such aspects could be underlying by abnormalities in brain circuits that regulate both aspects of sociality as well as sensory processing, such as, for example, the anterior cingulate cortex, the superior temporal cortex, and the insula [ 4 ]. Furthermore, the scientific literature is increasingly exploring differences in symptomatic expression based on sex. Specifically, the ASD phenotype in females may differ both in the intensity of the symptoms expressed and in the potential comorbidities that may develop over time [ 5 ]. In fact, for example, females with ASD tend to present a higher prevalence of internalizing comorbidities (such as depression and anxiety), which often serve as the primary reason for medical consultation and can lead to a missed ASD diagnosis [ 6 ]. For instance, it is well known that ASD women tend to employ camouflage strategies to a greater extent in social contexts; these behaviors result in higher levels of perceived social reciprocity, which can mask the underlying ASD symptomatology [ 7 ]. Sensory processing anomalies are also being studied to evaluate potential differential characteristics based on sex. For instance, Osorio J.M.A. et al. assessed how, in a sample of ASD youth, females exhibit greater differences in the domains of proprioceptive and auditory sensitivity [ 8 ]. Differences in sensory processing are also present from early childhood: Harris H.K. et al. highlighted that, in a sample of ASD preschool-aged children, females tend to exhibit more sensory seeking behaviors compared to their male peers [ 9 ]. ### Emerging Therapeutic Strategies for Child and Adolescent Obesity: A Narrative Review
**כתב עת:** 📄 Nutrients | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Oliaro T, Amianto F | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 21

Background/Objectives: Childhood obesity is a chronic, multifactorial disease requiring attention to biological, behavioral, psychological, family, and socioeconomic factors. This narrative review examined the integration of lifestyle, family-based, psychological, and pharmacological approaches, focusing on treatment engagement, eating disorders, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and treatment durability. Methods: A single search interface covering PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science (the University of Turin library discovery platform) was used to identify studies published primarily from 2010 to August 2026. Pediatric trials, observational studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, and public-health reports were prioritized. Results: Intensive family-based, multicomponent behavioral treatment remains the best-supported foundation of care, although psychological outcomes are infrequently assessed. Psychiatric comorbidity is heterogeneous; loss-of-control eating, emotional symptoms, ADHD, and executive-function difficulties may impair engagement in selected patients, and untreated ADHD has been associated with a slower BMI response to weight management. Professionally supervised obesity treatment containing a dietary component did not, on average, worsen eating psychopathology and reduced several related symptoms. Family participation improved outcomes, whereas logistical, socioeconomic, and psychological barriers reduced attendance. Across randomized trials, adding anti-obesity medication to behavioral treatment produced an additional, although on average modest, reduction in BMI. Semaglutide, liraglutide, and phentermine/topiramate produced larger numerical reductions than older agents in separate trials, but heterogeneity and the absence of head-to-head comparisons preclude formal ranking. Gastrointestinal adverse events were frequent with glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. Pediatric trials and real-world evidence indicate partial weight regain after discontinuation, while prescribing remains limited and unequal. Conclusions: Pediatric obesity care should be phenotype-informed, multidisciplinary, and longitudinal. Pharmacotherapy can enhance BMI reduction but should complement rather than replace behavioral, psychological, and family care. Outcomes should extend beyond BMI to metabolic, psychological, functional, and long-term measures.

1. Introduction Childhood obesity is a widespread global health issue [ 1 ]. When obesity is not secondary to endocrine, monogenic, or syndromic disorders, it can be considered ‘essential’ obesity: a chronic, relapsing, and multifactorial disease arising from interactions among biological susceptibility, eating behavior, environmental exposures, and socioeconomic conditions. In children and adolescents, body mass index (BMI) must be interpreted by age and sex. For individuals aged 5–19 years, the World Health Organization (WHO) defines overweight as BMI-for-age greater than one standard deviation above the reference median and obesity as greater than two standard deviations; comparisons across studies must nevertheless account for differences between WHO and International Obesity Task Force classification systems [ 1 , 2 ]. The global burden has increased sharply. In 2022, more than 390 million people aged 5–19 years were living with overweight, including approximately 160 million with obesity, and the prevalence of overweight in this age group had risen from about 8% in 1990 to 20% in 2022 [ 1 , 3 ]. In the sixth round of the WHO European Childhood Obesity Surveillance Initiative, 25% of children aged 7–9 years were living with overweight, including 11% with obesity [ 4 ]. Italian surveillance similarly indicates that approximately three in ten children aged 8–9 years have excess weight, with higher prevalence in southern regions [ 5 ]. Socioeconomic inequalities are marked: analysis of 53,275 Italian children showed less favorable dietary and activity patterns and higher obesity prevalence among children from families with lower parental education [ 6 ]. Pediatric obesity is associated with hypertension, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, obstructive sleep apnea, and other early or adult complications. It also frequently persists into adulthood and predicts later cardiometabolic morbidity and premature cardiovascular mortality [ 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ]. Its burden is also psychological and social: weight stigma, bullying, body dissatisfaction, anxiety or depressive symptoms, impaired quality of life, and disordered eating may occur, although these outcomes are heterogeneous and should not be assumed in every child [ 11 , 12 , 13 ]. Neurodevelopmental comorbidity is relevant in selected patients. Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is associated with a modest increase in the risk of pediatric overweight and obesity; reward-related behavior may overlap between the two conditions, whereas cognitive-control difficulties appear more specific to ADHD [ 14 , 15 , 16 ]. Given its complex pathogenesis and clinical expression, treatment cannot be reduced to dietary prescription. Contemporary care combines nutrition, physical activity, behavioral strategies, psychological support, and family involvement, while accounting for treatment accessibility and social context [ 10 , 17 ]. Recently, the therapeutic landscape has also expanded: while intensive nutritional, behavioral, and family-based interventions remain foundational, newer drugs such as liraglutide, semaglutide, and other anti-obesity medications can produce rapid and clinically meaningful BMI reductions in selected young people. Their long-term safety, durability after discontinuation, accessibility, and integration with psychological and family care remain incompletely defined [ 18 , 19 , 20 ]. The aim of the present review is to discuss recent evidence on the integration of psychological, family-based, and pharmacological approaches to pediatric obesity, with a focus on treatment engagement, eating psychopathology, and the more limited evidence on the relationship between obesity, ADHD, and stimulant-associated weight trajectories.

### Associations between ADHD behavior and attachment (in)security in middle childhood: A longitudinal study
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Development and Psychopathology (Dev Psychopathol) *(IF: 5.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Lievrouw S et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 23

Research has consistently suggested an association between ADHD behavior and insecure attachment, yet longitudinal data in middle childhood are largely absent. In particular, the directionality of this association and the role of oppositional defiant disorder (ODD) behavior remain unclear. This longitudinal study therefore examined these links across three annual waves in a Western-European community sample. Data were available for 614 children at Wave 1 (Mage = 10.28, SD = 0.58, range 9-12; 44% boys, 54% girls), 470 at Wave 2 (Mage = 10.28, SD = 0.61, range 10-13; 44% boys, 54% girls), and 385 at Wave 3 (Mage = 12.01, SD = 0.58, range 10-14; 45% boys, 54% girls). ADHD and ODD behavior were rated by mothers using the Child Behavior Checklist; children's attachment was assessed through both explicit (questionnaires) and implicit (secure base script knowledge) measures. Random Intercept Cross‑Lagged Panel Models revealed stable small to moderate between‑child associations between ADHD behavior and attachment (in)security ( β  = -0.550 to 0.367), which largely remained significant after controlling for ODD behavior ( β  = -0.517 to 0.295). Cross‑lagged effects, reflecting prospective within-child processes, were mostly non‑significant, providing little evidence for bidirectional associations in middle childhood.

### Adolescents' in vivo emotional responses to digital and in-person peer experiences: Differences by context and links to daily life depressive symptoms
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Development and Psychopathology (Dev Psychopathol) *(IF: 5.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Kilic Z et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 26

The rise of digital media has raised questions about potential links to adolescent depression, especially among girls. This study investigated whether affective responses to daily peer interactions, and depressive symptoms, varied by context - digital media or in-person. A sample of 110 adolescents assigned female at birth, aged 12-17 ( M  = 15.30,  SD  = 1.51; 83.6% White and 96.7% non-Hispanic) and enriched for risk for depression and suicidality, participated in 30 days of ecological momentary assessments over 3 months. Participants reported their most recent "worst" and "best" peer interactions, the context of these interactions (digital media or in-person), their affective responses to these interactions, and depressive symptoms. Multilevel models revealed that greater negative affect during negative interactions was associated with higher depressive symptoms, regardless of context ( B  = 0.02,  p  < .001). In contrast, positive in-person interactions were associated with higher positive affect than digital interactions ( B  = -2.01,  p  = .02), which, in turn, was associated with lower depressive symptoms ( B  = 0.08,  p  = .01). Findings highlight the protective role of positive in-person experiences in adolescent emotional well-being, and challenge assumptions that negative digital experiences are the primary causes of depressive symptoms.

### Intrinsic and extrinsic regulation of young children's frustration: A person-specific, dynamic approach
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Child Development (Child Dev) *(IF: 5.4)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Zhang X et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 26

Young children may draw on both intrinsic physiological systems and extrinsic caregiver support to manage frustration. While regulation is often conceptualized as feedback-control processes, there has been limited empirical evidence on how they unfold in real time. Taking a person-specific, dynamic network approach, we examined whether parasympathetic changes and maternal responsiveness serve as active regulators that effectively dampen children's regulatory demands (reflected in sympathetic arousal and challenging behaviors) on a moment-to-moment basis during a frustration challenge. Data from 139 mother-child dyads (30-60 months; 48% female; 94% White; data collected June 2017-December 2019 in central Pennsylvania, U.S.) revealed that parasympathetic responses can function as real-time regulators through both dynamic increases and decreases across children; physiological regulation was related to better effortful control (standardized coefficient β = .22). Parental regulation primarily modulated challenging behaviors and was less evident among children with greater negative affectivity and externalizing risks (βs = -.19 and -.22). Findings demonstrate how children's self-regulation emerges from heterogeneous, dynamic systems where intrinsic and extrinsic processes operate to maintain homeostasis. By linking micro-level regulation dynamics with children's emotional and behavioral characteristics, the findings further highlight the value of capturing active, unfolding regulation patterns for understanding early functioning strengths and risks.

In this study, we focused on how young children rely on their internal physiology and support from their mothers to handle a frustration challenge. By tracking physiological responses and observed behaviors for 139 mother-child pairs on a moment-to-moment basis, we found that children's internal calming systems worked in highly unique ways to help regulate their arousal and frustration-related behaviors, especially for those with better effortful self-control ability. Meanwhile, maternal responses emerged in relation to children's frustration behaviors and helped regulate these behaviors, although this appeared harder for children prone to strong negative emotions. In summary, investigating how these real-time interactions unfold moment-to-moment helps us understand how children cope with frustration and how that may relate to their emotional and behavioral characteristics.

### Susceptibility to task-irrelevant auditory distractors in relation to visual working memory in children with and without attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Child Development (Child Dev) *(IF: 5.4)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Kong Y et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 28

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a prevalent neurodevelopmental disorder associated with increased distractibility. Previous research has reported that susceptibility to task-irrelevant visual distraction is linked with visual working memory (WM) capacity in adults. Here, we examined how neural susceptibility to task-irrelevant auditory distractors related to visual WM capacity in children with and without ADHD. Across two experiments, electroencephalography (EEG) signals were recorded from 146 Chinese children aged 7-15 (63 with ADHD, 23% girls; collected in 2022-2024), during which they performed visual tasks of varying difficulty with auditory distractors. The mismatch negativity (MMN) amplitude was measured as neural susceptibility to auditory distractors, and a K-score was calculated to assess visual WM capacity. In typically developing (TD) children, those with higher visual WM capacity showed less susceptibility to auditory distractors under high audiovisual competition (r = 0.494), whereas this relation reversed under low competition (r = -0.319). Conversely, in children with ADHD, lower visual WM capacity predicted higher susceptibility under both high (r = 0.442) and low competition (r = 0.326). These findings indicate that susceptibility to task-irrelevant auditory distraction is related to visual WM capacity in children, but the patterns differ between typical and atypical neurodevelopment and are modulated by audiovisual competition.

This study investigated how children's sensitivity to distracting sounds was related to their visual memory capacity, and whether this relation differed between children with and without attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. Brain activity was measured while children performed visual tasks under different demand levels with background sounds. In typically developing children, higher visual memory capacity was associated with reduced distraction by sounds when the visual task was more demanding, whereas the relation was reversed when visual task demand was lower. In contrast, children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder who had lower visual memory capacity showed greater auditory distraction, regardless of visual task demand. These findings suggest a close relation between auditory distraction and visual memory capacity in children, depending on both developmental deficit and task contexts.

### Childhood exposure to interparental conflict and adolescent mental health difficulties: The mediating role of self-regulation difficulties
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Developmental Psychology (Dev Psychol) *(IF: 5.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Chebaia A et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 29

Interparental conflict (IPC) in early childhood is associated with mental health difficulties in adolescence. General psychological processes such as self-regulation that may underpin this relationship are important to understand during this developmental stage. The aim of the study was to investigate whether indicators of self-regulation difficulties in middle childhood, including (a) difficulties with persistence, (b) emotional reactivity, (c) behavioral disinhibition, and (d) sleeping difficulties, mediated the relationship between IPC in early childhood and mental health difficulties (anxiety and depressive symptoms; antisocial behavior) in adolescence. A second aim was to determine whether child sex moderated the associations between IPC, self-regulation difficulties, and mental health difficulties. Structural equation modeling using data from a population-based Australian sample ( N  = 9,476) supported a mediation model where higher IPC at age 4-5 years was associated with higher indications of self-regulation difficulties at age 10-11 years, which in turn predicted higher depressive symptoms, anxious symptoms, and antisocial behavior at age 14-15 years. Evidence of moderation by child sex was found. The relationship between IPC, indicators of self-regulation difficulties, and antisocial behavior was stronger for males than females, while the relationship between indicators of self-regulation difficulties and internalizing outcomes was stronger for females compared to males. These results are interpreted using perspectives on biopsychosocial development, and implications for intervention at multiple time points are discussed. ### The role of temperament and personality traits in the development of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) from late childhood through adolescence
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Developmental Psychology (Dev Psychol) *(IF: 5.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Lawson KM et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 29

Individual differences in temperament and personality traits may shed light on the presentation and development of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) across adolescence. The present study used multimethod, longitudinal data from a large sample of Mexican-origin youth ( N  = 674; 50% girls) to examine associations between ADHD symptoms (including combined, hyperactive-impulsive, and inattentive subtypes) and a wide range of temperament and personality traits assessed via self-, mother-, and father-reports and coded observational data. First, we found moderate 1-year rank-order stabilities of combined, hyperactive-impulsive, and inattentive ADHD symptoms (rs ranging from .27 to .51) but relatively low 8-year rank-order stability from age 10 to 17 ( r  = .19 for combined); 8-year stability was higher for boys than girls. Second, hyperactive-impulsive and inattentive symptoms were both negatively correlated with concurrent effortful control and conscientiousness and positively correlated with impulsivity, negative emotionality, and neuroticism; in addition, hyperactive-impulsive symptoms were positively associated with surgency and extraversion. Third, bivariate latent growth curve models showed that the levels and slopes of effortful control (and its facets) and negative emotionality (particularly the frustration, aggression, and depressed mood facets) were significantly related to the levels and slopes of ADHD symptom trajectories. Findings from random-intercept cross-lagged panel models showed that temperament domains and ADHD symptoms had meaningful between- and within-person associations but no significant cross-lagged associations. Associations were stronger for adolescent self-reports of temperament/personality than for mother-reports. Overall, findings highlight connections between temperament and personality traits with ADHD symptoms across adolescence. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

### A systematic review of observational and report-based measures of caregiver sensitivity: A Latin American perspective
**כתב עת:** 📄 Infant Mental Health Journal (Infant Ment Health J) *(IF: 2.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** סקירה שיטתית | **מחברים:** Retiz O et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov

Abstract Sensitivity is a cornerstone of early childhood mental health; however, its measurement relies heavily on tools developed in English‐speaking, high‐resource contexts. This systematic review identified observational and report‐based instruments used to assess sensitivity in caregivers of children aged 0–6 years. It evaluated the methodological robustness and usability of these instruments within Latin American contexts. Following a systematic search in Web of Science, Scopus, PsycInfo, and Redalyc, 732 eligible studies were identified. Based on a frequency‐of‐use threshold, 16 instruments (14 observational and 2 report‐based) represented across 616 papers were selected for in‐depth evaluation. Measures were coded for theoretical alignment with Ainsworth's sensitivity definition, psychometric evidence, and usability—specifically training accessibility, administration complexity, and evidence of use with diverse Latin American populations (e.g., caregivers in rural settings or with lower educational levels). Results revealed that only a minority of measures adhered explicitly to Ainsworth's original definition. Most instruments prioritized warmth and positive affect, potentially leading to cross‐cultural underdetection in contexts where responsiveness is expressed through proximity and routine‐based care. While observational tools predominated, report‐based measures showed limited validation and minimal regional use. These findings highlight the urgent need for culturally grounded validation and the development of accessible training programs for the region.

1 INTRODUCTION According to Attachment Theory, attachment refers to the affective bond that develops between an infant and their primary caregiver(s) during the first years of life and becomes organized into relatively stable patterns of security or insecurity through repeated interactions (Ainsworth, 1967 ; Bowlby, 1969 ). Secure attachment reflects the child's expectation of caregiver availability and responsiveness in contexts of stress, supporting the use of the caregiver as a secure base for exploration (Ainsworth et al., 1978 ). Key Findings Sensitivity measures often treat warmth and positive affect as central, which can miss responsive caregiving expressed through proximity and routine‐based care in some Latin American and other non‐Western contexts. Many widely used observational tools require training and materials that are not available in Spanish, limiting feasibility and standardization in Latin American low‐resource settings. Report‐based sensitivity measures are brief and potentially scalable but currently lack evidence linking them to attachment outcomes and observational convergence. Statement of relevance to infant and early childhood mental health Accurate assessment of caregiver sensitivity is essential for identifying relational risk and tailoring early caregiving interventions central to infant and early childhood mental health. This review clarifies how sensitivity is operationalized across common instruments and highlights when measures may under‐detect responsiveness in contexts where caregiving is expressed through proximity and daily routines. By integrating psychometric indicators with usability and Spanish‐language training access, the study supports practitioners and researchers in selecting measures that better fit real‐world service and research settings. A substantial body of research has documented the long‐term implications of attachment security for socioemotional development and mental health. Secure attachment has been associated with greater emotional understanding and regulation, as well as more adaptive interpersonal functioning, whereas insecure attachment is linked to long‐term elevated risks of relational difficulties, externalizing behaviors, and mental health problems (Dagan et al., 2018 ; Deneault et al., 2023 ; Obeldobel et al., 2023 ). In this context, caregiver sensitivity has consistently been identified as a robust predictor of secure attachment (Madigan et al., 2024 ). Sensitivity is defined as the caregiver's capacity to perceive, interpret, and respond appropriately to the child's signals (Ainsworth et al., 1974 ). Through ongoing reciprocal exchanges, sensitive caregiving supports trust in caregiver availability and the development of secure internal working models, providing a relational foundation for socioemotional competence and mental health across development (Thompson, 2016 ). 1.1 Assessing sensitivity Given its foundational role in early socioemotional development, attachment research has increasingly emphasized the need for reliable and context‐sensitive measures of caregiving processes, particularly sensitivity (Halty & Berástegui, 2021 ). Observational measures are often treated as the reference approach for assessing sensitivity (Lotzin et al., 2015 ), as they allow direct evaluation of caregiver‐child interaction and capture sensitive behaviors that are often intuitive and automated rather than consciously planned (Mesman, 2010 ), making them less accessible through self‐report. Ainsworth's Maternal Sensitivity Scale (AMSS; Ainsworth et al., 1974 ) is the gold standard observational measure in the study of parental sensitivity, in that it operationalizes Ainsworth's original conceptualization of sensitivity as the caregiver's prompt, appropriate, and child‐attuned responding to signals (Bretherton, 2013 ). Building on this framework, numerous observational instruments have been developed, differing in their conceptualization and operationalization of sensitivity (Mesman & Emmen, 2013 ). While some measures adhere closely to Ainsworth's original definition by focusing on contingent and contextually appropriate responses to the child's signals, others incorporate additional behavioral indicators such as warmth, verbal engagement, or structured interactional tasks (Mesman & Emmen, 2013 ). As Mesman ( 2021 ) highlighted, these operational choices reflect implicit assumptions about how sensitivity is expressed and have important implications for the cultural flexibility and comparability of observational measures. Previous systematic reviews have synthesized observational measurements of parental sensitivity. Mesman and Emmen identified eight instruments most frequently used in the literature, all developed in English and primarily validated in North American or European contexts (Mesman & Emmen, 2013 ). A more recent review by Halty and Berástegui ( 2021 ) reported similar limitations in the linguistic and cultural scope of parental responsiveness measures, conceptualized as the behavioral manifestation of sensitivity. That review also underscored the need for more systematic evaluation of sensitivity instruments using objective, comparable criteria. To date, however, the field lacks an integrative assessment that jointly considers psychometric indicators and practical usability, including training accessibility and feasibility across diverse research settings. Importantly, methodological rigor and practical usability do not necessarily converge in the assessment of sensitivity. Instruments with theoretical and empirical support often require extensive training, lengthy administration procedures, and specialized resources (Fourment et al., 2022 ), limiting their feasibility in large‐scale studies and low‐resource settings. These constraints are particularly salient in contexts such as Latin America, where access to standardized attachment measures is often restricted by financial limitations, lack of institutional infrastructure, and the high cost of training for key observational instruments, which are frequently available only in English (Mesman et al., 2016a ). ### Higher Education Institutions: An Underused Setting for Addressing Adverse Childhood Experiences
**כתב עת:** 📄 Prev Chronic Dis | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Zhang S, Collins-Anderson A | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 29

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are potentially traumatic experiences that occur from birth through age 17 years and can undermine a child’s safety and stability ( 1 ). From various forms of abuse and neglect to other aspects that can impair a child’s well-being, ACEs are a major public health concern. In 2024, the National Survey of Children’s Health found that approximately 38% of children experienced at least 1 ACE ( 2 ). Among US adults, 64% reported at least 1 ACE, and nearly 18% have reported 4 or more ( 3 ). These experiences warrant attention because the effects may accumulate, potentially resulting in lifelong adverse health and social consequences. People exposed to ACEs are more likely to report mental illness ( 4 ), substance use ( 5 ), interpersonal violence ( 6 ), and other health-risk behaviors. The cumulative effects of ACEs may also contribute to toxic stress, disrupt healthy development, and increase vulnerability to chronic health conditions later in life ( 7 ). Because these health outcomes and potential negative effects occur across the life course, efforts to reduce exposure to ACEs are critical. Preventing ACEs requires creating safe, stable, and nurturing relationships and environments that reduce children’s exposure to adversity ( 8 ). However, opportunities to identify and respond to ACEs through screening, referral, and supportive services may not be accessible to all. These challenges warrant consideration of more opportunities to screen for ACEs and offer support beyond childhood for people with a history of ACEs. One promising approach is to focus on emerging adults. The objective of this essay is to describe how higher education institutions (HEIs) can serve as an opportune setting to address ACEs among emerging adults. Expanding the Focus on ACEs to Emerging Adulthood Policies, programs, and early interventions can decrease the prevalence of ACEs by reducing children’s exposure to adversity and strengthen relationships and environments that protect their well-being. However, some approaches implemented during childhood rely on the involvement of parents or guardians, whose engagement may not always be available or possible. Extending attention beyond childhood provides opportunities to address earlier adversity as people enter new stages of life. Emerging adulthood, generally spanning ages 18 to 29 years, is a period during which people undergo transition, including navigating their independence, exploring their identity, and embracing their career and educational choices ( 9 ). Focusing on emerging adults provides an opportunity to strengthen protective factors and, from a life course perspective, intervene before the consequences accumulate or become more pronounced ( 10 ). Access to supportive relationships, health and behavioral health services, stable housing and financial resources, and other forms of social support may help emerging adults navigate the effects of prior adversity while managing new challenges ( 11 , 12 ). In the early stages of this period, emerging adults may no longer be interacting with child- and family-serving systems through which ACE prevention and early intervention efforts are often delivered. Identifying settings that maintain sustained contact with emerging adults is therefore important for extending ACE-related support. For the millions of emerging adults who pursue postsecondary education, colleges and universities are one such setting. Strengths of HEIs in Addressing ACEs Millions of emerging adults are enrolled in colleges and universities in the US, positioning HEIs to offer ACE-related support. Some HEIs have implemented efforts to address trauma and childhood adversity, such as The Ohio State University’s Advocacy and Resources for Trauma initiative ( 13 ); however, the potential role of HEIs in addressing ACEs extends beyond individual programs or initiatives. Recognition of the opportunity for HEIs to assume a greater role in addressing trauma and adversity is growing ( 14 , 15 ). As complex institutions, HEIs engage emerging adults across multiple aspects of their lives and have many strengths that make them an ideal setting: for example, their infrastructure, resources, established systems of support, and opportunities for social support. Thus, a university-wide approach that includes student screenings, referrals, and trauma-informed interventions can be advantageous. The diverse resources, relationships, and organizational structures found at HEIs provide a strong foundation and capacity to promote student health and play a meaningful role in addressing ACEs. For this essay, we outline 2 examples of strengths, existing infrastructure and the social environment. Established infrastructure is one of the greatest strengths of HEIs. Their institutional capacity allows ACE-related efforts to be integrated into existing systems and, where feasible, coordinated across multiple areas of the institution. For example, communication platforms (eg, Canvas, social media) are routinely used to announce campus events, share resources, and connect with students. These platforms could similarly be used to disseminate key information about ACEs, increase awareness of available resources, and highlight available campus- or community-based services. HEIs also have varying levels of a health infrastructure to support student well-being, ranging from wellness and counseling centers to health clinics, pharmacies, and at some institutions, university-affiliated hospitals. Clinics and counseling centers may incorporate trauma-informed practices, identify students who may benefit from additional support, and establish referral pathways. Moreover, because HEIs are valuable sources of health care and supportive services, particularly for students who have relocated to attend college, these services and pharmacies can support continuity of care for those receiving treatment related to past trauma or adversity ( 16 ). Another strength is the social environment. Through residential life, extracurricular activities, campus events, and other opportunities for collegiate interaction, students are in an environment that can help them develop their social network and support systems. The many student organizations and groups on campus, for example, can offer a sense of belonging and community, an essential protective factor ( 17 ). Supportive relationships with faculty, advisors, and other trusted adults may also serve as a protective factor for students who have a history of trauma or abuse, potentially serving as a vital resource that fosters resilience and emotional safety ( 18 ). As students’ needs and circumstances evolve throughout their educational journey, positive interactions with others may increase their willingness to participate in ACE-related initiatives or support their healing journey. Ultimately, the social aspect of attending an HEI is a critical strength. Beyond the classroom, campuses support positive student interactions and provide opportunities for students to build meaningful relationships that can provide sources of social and emotional support for students.

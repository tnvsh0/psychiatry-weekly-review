### Evaluating large language models versus human therapists in generating interventions for patients with depression in psychodynamic psychotherapy
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology (J Consult Clin Psychol) *(IF: 5.2)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Bar-Shachar Y et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 29

OBJECTIVE: Recent advances in artificial intelligence, especially the rise of large language models (LLMs), hold great promise for transforming mental health care, but their growing integration also raises pressing questions about their ability to deliver meaningful therapeutic interventions. This study compared the capabilities of LLMs to those of human therapists in producing context-sensitive psychotherapy responses within 16-session supportive-expressive psychodynamic psychotherapy for patients with depression. Specifically, it compared LLM and human therapists on (a) the frequency and typicality of their intervention choice and (b) the quality and general helpfulness of their interventions.

METHOD: We developed an LLM-based framework composed of four specialized LLM agents, each representing a different therapeutic dimension, and a Judge-LLM that selects the most appropriate intervention for a given context. A sample of 300 therapy session segments, each containing both human- and LLM-generated responses, served as the evaluation data set. Expert clinical judges assessed interventions for typicality and quality.

RESULTS: LLMs and human therapists relied on broadly similar intervention types. However, the contextual choice of when to use a specific type differed. Human interventions were rated as generally more helpful. Specifically, humans produced higher quality meaning-making interventions, while no differences were found for supportive, directive, or exploratory interventions.

CONCLUSIONS: The findings suggest that under the specific locally deployed multiagent configuration tested, LLMs can generate a range of context-sensitive therapeutic responses but showed limitations in producing high-quality meaning-making interventions. These findings highlight both the promise and the current boundaries of such systems as supervised therapeutic adjuncts. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

### Job Coaching and Smartphone-Based Cognitive Behavioral Techniques for Returning to Work and Coping With Unemployment (DRIVEN): Randomized Controlled Trial
**כתב עת:** 📄 J Med Internet Res | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Griffith JW et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 20

Abstract Background Unemployment has economic consequences and is associated with emotional distress and impairment. Joblessness is often associated with depression, leading to a vicious cycle of reduced drive to seek work, deepening financial stress, and worsening depression. Few scalable interventions exist that integrate job-search strategies with cognitive behavioral techniques. Objective The study aimed to determine the efficacy of a Distress-Informed Return-to-Work Intervention (DRIVEN). The primary hypothesis was that DRIVEN would be more efficacious than the control condition in improving job-search behavior. Secondary analyses examined job attainment, depression, and positive affect. Methods A randomized controlled trial was conducted from August 2023 to September 2024. Assessments were at baseline, weeks 1 through 6, and 8 and 16 weeks postintervention. Participants were a nationwide sample of unemployed job seekers aged 18 years or older who had never earned more than US $100,000 in a year. Participants received either (1) DRIVEN, which included 6 weeks of cognitive behavioral exercises in a mobile app combined with three 1-hour coaching sessions over videoconference or (2) emailed articles containing job-search advice. Primary outcome measures were subscales of a modified Job Search Behavior Scale (preparatory and active job search). Secondary outcomes included job attainment and mental health outcomes. Results Participants were a nationwide sample (N=126; mean age 33.6, SD 6.6 y), including 62 (49.2%) females. During the acute treatment phase, mixed-effects linear models showed that for DRIVEN, preparatory job-search behavior increased by an estimated 0.053 (95% CI 0.031-0.076) units per day from baseline, which was a larger effect than in the control condition (0.011, 95% CI −0.011 to 0.033 units per day; P =.008). Active job-search behavior increased by 0.061 (95% CI 0.038-0.084) units per day from baseline, which was significantly larger than in the control group (point estimate: 0.025, 95% CI 0.001-0.047; P =.03). Depression decreased more in the DRIVEN group vs control (DRIVEN point estimate=−0.214, 95% CI −0.255 to −0.172 vs control group point estimate=−0.122, 95% CI −0.163 to −0.081; P =.002), and participants were more likely to get a job in the DRIVEN group (68.3% vs 50.8%; P =.046). Self-report outcomes did not significantly differ during the follow-up periods. Conclusions DRIVEN was superior to emailed job-search advice for the primary outcome (job-search behavior) and secondary outcomes (job attainment, depression, and positive affect). This intervention may be beneficial for distressed unemployed individuals seeking work.

Introduction Employment is a social determinant of health that can affect an individual’s economic status, social connection, and access to health care, especially in the United States, where health insurance is often obtained through an individual’s employer [ 1 - 3 ]. As of May 2026, the US Department of Labor reported that approximately 4.3% of the population (over 7 million individuals) were unemployed [ 4 ], which not only creates costs to the government to cover unemployment insurance but also leaves these individuals without income, social connections, and a sense of meaning and purpose [ 2 ]. As such, it is unsurprising that unemployment has been associated with both symptoms of depression and major depressive disorder [ 5 ]. Joblessness also can lead to a vicious cycle of reduced drive to seek work, deepening financial stress, and worsening depression [ 6 - 8 ]. Thus, there is an urgent need for scalable programs that can both treat emotional distress—such as depression and anxiety—as well as teach effective job-search strategies. In fact, previous meta-analyses of job-search interventions have found they can reduce depression, but few studies have assessed mental health outcomes more broadly [ 9 ]. For these reasons, we created a smartphone app–delivered intervention for job seekers (Distress-Informed Return-to-Work Intervention [DRIVEN]) that (1) integrates job-search strategies with cognitive and behavioral techniques to address emotional distress and diminished motivation and (2) includes brief videoconference meetings with a job coach to support motivation, review intervention techniques, and discuss job-search strategies. In this way, DRIVEN combines the strong evidence for cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for depression [ 10 ] with practical support and coaching specific for a job search. This research builds upon existing evidence that cognitive behavioral skills may aid job seekers. For example, in a sample of 75 job seekers, cognitive behavioral skills predicted job-search self-efficacy, even after covarying for depression [ 11 ]. In addition, Della-Posta and Drummond [ 12 ] found unemployed job seekers (n=39) experienced less distress and were more likely to find work when receiving a combination of cognitive behavioral techniques and job-search support vs standard job-search support alone. In another study, unemployed individuals completed either a 7-week CBT (n=94) or control program (n=89) that both emphasized social support. After the program, the CBT group reported better mental health outcomes and more participants found full-time employment [ 13 ]. The purpose of this research was to build upon previous studies to determine whether DRIVEN—which was designed to be brief, mostly self-guided, and smartphone-based—could help unemployed job seekers attain jobs and improve mental health. Our primary hypothesis was that the DRIVEN intervention would lead to more job-search behavior at a postintervention assessment point and follow-ups at 8 and 16 weeks, relative to a control condition designed to reflect usual job-search behavior (ie, weekly emails containing articles and recommendations for job seekers). Secondary outcome measures included self-report measures of depression, positive affect, and job attainment. Job-search behaviors and other outcomes were captured at baseline, during each of the 6 weeks of the intervention, and again at the 8- and 16-week postintervention follow-ups. Additional details about the study design, study protocol, and statistical analysis plan can be found in our protocol study [ 14 ]. The study was funded by the National Institute of Mental Health; the sponsor had no role in the design, conduct, analysis, or reporting of the trial. Throughout the study, an advisory board was maintained, comprising community members and experts in human resources, business, clinical psychology, and community engagement. A Data Safety Monitoring Board comprising a clinical psychologist with expertise in digital interventions and 2 statisticians was maintained; it met midway through the trial and approved continuation based on its review of data and procedures. The first participant was enrolled on August 7, 2023. The study was retrospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov on August 30, 2023, and the registration was publicly visible on September 7, 2023. Ethical Considerations Data were stored in REDCap, which allowed data to be exported for analysis without identifying information, such as names and addresses [ 15 ]. Access to the REDCap database was available only to approved study team members, and data files used for analysis were kept on secure computers. ### A Behavior Change Technique-Based Text Messaging Intervention for Obesity Among Employed Adult Men Younger Than 40 Years: Pilot Randomized Controlled Trial
**כתב עת:** 📄 JMIR Form Res | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Tomotake C, Ohyama Y | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 20

Abstract Background In Japan, employed men younger than 40 years are not eligible for specific health guidance, and evidence regarding obesity interventions targeting this population is limited. Furthermore, due to the shortage of human resources in occupational health settings, a need has arisen for simple and implementable support methods. Support via SMS text message is expected to be a low-cost and scalable method for health guidance; however, its effectiveness in this target population has not been sufficiently evaluated. Objective This pilot randomized controlled trial aimed to assessed the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary effectiveness of an SMS text messaging intervention among employed men younger than 40 years with obesity (BMI ≥25 kg/m²). Methods An unblinded randomized controlled trial was conducted with 35 participants (intervention group, n=17; control group, n=18). The intervention group received one-way SMS text messaging based on behavior change techniques targeting diet and physical activity, delivered 3 times per week for 12 weeks (36 messages total). The control group received a pamphlet and a self-monitoring record sheet at baseline. The primary outcome was the proportion achieving ≥3% weight loss. Secondary outcomes included BMI, waist circumference, and psychological and behavioral measures. Feasibility and acceptability were also assessed. Results The proportion achieving ≥3% weight loss was 23.5% (4/17) in the intervention group and 5.6% (1/18) in the control group ( P =.18; odds ratio 5.23). Waist circumference significantly decreased in the intervention group compared to baseline ( t 16 =2.71, P=. 02), whereas no significant between-group differences were observed in weight, BMI, or psychological measures. The SMS delivery rate was 95.5%, with a total cost of JP¥ 15,264 over 12 weeks. The follow-up rate was 100%, and the ratings for satisfaction, utilization, and willingness to recommend were 3.5, 2.8, and 3.0 out of 5, respectively. Conclusions The findings support the feasibility and acceptability of an SMS text messaging intervention for health guidance among employed men younger than 40 years with obesity, although evidence of its effectiveness remains preliminary. Further research with larger samples is needed to evaluate its effectiveness and optimize the intervention.

Introduction Obesity is a major global health issue, with projections suggesting that by 2035, approximately 50% of the world’s population will be overweight or obese [ 1 ]. In Japan, given the risks of obesity-related health problems, a BMI of 25 kg/m² or higher is classified as obesity [ 2 ]. This differs from the World Health Organization classification, which defines obesity in adults as a BMI of 30 kg/m² or higher [ 3 ]. Obesity in men is characterized by its onset in young adulthood. According to the 2024 National Health and Nutrition Survey conducted by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare, the prevalence of obesity (BMI ≥25 kg/m²) among Japanese adults was 25.3%; among men aged 20‐29 and 30‐39 years, the rates were 21.0% and 34.3%, respectively [ 4 ]. These findings suggest that intervention from young adulthood through early middle age is crucial. Factors contributing to obesity in this age group include work-related stress, irregular eating habits, and physical inactivity [ 5 ]. In individuals with obesity, the accumulation of visceral fat increases the risk of metabolic disorders and atherosclerotic diseases; however, weight loss achieved through lifestyle modifications can improve blood glucose levels, lipid levels, and blood pressure [ 2 ]. Therefore, interventions supporting lifestyle changes are crucial for the prevention and management of obesity. In Japan, specific health checkups and specific health guidance are conducted for individuals aged 40 years and older, and certain improvements have been reported [ 6 , 7 ]. Some reports also indicate that improvements in cardiovascular risk factors other than obesity have been limited [ 8 ]. On the other hand, health management support for employed men younger than 40 years, who are not eligible for specific health checkups, is primarily left to voluntary corporate initiatives; however, due to a shortage of occupational health professionals, many workplaces have difficulty providing sufficient support [ 9 ]. Evidence for obesity interventions targeting employed men younger than 40 years is also scarce. To the best of our knowledge, obesity intervention studies targeting young employed men are limited to a single report combining website use with face-to-face support [ 10 ]. Against this backdrop, there is a need to develop simple and feasible intervention methods for this population. In recent years, the spread of information and communication technology has led to the expansion of lifestyle improvement support using mobile health (mHealth). In obesity interventions, support using text messages has been reported to offer implementation advantages over app-based interventions, such as lower dropout rates and higher reach and open rates [ 11 , 12 ]. Although the effectiveness of SMS text messaging–based obesity interventions in adults has been demonstrated [ 13 ], the same review identified a lack of sufficient analysis of the sex-specific effects of SMS text messaging interventions as a key challenge. Men are significantly underrepresented in obesity intervention studies [ 14 , 15 ]; to the best of our knowledge, no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have evaluated SMS text messaging as the primary intervention method for employed men younger than 40 years. Behavior change techniques (BCTs) constitute an internationally standardized framework based on intervention components common to numerous existing health behavior theories [ 16 , 17 ]. BCTs have been incorporated into text message interventions for various health issues and applied to improve physical activity and dietary habits [ 18 - 21 ]. The Game of Stones Trials is an example of a text message intervention designed to explicitly incorporate BCTs and target individuals with obesity. In this trial, narrative-style text messages incorporating BCTs were developed and evaluated in a multiarm parallel-group randomized controlled trial involving men with obesity aged 18 years or older with a BMI of 30 kg/m² or higher [ 22 ]. However, this study targeted the general adult male population, and findings on employed men younger than 40 years are limited. ### Crisis Interventions for Children and Adolescents with Depression and Suicidality: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
**כתב עת:** 📄 Curr Psychiatry Rep | **סוג מחקר:** מטה-אנליזה | **מחברים:** Díaz-Téllez C et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 26

PURPOSE OF REVIEW: To review the evidence on crisis services for children and adolescents experiencing acute psychopathological crises, particularly severe depression, suicidal ideation, self-harm, and suicidal behaviour, and to examine their effectiveness in reducing symptoms, improving family functioning, and preventing hospitalisation.

RECENT FINDINGS: A systematic review was conducted following PRISMA guidelines and registered in PROSPERO. Searches of PubMed, Web of Science, PsycInfo, and ClinicalTrials.gov identified 15 eligible studies evaluating 15 crisis interventions. Four interventions targeted suicidal ideation and self-harm through brief, structured approaches, demonstrating reductions in suicidal thoughts, suicide attempts, and emergency department visits. Seven family-focused interventions (evaluated in eight studies) improved communication, family cohesion, and reduced hospitalisation rates. Three interventions aimed to stabilise crises and facilitate linkage with community services, improving continuity of care and decreasing emergency service utilisation. One residential intervention (evaluated in two studies) showed mixed findings, with some behavioural improvements but limited effects on internalising symptoms. Current evidence suggests that crisis services might be a promising alternative to hospitalisation for children and adolescents experiencing acute mental health crises. These interventions provide intensive support within the young person's natural environment, promote family involvement, and were associated with reductions in suicidal ideation, depressive symptoms, emergency visits, and hospital admissions. Despite promising findings, the evidence base remains limited, largely observational and heterogeneous, and causal conclusions cannot be drawn. Future research should directly compare different crisis service models and include long-term follow-up to determine the most effective approaches and their sustainability over time.

### Electroacupuncture Combined with Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia Alleviates Insomnia-Anxiety Comorbidity in Gynecological Cancer Patients Undergoing Chemotherapy: A Randomized Controlled Trial
**כתב עת:** 📄 Curr Oncol | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Xu T et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 19

Simple Summary Insomnia and anxiety often occur together in gynecological cancer patients receiving chemotherapy. These symptoms reduce quality of life and can interfere with treatment. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) helps many patients, but responses vary. This randomized trial tested whether adding electroacupuncture (EA) to CBT-I could improve outcomes. Seventy-six patients received either EA plus CBT-I or sham EA plus CBT-I for 4 weeks. The real EA group had better sleep, less insomnia, and lower anxiety. More patients in this group achieved meaningful improvement. Sleep tracker data also indicated increases in deeper sleep and REM sleep. No serious adverse effects occurred. These findings suggest that adding EA to CBT-I is a safe and effective option for this population.

Abstract Insomnia and anxiety affect many gynecological cancer patients on chemotherapy, and non-pharmacological options are limited. This randomized trial examined whether adding electroacupuncture (EA) to cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) improves both conditions. Seventy-six patients received EA + CBT-I or sham EA + CBT-I for 4 weeks. Outcomes included sleep quality (PSQI), insomnia severity (AIS), anxiety (HAMA), objective sleep parameters, and overall response rate. At Week 4, the EA group showed a greater reduction in PSQI global score (mean difference in change from baseline: −3.11, 95% CI: −4.11 to −2.11, p < 0.001). More patients in the EA group achieved AIS ≤ 6 (71.1% vs. 42.1%, p = 0.010) and HAMA ≤ 14 (92.1% vs. 65.8%, p = 0.005). Deep sleep ( p = 0.001), REM sleep ( p = 0.045), and sleep score ( p = 0.008) also increased more with EA group. The overall response rate was higher with EA (92.1% vs. 71.1%, p = 0.016). No serious adverse events occurred. These findings suggest that adding EA to CBT-I improves sleep and anxiety in this population, with a favorable safety profile.

1. Introduction Insomnia symptoms—difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or early morning awakening—affect 30% to 60% of cancer patients, well above the 10% to 20% observed in the general population [ 1 , 2 ]. These figures come mainly from self-reported symptom scales rather than structured diagnostic interviews, and they cover mixed cancer populations and treatment stages. Among gynecological cancer patients, the risk is even higher. Female sex, hormone changes after surgery, and chemotherapy neurotoxicity all play a role [ 3 ]. Poor sleep in these patients lowers quality of life, weakens immune function, and may reduce treatment adherence and worsen cancer outcomes [ 4 ]. Sleep and immunity also are also closely linked: poor sleep promotes inflammation, which may in turn drive tumor progression and treatment resistance [ 5 ]. Insomnia and anxiety frequently coexist in cancer patients, with comorbidity rates of 30–50%. Patients with both conditions generally have more severe symptoms and poorer treatment responses than those with either condition alone [ 6 ]. Some observational studies have also linked insomnia symptoms to poorer overall survival in cancer patients, although these findings are correlational and cannot establish causality [ 7 ]. Common mechanisms include HPA axis over-activity, GABA and serotonin dysregulation, and chronic low-grade inflammation [ 8 ]. This suggests that insomnia and anxiety reflect a shared underlying dysregulation and should be treated together rather than as separate problems [ 9 ]. Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs, commonly used for insomnia, carry risks of tolerance, dependence, daytime sedation, and interactions with chemotherapies [ 10 ]. These risks, together with many cancer patients’ preference for non-drug options, have driven interest in behavioral and mind–body approaches [ 11 ]. CBT-I is the first-line non-pharmacological treatment and has shown benefit in cancer survivors [ 12 ]. However, many patients do not achieve full remission with CBT-I alone. Its effect on co-occurring anxiety is limited, especially in the short term, and delivering CBT-I requires time and trained staff, which are often in short supply in oncology clinics [ 13 ]. Over the past 20 years, acupuncture has become increasingly integrated into cancer care, with evidence supporting its effects on pain, nausea, and fatigue, and newer data suggesting benefits for sleep and mood problems [ 14 ]. Electroacupuncture (EA) was selected over manual acupuncture for several reasons. EA provides standardized, reproducible stimulation parameters, which reduces variability between practitioners and enhances methodological rigor. A recent Bayesian network meta-analysis of acupuncture therapies for insomnia found that EA ranked favorably for improving sleep efficiency [ 15 ]. EA has also been investigated specifically for insomnia during systemic cancer therapy; a meta-analysis of randomized trials in breast cancer patients showed significant improvements in sleep outcomes [ 16 ]. Furthermore, EA has demonstrated anxiolytic effects in clinical and preclinical studies, potentially through modulation of HPA axis activity, GABAergic transmission, and neuroimmune pathways [ 17 ]. It has also been used safely during chemotherapy without evidence of increased adverse events or interference with chemotherapy efficacy. Key questions remain. Few studies have focused specifically on acupuncture for insomnia–anxiety comorbidity in gynecological cancer patients receiving active chemotherapy [ 18 , 19 ]. Most existing trials have small samples, lack sham controls, or rely only on subjective measures [ 20 ]. It is also unclear whether adding acupuncture to CBT-I provides extra benefit—a question with direct clinical relevance. To address these questions, we designed a randomized, sham-controlled trial based on the “Tiaoshen Ningxin” acupuncture principle. This traditional Chinese medicine framework focuses on regulating the “brain spirit” through Governor Vessel points (Baihui GV20, Shenting GV24) and calming the “heart spirit” through Heart Meridian points (Shenmen HT7), with the addition of Yintang (EX-HN3) and electroacupuncture applied at Baihui and Yintang. We hypothesized that this protocol, combined with CBT-I, would improve insomnia and anxiety more than sham acupuncture plus CBT-I.

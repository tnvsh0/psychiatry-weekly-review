### Context Matters, Doesn't It? The Role of Context in Everyday Emotion Regulation Strategy Use
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Psychological Science (Psychol Sci) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Tran A et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 29

A common maxim holds that "context is everything." This perspective transformed the field of emotion regulation, which evolved from understanding  which  regulation strategies people use, to understanding  how  strategy use varies by context (i.e., strategy-situation fit). Theoretical accounts suggest contextual features are crucial in guiding strategy use. However, empirical evidence is isolated in piecemeal studies with inconclusive results. To comprehensively explore context-dependent emotion regulation, we need data on emotion regulation across many contexts, at scale. Combining up to 11 experience-sampling data sets collected from Belgian and Australian samples in an integrative analysis ( N ID  = 500-1,696,  N observations  = 27,023-101,114,  M age  = 25.38 years, 71% women), we examined how regulation-strategy use varies as a function of three theory-derived contextual features- intensity ,  controllability , and  social features  (i.e., the presence and closeness of others). We found limited support that context matters for daily emotion-regulation strategy use. Our study provides a well-powered foundational step to test a theory central to emotion-regulation research and highlights the complexity of capturing strategy-situation fit in daily life.

### How we see relations: The case of containment and support
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Vettori S et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov

To navigate and act in the environment, it is necessary to recognize objects and the relationships between them. Recognition of objects in visual scenes is rapid and automatic. Are relations between objects in a scene also extracted rapidly and automatically during perception? Here, we considered containment and support, two relations that are fundamental to our understanding of the physical world. Using frequency-tagging electroencephalography on human subjects, we measured stimulus-driven neural responses during rapid visual presentation of images (4 Hz), with a periodic oddball structure to isolate the selective response to object pairs presented side-by-side (e.g., a book and a table) versus two objects in support or containment relations (e.g., a book on a table). We found enhanced responses to objects in support/containment relations (vs. objects side-by-side) when a relation was instantiated by different exemplars of the same scene (e.g., a book on a table), but not by different scenes involving distinct object categories (e.g., a book on a table, a lamp on a stand, a laptop on a desk). These findings indicate that, during scene perception, relations are extracted in a format that generalizes within, but not across, types of visual scenes. We propose that, during perception, relations are not represented as discrete, abstract tokens ("in" or "on") but rather as objects' properties, such that changes in the objects entail changes in the relational representation. These results provide new insight into the perceptual foundations of relational processing underlying physical reasoning. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

### Investigating how reward retroactively improves memory for associations and items: A registered report
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Tarder-Stoll H et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov

Given we cannot remember all our experiences, it seems intuitive that we should prioritize events of consequence, like those that lead to a reward. However, rewards have had inconsistent effects on people's memory for preceding neutral experiences in experiments, raising questions as to whether our minds are equipped to remember adaptively. Here, we used online testing in a registered report to investigate whether rewards tend to retroactively enhance certain types of memories. Consistent with prior research and theories involving how dopamine modulates the hippocampus, we hypothesized that reward would retroactively enhance memory in a delay-dependent manner, emerging only after a period of consolidation. Crucially, we further predicted that this long-delay modulation of memory would be stronger for associative memory than for item recognition-a factor not previously considered despite its clear theoretical grounding. Our planned confirmatory analyses did not reveal any evidence for retroactive effects of reward on either associative or item memory. However, in exploratory analyses examining only individuals who were behaviorally sensitive to the reward manipulation, as measured by an independent task, we found evidence that reward retroactively enhanced associative-but not item-memory in a delay-dependent manner. This work sheds light on important open questions about the mechanisms by which reward retroactively enhances memory, elucidating the conditions under which this key aspect of adaptive memory emerges. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

### Stronger mnemonic prediction errors can disproportionately impair memory updating in older adults
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Seo MS et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 26

Mnemonic prediction errors are discrepancies between memory-based expectations and actual events that can trigger memory updating. However, the conditions under which these prediction errors result in successful memory updating, and whether these effects vary between younger and older adults, remain less understood. In two experiments, the present study investigated how prediction strength affects memory for events that violated memory-based expectations in younger and older adults. Prediction strength was manipulated by first presenting three-word sequences (i.e., trains), each with exemplars from the same category ( Bluejay-Hawk-Eagle ), either once or four times. Predictions based on memory for trains were then confirmed by repeating sequences entirely ( Bluejay-Hawk-Eagle ) or violated by repeating the first two words and changing the ending word ( Bluejay-Hawk-Robin ). Differences in prediction strength, created by varying repetitions, were validated using retrospective judgments (Experiment 1) and prospective predictions of original train endings along with confidence ratings (Experiment 2). Prediction error effects on memory updating were assessed by presenting the first two words of trains as cues for recalling changed ending words. Stronger predictions impaired memory for changed endings disproportionately for older adults, and older adults were less able to recollect that endings had changed when asked directly. Regardless of prediction strength and age, change recollection was consistently associated with better memory for changed ending words. Collectively, these findings suggest that stronger mnemonic predictions exacerbate the negative impact of prediction errors on the resolution of proactive interference, an ability that is often impaired in healthy aging. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

### Strength-based organizational approaches facilitate workplace opportunities for people from lower socioeconomic status backgrounds
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Silverman DM et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov

The workplace stands as one of the most powerful drivers of societal (in)equality. For employees from lower socioeconomic status backgrounds, it can represent both a ticket toward upward mobility and a consequential source of stigma. Four studies build on emerging strength-based research to test whether workplaces can challenge this stigma by creating overarching cultures that recognize the beneficial skills employees gain from their otherwise marginalized identities. Study 1 provided foundational insights into these "strength-based organizational approaches" and their implications for key outcomes derived from the State Authenticity as Fit to Environment model. Employees from lower socioeconomic status backgrounds were especially unlikely to report that their workplaces valued their identities and associated skills, which partially explained subsequent disparities in employees' broad sense of comfort and efficacy at work. Studies 2a and 2b established these patterns experimentally by demonstrating that exposing participants to a workplace that does communicate a strength-based organizational approach facilitated these professionally productive outcomes while increasing participants' likelihood of disclosing their marginalized identities within a job application task. Study 3 extended these findings to consider how the approaches may also indirectly expand employees' equitable access to opportunity through reducing common biases among their supervisors. Randomly assigning workplace supervisors to briefly engage with a company that highlighted the value of employees' lower socioeconomic status backgrounds increased supervisors' positive evaluations and likelihood of promoting these employees by 38% relative to an active control condition. Collectively, these preregistered findings illuminate new opportunities to improve workplace functioning and pinpoint the organizational forces underlying person-context interactions. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

### Children can suppress salient distractors
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Ramgir A et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov

From a very young age, our perceptual system is particularly sensitive to salient events. When such events are irrelevant, adults can prevent them from capturing attention by suppressing the salient features and by prioritizing task-relevant information. Previous research suggests that both distractor-suppression and target-prioritization mechanisms are not yet fully developed at ages 5-6. However, this work has not clearly disentangled the two mechanisms. Here, we specifically investigated whether children can suppress the salient feature of an irrelevant distractor. Two groups of children (aged 5-6 and 8-9) searched for a shape-defined target among heterogeneously shaped nontargets. All items had the same color throughout the experiment, but on two thirds of the trials, one item (the singleton distractor) had a unique color that changed at the beginning of each block. We expected this distractor to interfere with search performance during the first half of each block (i.e., when its color was novel). The critical question was whether this interference would be reduced during the second block half (i.e., with enough experience with the distractor's color). This finding would indicate that the children suppressed the salient distractor color. We found clear evidence for feature-based suppression, and this effect was similar for the two age groups. These findings indicate that feature-based suppression develops early and may already be established by ages 5-6. More broadly, they are consistent with accounts in which suppression emerges through implicit learning or habituation-like mechanisms that are operational early in development. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

### Beliefs about the causal interdependence of virtues and vices
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Hardin B et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov

How do people think different positive and negative moral traits (i.e., virtues and vices) affect one another? For example, do they think that improving one virtue (e.g., honesty) will facilitate improvements in another virtue (e.g., compassion)? Do people believe in "master virtues" that, when improved, have disproportionate positive impacts on other moral virtues? Here, we examine perceived causal interdependence among 15 moral virtues and 13 moral vices. Across four studies, participants indicated their beliefs about how increasing their virtues would impact their other virtues (Study 1; 275 Prolific participants), how increasing their vices would impact their other vices (Study 2; 423 undergraduate students), and how increases in virtues would affect vices (or vice versa; Studies 3 and 4; 337 undergraduate students and 298 Prolific participants). We find people generally believe in facilitation effects, where improving one trait improves other traits and worsening one trait worsens other traits. Specifically, people believe that increasing one virtue tends to increase other virtues (Study 1) and decrease other vices (Studies 3 and 4). Similarly, people believe that increasing one vice tends to increase other vices (Study 2) and decrease other virtues (Studies 3 and 4). Perceived trade-offs were rare and involved laziness and cowardice decreasing or being decreased by other vices. There was little evidence for beliefs in a "master virtue." However, we find some evidence that people think cruelty and greed are disproportionately impactful "master vices." These results enhance our understanding of lay moral concepts by painting a comprehensive picture of people's beliefs about the causal interdependence among moral virtues and vices. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

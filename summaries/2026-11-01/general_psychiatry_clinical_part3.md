### Prefrontal Cortical Changes With Mindfulness-Oriented Recovery Enhancement in Opioid Use Disorder: A Randomized Clinical Trial
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Psychiatry *(IF: 25.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Huang Y et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 28

Key Points Question What are the neural mechanisms through which Mindfulness-Oriented Recovery Enhancement (MORE) restructures hedonic processing in individuals with opioid use disorder (OUD), and how are these changes related to clinical outcomes? Findings In this neuroimaging randomized clinical trial, compared with psychoeducational supportive therapy (PST), MORE restructured drug-biased prefrontal activity during regulation of drug vs nondrug reward as further associated with lower drug craving. Compared with PST, MORE also reduced prefrontal synchronization during a naturalistic drug-related stimulus and extended abstinence duration at follow-up. Meaning These findings suggest that MORE modulates prefrontal systems involved in reward, salience, and cognitive control, supporting its potential as a mechanistically informed adjunct to pharmacotherapy for OUD.

Importance Despite decades of clinical implementation of medications for opioid use disorder (OUD), overdose mortality rates remain high, underscoring a critical need for interventions that target the brain mechanisms underlying addiction. Objective To assess the neural mechanisms of Mindfulness-Oriented Recovery Enhancement (MORE) and their associations with clinical outcomes, including craving and abstinence duration. Design, Setting, and Participants This randomized clinical trial was conducted from November 2020 to September 2024 among individuals with OUD recruited from an inpatient rehabilitation facility in New York and randomized to MORE or psychoeducational supportive therapy (PST). Data were analyzed from October 2024 to August 2026. Interventions Treatment with MORE (comprising training in mindfulness, drug cue reappraisal, and savoring positive experiences) or PST (active control condition) across 8 weekly 2-hour group sessions. Main Outcomes and Measures Outcomes included treatment-related changes in functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) blood oxygen level–dependent signal (primary), abstinence length (secondary), and cue-induced craving (secondary). Results Drug cue reactivity task analyses included 59 participants (MORE, n = 24; 5 women [20.8%]; mean [SD] age, 43.6 [11.3] years; PST, n = 35; 8 women [22.9%]; mean [SD] age, 42.8 [10.0] years). Movie-watching and follow-up analyses were conducted in subsets of 37 (MORE, n = 18; PST, n = 19) and 38 participants (MORE, n = 13; PST, n = 25), respectively. Compared with PST, MORE reduced drug-biased activity in the middle frontal gyrus and other brain areas, including the precuneus, during downregulation of responses to drug cues relative to upregulation of responses to natural reward cues (even when controlling for passive cue viewing; z > 3.1; P < .05). The shift from drug to natural reward responses in the lateral and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (PFC) was associated with lower cue-induced craving exclusively in the MORE group ( z > 3.1; P < .05). Relative to PST, MORE also reduced medial PFC synchronization to naturalistic drug-related movie scenes (q = 0.019) and significantly extended abstinence duration at follow-up (approximately 4 months posttreatment; χ 2 = 6.7; P = .04; Cohen w = 0.19). Conclusions and Relevance This neuroimaging randomized clinical trial demonstrates that MORE may restructure function in PFC nodes of the reward, salience, and control networks, positioning it as a biologically grounded adjunct to pharmacotherapy for OUD. Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04112186

### Instrumental Support Calls vs. Caring Contacts Texts to Prevent Suicide in Adolescents and Adults: The SPARC Randomized Clinical Trial
**כתב עת:** ⭐ The American Journal of Psychiatry (Am J Psychiatry) *(IF: 18.1)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Radin AK et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 28

OBJECTIVE: The authors sought to determine whether augmenting usual suicide care with follow-up in the form of instrumental support calls (ISC) or two-way Caring Contacts texts (CC) works best to reduce suicidal ideation and behavior (SI/SB) in adolescents and adults.

METHODS: The Suicide Prevention Among Recipients of Care (SPARC) Trial is a pragmatic comparative effectiveness randomized clinical trial stratified by age, with participants randomized in a 1:1 ratio to ISC or CC. Participants were adolescents (12-17 years, N=723) and adults (18+ years, N=797) who screened at risk for suicide in emergency departments (N=9) and primary care clinics (N=36) between May 2021 and July 2022; primary data collection was completed in August 2023. Outcomes included Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale SI/SB score at 6 months (primary) and 12 months (secondary).

RESULTS: Adolescents (N=723; mean age, 14.9 years [SD=1.6]) were mostly female (75%), White (84.1%), and non-Hispanic (84.2%). Adults (N=797; mean age, 38.3 years [SD=15.9]) were mostly female (70%), White (89.1%), and non-Hispanic (90%). At 6 months, outcomes were similar by condition among adolescents, but among adults, SI/SB scores were significantly higher among those receiving CC compared to those receiving ISC (adjusted difference in means [ADM]=0.50, 95% CI=0.12, 0.88). At 12 months, adolescents' SI/SB scores were significantly lower in the CC compared with the ISC condition (ADM=-0.64, 95% CI=-1.11, -0.18), but outcomes were similar by condition among adults.

CONCLUSIONS: The best suicide prevention follow-up care may vary by age, with adolescents benefiting most from longer-term connection-focused two-way CC and adults benefiting most from more intensive early instrumental support through ISC.

### Hippocampal subregion volume alterations at baseline and following antidepressant treatment in youth at high risk for bipolar disorder
**כתב עת:** 📄 Transl Psychiatry | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Tang B et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Aug 1

Abstract Understanding hippocampal neurostructural alterations at baseline and following antidepressant treatment in high-risk youth, particularly within distinct subregions, is critical for elucidating bipolar disorder (BD) pathophysiology and treatment planning. In this prospective randomized clinical trial, 105 high-risk youth with a first-degree BD-I relative(s) and moderate to severe depression and/or anxiety disorder, along with 51 healthy controls (HCs), were recruited. All participants underwent baseline structural MRI and clinical evaluations for depression, anxiety, and adverse events. High-risk youth were then randomized to receive psychotherapy and with either escitalopram or placebo. After 4 weeks of treatment, high-risk youth underwent repeated structural MRI and clinical assessments. These clinical assessments continued biweekly over the remaining 12 weeks of treatment (up to week 16). Volumetric measurements of hippocampal subregions were analyzed at baseline and 4 weeks. Compared to HCs, high-risk youth had significant baseline volume increases in the right cornu ammonis (CA)3, bilateral CA4, bilateral granule cell and molecular layers of the dentate gyrus (GC-ML-DG), and bilateral molecular layer, but reduced volumes in the bilateral fimbria and left parasubiculum. Volume reduction in the left hippocampal tail after 4 weeks of escitalopram treatment was associated with improvements in disinhibition and impulsivity symptoms from baseline to 4 weeks, and this association remained significant from baseline to 16 weeks. CA3-4 and GC-ML-DG enlargement may reflect adaptive changes, while fimbria and parasubiculum atrophy indicate vulnerabilities in BD-risk youth. Escitalopram-related volume reduction in the left hippocampal tail may reflect neural plasticity associated with emotional symptom improvement in BD high-risk youth. Clinical trial registration information Mechanism of Antidepressant-Related Dysfunctional Arousal in High-Risk Youth; https://clinicaltrials.gov/ ; NCT02553161 .

Introduction Antidepressants are commonly prescribed to manage mood and anxiety disorders in youth, in spite of offering moderate benefits and being associated with potentially consequential psychiatric adverse events, such as treatment-emergent mania [ 1 , 2 ]. The risk for such adverse events is particularly pronounced in youth with a family history of bipolar disorder (BD), where the intersection between antidepressant use and familial risk factors may exacerbate inherent vulnerabilities toward adverse outcomes within key neurobiological systems that regulate emotion [ 3 ]. The hippocampus, critical for the regulation of emotion, cognitive functions and response to stress [ 4 , 5 ], plays a key role in understanding the pathophysiology and treatment of mood disorders, and undergoes significant plasticity with antidepressant exposure [ 6 ]. However, the specific impact of antidepressants on hippocampal structure — both in terms of efficacy and adverse events—remains inadequately explored in youth at familial risk for BD. Hippocampal volumes in BD range from being reduced or not significantly different from healthy controls (HCs) [ 7 – 9 ]. Whole volume analyses may have missed unique functional variations in hippocampal subregions, which play distinct roles in the pathophysiology of BD [ 10 , 11 ]. With advances in the resolution and accuracy of automatic hippocampal segmentations, fine-grained volume reductions have been observed in widespread hippocampal subfields such as cornu ammonis (CA)1-4, the granule cell and molecular layers of the dentate gyrus (GC-ML-DG), molecular layer, hippocampal-amygdala transition area (HATA), subiculum, and hippocampal tail in patients with BD [ 12 – 14 ]. Such patterns have also been supported by post-mortem neuropathological investigations of individuals with BD [ 15 ]. BD is among the most familial psychiatric disorders with high heritability rates; however, specific biomarkers of familial risk for BD have yet to achieve biomarker validation. Examination of anatomical variations in the hippocampus and its subregions in youth at high-risk (HR) for BD could determine whether these subregions could serve as risk factors for BD, potentially advancing predictive capabilities and preventative strategies for BD [ 16 ]. Like studies described above in BD, prior HR studies focused on total hippocampal volumes have reported inconsistent results, possibly due to volumetric averaging of its subregions. For example, in one study, increased volume in left parahippocampal/hippocampal gyrus was observed in HR youth compared to HCs [ 17 ], while another study noted trends for larger hippocampus volumes in unaffected co-twins discordant for BD compared to low-risk twins [ 16 ]. In a third study, Nery et al. reported decreased gray matter volumes in the left hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus, and thicker right hippocampus in BD unaffected first-degree relatives compared to HCs [ 18 ]. However, Hajek et al. reported comparable hippocampal volumes among unaffected relatives, affected relatives for BD, and HCs [ 19 ]. These studies underscore the significant variability of hippocampal volume differences among those at risk for BD. More fine-grain examinations of hippocampal subregions in youth at risk for BD, and in the context of symptom manifestations and treatment, may uncover subtle predispositions for BD than reported in the extant literature. Given that antidepressant exposure may be a major confound in the evaluation of bipolar risk, directly examining the effects of antidepressants on the hippocampus and its subregions provide a unique opportunity to both advance understanding of risk for developing BD and the effect of antidepressant treatment on hippocampal plasticity. Indeed, it is well known that antidepressant treatment can alter the structure of the hippocampus across species, and in specific subregions [ 20 ]. Antidepressants can both lead to adverse events or promote neurogenesis and enhance synaptic plasticity in the hippocampus, as antidepressants increases the dendritic spine density of neurons in CA pyramidal cell subfields and stimulates neurogenesis in the dentate gyrus [ 21 – 23 ], suggesting high potential for structural remodeling. Indeed, antidepressant exposure can lead to significant alterations in hippocampal subregion volumes (such as dentate gyrus) in rodent models, modulating function in areas critical for stress response and emotional regulation [ 24 , 25 ]. These insights provide a foundation for understanding how antidepressants might similarly affect the hippocampal architecture in HR youth for BD given that it is not yet unknown whether antidepressant-related hippocampal plasticity in humans is adaptive (toward improved symptoms) or linked to a risk of psychiatric adverse events. Direct evidence linking these neurobiological mechanisms to treatment outcomes and psychiatric adverse events in this vulnerable group is now needed. Youth at risk for BD are uniquely suited to examine adaptive versus maladaptive effects of antidepressants, as they may be at high risk for treatment-emergent mania compared to the general population [ 2 ]. ### Cancer risk among individuals with mental illness - a systematic review and meta-analysis of nordic register-based prospective studies
**כתב עת:** 📄 Transl Psychiatry | **סוג מחקר:** מטה-אנליזה | **מחברים:** Xiao X et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Aug 1

Although cancer patients have a high burden of mental illness, it is less clear whether patients with mental illness experience a higher-than-expected cancer risk. This systematic review investigated the associations between a wide range of types of mental illness and subsequent cancer risk. Five electronic databases were searched for articles published from database initiation to October 24, 2024. Studies using nationwide Nordic register data to conduct a prospective analysis of the association between mental illness diagnosis and cancer incidence were included. When data from three or more countries were available for a given type of mental illness, random effects meta-analyses were performed; otherwise, narrative syntheses were conducted. A total of 7,767 non-duplicate articles were screened, of which 20 were included. Meta-analyses showed no association between schizophrenia diagnosis and overall cancer risk; however, a high level of heterogeneity was detected between studies. Individuals with schizophrenia appeared to have a higher risk of breast cancer (pooled standardized incidence ratio [SIR]: 1.28, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.08-1.52), and lower risks of rectal cancer (pooled SIR: 0.75, 95% CI: 0.59-0.95) and prostate cancer (pooled SIR: 0.53, 95% CI: 0.48-0.59), compared to individuals in the general population. A higher risk of overall cancer was observed for mood, anxiety, and stress-related disorders, as well as autism and intellectual disability, in multiple studies; however, the results differed by cancer types and sex. In summary, these findings suggest an altered risk of cancer among individuals with mental illness living in Nordic countries. Further research is needed to enable the development of targeted cancer prevention strategies to reduce mental health disparities in cancer protection.

Introduction Cancer and mental illness constitute two major contributors to the global burden of disease. In 2020, there were 19.3 million new cancer cases and ten million cases of cancer-related death worldwide [ 1 ]. Despite advances in cancer diagnosis and treatment, and the resulting continual improvements in cancer survival, over the past decades, the health burden due to cancer is still substantial. The average years of life lost due to cancer were 15.2 in 2020 and the global economic cost for cancer is estimated at as high as $25.2 trillion from 2020 to 2050 [ 2 ]. Mental illness, including psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, mood disorders, anxiety, and stress-related disorders) and neurodevelopmental conditions (e.g., autism, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder [ADHD], and intellectual disability), is also one of the most common causes of morbidity today. In 2019, approximately 970 million people worldwide were reported to experience mental illness, and the global number of disability-adjusted life-years due to mental illness increased from 80.8 million in 1990 to 125.3 million in 2019 [ 3 ]. In total, mental illness was estimated to cost the world economy approximately $2.5 trillion per year in poor health and reduced productivity in 2010, a cost estimated to increase to approximately $6 trillion per year by 2030 [ 4 ]. Given their high prevalence, comorbidity of cancer and mental illness is inevitable. It is well established that cancer patients have increased risks for mental illness. We and others have, for instance, demonstrated an increased risk of mental illness during the entire survivorship of cancer patients, especially immediately following cancer diagnosis [ 5 , 6 ]. Although it is difficult to discern the exact reasons for this risk increment, severe psychological distress experienced during cancer diagnosis and treatment, in addition to cancer biology itself and side effects of cancer treatment, might play a role. Importantly, we have also demonstrated that comorbid mental illness before or after cancer diagnosis increases the risk of overall and cancer-specific mortality [ 7 , 8 ], as well as other severe adverse outcomes (e.g., sepsis) after cancer diagnosis [ 9 ]. On the other hand, conflicting findings have been observed regarding the risk of cancer among individuals with mental illness [ 10 ]. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have investigated cancer incidence among individuals with schizophrenia [ 11 , 12 ], depression or anxiety [ 13 ], and anorexia nervosa [ 14 ], demonstrating high levels of heterogeneity between studies. Study results also vary by cancer type, sex, and the type of mental illness. Namely, the risk of overall cancer was shown to be slightly increased among individuals with anxiety or depression, slightly decreased among individuals with schizophrenia, and unaltered among individuals with anorexia nervosa. As a comprehensive understanding of cancer risk among individuals with mental illness is essential for effective cancer prevention strategies, we aimed to conduct an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of cancer risk among individuals with a broader range of mental illness types, including both psychiatric and neurodevelopmental conditions. Methods Search strategy and selection criteria We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of the risk of cancer after the diagnosis of mental illness, according to a protocol registered on PROSPERO prior to formal initiation of the review (identifier: CRD42024593919). Due to the extensive quantity of literature published, using varying quality of data, we subsequently restricted our review to studies using nationwide Nordic register data, to ensure minimal risk of bias. As such, studies were eligible for inclusion if they used Nordic registers to define both the exposure and outcome variables, using diagnostic codes, for the entire national population. One or more of the following types of mental illness must have been included as the exposure variable(s): schizophrenia, mood disorders (including bipolar disorder and depression), anxiety (including obsessive-compulsive disorder [OCD]) and stress-related disorders, eating disorders, and neurodevelopmental conditions (including autism, ADHD, and intellectual disability). Studies were eligible for inclusion if they used the risk of ‘any cancer’ as a combined outcome, and/or studied one or more of the following most prevalent cancer types in Europe: breast, colorectal, lung, prostate, urinary bladder, uterine, kidney, or pancreatic cancer [ 15 ]. Additionally, studies must have conducted prospective analysis of the association between the exposure (diagnosis of mental illness) and the outcome (cancer incidence) (i.e., cohort/nested case-control studies), quantified using one of the following measures of association: odds ratio (OR), hazard ratio (HR), relative risk (RR), risk ratio (RR), incidence rate ratio (IRR), standardized incidence ratio (SIR), or standardized morbidity ratio (SMR). Studies were excluded if they focused on paediatric or elderly populations, or cancer outcomes (e.g., survival or treatment) other than incidence. Studies using other designs (i.e., cross-sectional studies, case studies, case series, randomized controlled trials [RCTs], or qualitative studies), systematic reviews, conference abstracts, and studies not published in English or subsequently translated to English were also excluded. ### Expert guidance to inform coordinated suicide postvention responses: A Delphi study
**כתב עת:** 📄 BMC Public Health | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Reifels L et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Jul 29

Background The impact of suicide is profound for affected individuals, families and communities. Suicide postvention responses, inclusive of bereavement support, have been recognised as having made a valuable contribution to suicide prevention efforts. Postvention Protocol Response Groups (PPRGs) have emerged as a central mechanism to facilitate a coordinated service system response following a suspected suicide, mobilising a range of local services to provide early intervention support to bereaved communities across a defined geographical area. There is an important role to be played by these key agencies, support services, and community groups in responding to these impacts. However, little is known about the effective implementation of PPRGs. Methods We used the Delphi method to obtain expert consensus regarding best practice implementation of PPRGs. The two-round online Delphi survey involved a panel of 30 Australian experts (comprising past or present PPRG members, postvention experts, and response beneficiaries) who rated statements in terms of their importance for PPRG implementation. Results The 126 statements endorsed by 80% or more of panel members as “essential” or “important” regarding PPRG implementation covered key aspects of stakeholder engagement, governance, practice principles, operational aspects, data surveillance, preventative capacity, sustainability, and special considerations in rural areas. Expert recommendations will serve as the foundation for the development of a PPRG implementation guide. Conclusions This study provides valuable expert guidance and practice-based evidence to inform the establishment of PPRGs in areas of need, enhance the effectiveness and sustainability of existing PPRGs, and strengthen the implementation of localised postvention responses. Supplementary Information The online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28686-2 .

Background Suicide is a major public health problem with wide ranging human and societal impacts. Globally, more than 720,000 lives are lost to suicide every year, at a rate of approximately 9 suicides per 100,000 population [ 1 ], whereas Australia counted 3,264 suicides in 2023, representing a rate of almost 12 suicides per 100,000 population [ 2 ]. Each death by suicide has wide reaching impacts for affected individuals, families and social networks, workplaces and communities. Each suicide death has been estimated to affect between 6 family members and 135 community members [ 3 , 4 ], with potential to cause significant distress among those affected. This varies in intensity and duration related to their social, psychological, and geographical proximity to the deceased. People impacted by a suicide may themselves have an elevated risk of developing mental health concerns, as well as future suicidal distress [ 5 ]. People bereaved by suicide have approximately a three-fold risk of dying by suicide compared to those non-bereaved [ 6 ]. Mainstream and social media attention following a suicide can raise the risk of further contagion of suicidal behaviour [ 7 ] through mechanisms such as social influence, affiliation, imitation, or exposure to suicide related information. This, in turn, can drive the emergence of suicide clusters, involving a greater than expected number of suicides in a specific location or linked suicides that are spread across a geographic area over a short period [ 8 ]. Suicide clusters have been estimated to account for 5.6% of youth and 2.3% of adult suicides in Australia [ 9 ] and their impact can be overwhelming for local communities, stakeholders and services [ 10 ]. Taken together, these wide-ranging suicide impacts highlight the importance of a well-coordinated postvention response to identify and provide support to those bereaved, reduce community distress, avoid duplication of response efforts, and prevent further suicide [ 8 , 11 ]. Suicide postvention has been recognised as an important component of national suicide prevention strategies [ 12 ]. However, little is known about the effective implementation of coordinated community responses [ 13 ], underscoring the need for evidence-based guidance to address varied support needs across settings. While postvention guidelines have been developed for specific settings such as schools and universities [ 14 ], there is little guidance to coordinate effective multi-sectoral and multi-agency postvention responses across an entire geographical area. Postvention as an intervention has historically been about the provision of therapeutic and psychosocial bereavement support to individuals and families impacted by a suicide. Bereavement support is acknowledged as being preventative, with the understanding that individuals bereaved by suicide are themselves at disproportionate risk [ 11 ]. The original approach to suicide postvention was based on a crisis intervention model and mainly delivered post-hoc by individuals/organisations in response to concerns about the risk of contagion and clusters. More recently, however, Postvention Protocol Response Groups (or PPRGs as they are known in Victoria), have increasingly been developed across Australia. PPRGs facilitate a coordinated service system response following a suspected suicide, mobilising local services to provide early intervention support to bereaved communities, within an agreed framework that authorises and guides collective actions. PPRGs shifted away from the crisis intervention model to a public health approach, which integrates seamlessly with contemporary suicide prevention strategies. The earliest iteration of a PPRG was established in 2012 in response to a Victorian youth suicide cluster [ 15 ]. A subsequent coronial inquiry highlighted the critical role that local organisations play in both the identification of and response to suicide, commending the coordinated ensuing response, and recommending the development of a suicide prevention and postvention response framework [ 16 ]. These early service system responses have formed the foundation of PPRGs which have matured to be key enablers of a broader and more comprehensive postvention response to people impacted by suicide. While the height of PPRG activity was originally located in Victoria during the first decade, increasing national policy and funding momentum over recent years resulted in the establishment of PPRGs in other Australian states and territories. With several PPRGs now at varying stages of operation and a growing interest in PPRGs nationally, it is essential that key insights from their establishment and implementation be harnessed, and a solid evidence base be developed to enable and guide their future implementation. To this end, we conducted a Delphi expert consensus study with the aim of developing the evidence base for best practice establishment and implementation of PPRGs and to inform the development of an implementation guide.

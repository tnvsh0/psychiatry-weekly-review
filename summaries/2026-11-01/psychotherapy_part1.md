### Internet Intervention for Functional Seizures: A Randomized Clinical Trial
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Neurol *(IF: 120.7)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Higson L et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 26

This randomized clinical trial investigates if a self-guided internet-based intervention reduces seizure frequency compared with standard care in adults with functional seizures.

Key Points Question Does a self-guided internet-based intervention reduce seizure frequency compared with standard care in adults with functional seizures? Findings In this 3-arm randomized clinical trial of 107 adults with functional seizures, a self-guided internet-based intervention produced significantly greater reductions in monthly seizure frequency than standard care at 6 weeks, 6 months, and 12 months after randomization, with sustained improvements in anxiety. Reductions in seizure frequency in the therapist-guided arm compared with standard care did not reach statistical significance; direct comparison between the 2 active arms was not statistically significant. Meaning Results suggest that a brief, self-guided internet-based intervention offers a scalable option for reducing seizure frequency in adults with functional seizures who cannot access specialist psychological care, with self-guided delivery showing comparable efficacy with therapist-guided delivery.

Importance Functional seizures (FS) are a common and disabling presentation in neurology clinics, but evidence-based treatments are difficult to access in Australia and in many regions globally. A brief, scalable, internet-delivered intervention could narrow this treatment gap. Objective To compare a self-guided and a therapist-guided internet-based intervention for FS, each delivered alongside standard care, with standard care alone. Design, Setting, and Participants This was a 3-arm, parallel, randomized clinical trial conducted between February 2022 and April 2024, with follow-up through to 12 months after randomization. The location was a specialist outpatient clinic in Melbourne, Australia. Included were adults (≥18 years) with a documented diagnosis of FS confirmed by video electroencephalogram monitoring and at least 4 seizures per month, excluding comorbid active epilepsy. Interventions Participants were randomized 1:1:1 to a self-guided internet-based intervention plus standard care, therapist-guided internet-based intervention plus standard care, or standard care alone. Re-PROGRAM is an internet-based intervention developed for FS, comprising 5 structured sessions integrating lifestyle factors, seizure management techniques, and elements of cognitive behavioral, acceptance and commitment, and dialectical behavior therapy. Main Outcomes and Measures The primary outcome was monthly seizure frequency at 6 weeks, 6 months, and 12 months after randomization, recorded by daily prospective seizure diary, with retrospective estimates used for incomplete records. Secondary outcomes included depression, anxiety, quality of life, psychosocial functioning, and somatic symptoms. Results Of 220 individuals screened for eligibility, 107 (mean [SD] age 36.0 [12.4] years; 89 female [83%]) were randomized. Of these participants, 96 contributed to the full analysis set (29 self-guided, 34 therapist-guided, 33 standard care). The self-guided arm produced significantly greater overall reductions in monthly seizure frequency than standard care (rate ratio [RR], 0.35; 95% CI, 0.18-0.69; Holm-Bonferroni [HB] method P = .006), with sustained between-group effects at 6 weeks (RR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.18-0.76; HB P = .02), 6 months (RR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.12-0.78; HB P = .04), and 12 months (RR, 0.35; 95% CI, 0.16-0.78; HB P = .03). The self-guided arm also showed greater improvements in anxiety than standard care at all 3 time points and in depression and mental quality of life at 1 or more time points. The therapist-guided arm showed greater improvement in anxiety at 6 months, but reductions in seizure frequency did not reach statistical significance. Direct comparison between the 2 active arms showed no significant difference in seizure reduction (RR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.33-1.36). Sensitivity analyses confirmed robustness of the primary findings. No adverse events occurred in any arm. Conclusions and Relevance Results of this randomized clinical trial show that in adults with FS, a self-guided internet-based intervention reduced seizure frequency and improved anxiety relative to standard care, offering a scalable option for patients unable to access specialist psychological care. Trial Registration Identifier: ACTRN12622000262707

### Fear conditioning research through the lens of meta-analyses: A systematic methodological review
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Behaviour Research and Therapy (Behav Res Ther) *(IF: 5.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Bruntsch M et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 16

Meta-analyses have become foundational tools for evidence synthesis and are receiving increasing attention across research disciplines, including fear conditioning - a central paradigm in affective, behavioral, and clinical neuroscience and psychology - where the number of meta-analyses has notably increased in recent years. However, substantial methodological heterogeneity among these meta-analyses complicates their interpretation and comparability. This review systematically summarizes existing meta-analyses in fear conditioning (n = 26), with particular emphasis on methodological choices and reporting standards. Our findings indicate substantial variability in reporting practices, particularly regarding review processes, applied methodological models, and the operationalization of certain variables, which may challenge reproducibility and interpretability. Most meta-analyses used aggregate data and random-effects models, while moderator analyses were frequently conducted but yielded few significant moderators. Outcome measures were frequently aggregated (e.g., skin conductance, startle responses, ratings) without a clear theoretical rationale. We discuss the implications of moderator analyses, the limited attention to temporal dynamics, and the aggregation of diverse outcome measures. We argue that the field would benefit from more precise reporting, theory-driven analytical decisions, and increased data sharing at both the primary and meta-analytic levels. Such improvements would facilitate individual participant data meta-analyses, which preserve within-study variability, enhance moderator analyses, and address challenges inherent to aggregate-level data synthesis. Establishing and expanding shared infrastructures represents a necessary step toward cumulative, reproducible, and clinically informative work in the future.

### Effectiveness of Hippotherapy in Balance Rehabilitation for Stroke Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials
**כתב עת:** 📄 Acta Neurol Taiwan | **סוג מחקר:** מטה-אנליזה | **מחברים:** Abouelmagd ME et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 1

BACKGROUND: Stroke frequently leads to balance and mobility impairments, significantly affecting patients' independence and quality of life. Hippotherapy, a therapy involving horse-based movements, has gained attention as a potential intervention for balance rehabilitation.

OBJECTIVES: This study evaluates the effectiveness of hippotherapy in improving balance and mobility in stroke patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS: A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following PRISMA guidelines through PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Physiotherapy Evidence Database for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) assessing hippotherapy's effects on balance in adult stroke patients (Prospero registration number: CRD42024552688). Effect sizes were pooled using RevMan software.

RESULTS: Ten RCTs met the inclusion criteria, with seven included in the meta-analysis (n = 389 participants). Hippotherapy significantly improved balance outcomes compared to conventional exercise programs, as measured by the Berg Balance Scale (BBS) (mean difference [MD] =1.72; 95% confidence interval [CI] [1.38, 2.07], P < 0.00001) and the timed up and go (TUG) test (MD = -2.97 s; 95% CI [-4.83, -1.11], P = 0.002). Exploratory subgroup analysis showed no statistically significant difference between mechanical and real-horse hippotherapy (P = 0.64).

CONCLUSIONS: Stroke patients may benefit from hippotherapy as an effective adjunct to conventional rehabilitation. Hippotherapy significantly improves balance and functional mobility, as demonstrated by improvements in the BBS and TUG test. However, evidence regarding quality of life and psychological outcomes remains limited and inconclusive, underscoring the need for further high-quality RCTs.

### The effect of virtual reality glasses used during chest tube removal following open-heart surgery on pain and hemodynamic parameters: a randomized controlled trial
**כתב עת:** 📄 BMC Cardiovasc Disord | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Yanik TÇ et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 8

Background Chest tube removal (CTR) following open-heart surgery is a painful procedure that may adversely affect patients’ hemodynamic parameters. Purpose This study aimed to determine the effect of virtual reality glasses (VRG) used during chest tube removal after open-heart surgery on pain levels and hemodynamic parameters. Methods This prospective, two-arm (1:1), randomized controlled trial included 82 patients undergoing open-heart surgery. Patients in the VRG group ( n = 41) were exposed to relaxing forest-walk-themed virtual reality content starting five minutes before CTR and continuing for five minutes after the procedure. The control group ( n = 41) received routine care without any additional intervention. Pain levels were assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and hemodynamic parameters—systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂)—were recorded before CTR, immediately after, and at 5, 15, and 30 min post-procedure. Results A significant group × time interaction was observed for pain ( p = 0.003). After adjustment for baseline pain and multiple comparisons, pain was significantly lower in the VRG group than in the control group immediately after CTR (adjusted mean difference [VRG − Control]: −1.26, 95% CI: −1.85 to − 0.68; adjusted p < 0.001), whereas no significant between-group differences were observed at 5, 15, or 30 min. A significant group × time interaction was also observed for SpO₂ ( p < 0.001), with significantly higher SpO₂ in the VRG group immediately after CTR (adjusted mean difference: 2.70, 95% CI: 1.88 to 3.53; adjusted p < 0.001). No significant group × time interactions were observed for SBP, DBP, HR, or RR. Conclusion VRG use during CTR was associated with lower pain immediately after the procedure and higher SpO₂ immediately after CTR. No significant effects were observed for SBP, DBP, HR, or RR. These findings suggest that VRG may be a useful non-pharmacological adjunct for short-term pain management during CTR following open-heart surgery. Further studies are needed to confirm its effects on oxygenation and other hemodynamic parameters. Trial registration ClinicalTrials.gov ( NCT05585853 ) first submitted on October 12, 2022, and first posted on October 19, 2022. Supplementary Information The online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06778-6 .

Introduction Open-heart surgery is a major surgical procedure widely used in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases. Because this procedure requires advanced techniques, such as extensive tissue dissection and cardiopulmonary bypass, the recovery process can be prolonged and challenging [ 1 , 2 ]. Postoperatively, pain associated with incision sites, as well as pain caused by invasive chest tubes (CTs) placed to prevent complications, represents a significant clinical concern. In particular, the pain experienced during the removal of chest tubes can lead to haemodynamic instability in patients [ 1 , 3 , 4 ]. The primary purpose of using a chest tube (CT) in patients following open-heart surgery is to ensure the drainage of blood, air, and fluid accumulated in the chest cavity [ 1 , 3 , 5 ]. CTs are typically removed when drainage is less than 100 mL within the previous 24 h, the lungs are fully expanded, and no air leak is present [ 2 , 3 ]. Although CTs help reduce the risk of complications and accelerate postoperative recovery, their adhesion to surrounding tissues after placement can cause acute and severe pain. Consequently, the routine removal of CTs is a painful procedure that may trigger significant stress responses in patients [ 2 , 3 , 6 , 7 ]. In individuals with high cardiovascular sensitivity, such as those who have undergone cardiac surgery, pain can initiate a neuroendocrine response that negatively affects haemodynamic parameters, including heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and respiratory rate (RR) [ 8 ]. Therefore, effective pain management prior to chest tube removal (CTR) following cardiac surgery is of critical importance for enhancing patient comfort and reducing the risk of complications [ 4 , 8 , 9 ]. During CTR, mediators such as bradykinin, kinins, and histamine are released as part of the inflammatory response, lowering the pain transmission threshold. This process can lead to peripheral sensitization and hyperalgesia, resulting in increased pain sensitivity beyond normal tolerance and a heightened perception of pain [ 7 , 10 , 11 ]. In addition to the direct effects of pain, sympathetic nervous system activity increases, leading to elevations in HR, SBP, DBP, and RR, while peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) may decrease [ 1 , 8 , 9 ]. Furthermore, patients’ fear of experiencing pain prior to the procedure can exacerbate pain levels through nociceptive responses mediated by the limbic system. Therefore, identifying effective strategies to manage pain associated with CTR during the postoperative period is essential for reducing cardiac workload and improving long-term outcomes [ 3 , 10 , 12 ]. Non-pharmacological methods are increasingly used for pain management during CTR following cardiac surgery [ 1 , 8 ], including virtual reality glasses (VRG) [ 1 , 2 , 9 , 13 ]. VRG provides an interactive audiovisual environment that may reduce pain perception through distraction [ 1 , 5 ]. Several individual studies have reported reductions in pain associated with VRG use [ 5 , 9 , 13 , 14 ]; however, the overall evidence remains inconsistent. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Micheluzzi et al. found no statistically significant effect of VR on pain compared with standard care [ 15 ]. Previous studies have reported lower pain levels during and after CTR with VRG use [ 1 , 2 ], while another study reported differences in SBP, DBP, HR, and RR following CTR [ 9 ]. Although previous studies have investigated the effects of VRG on pain during CTR following open-heart surgery [ 1 , 2 , 9 ], evidence regarding its effects on pain and physiological parameters assessed at multiple time points remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the effect of VRG used during CTR following open-heart surgery on pain and physiological parameters. We hypothesized that, compared with routine care, VRG use during CTR would reduce pain intensity and favorably affect physiological parameters, including systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and SpO₂. Method Aim and study design This prospective, parallel-group, two-arm (1:1) randomized controlled trial aimed to evaluate the effect of virtual reality glasses (VRG) applied during chest tube removal (CTR) following open-heart surgery on postoperative pain and hemodynamic parameters (SBP, DBP, HR, RR, and SpO₂). This randomized controlled trial was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines [ 16 ]. The study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT05585853 ) and completed without protocol modifications. The study protocol was prospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov ( NCT05585853 ) in 2022. The ethical approval process took longer than initially anticipated and was completed in July 2023. Following ethical approval, initiation of participant recruitment was delayed due to clinical scheduling considerations and the availability of eligible patients undergoing open-heart surgery. ### Music-Based Intervention in Postoperative Lung Transplant Care: A Feasibility Study
**כתב עת:** 📄 J Music Ther | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Tague DB et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Jul 24

Music-based interventions may support recovery in medical and surgical settings; however, their implementation during the immediate postoperative period following lung transplantation remains understudied. This single-center randomized feasibility study examined whether a structured music-based intervention delivered by board-certified music therapists could be implemented at clinically meaningful postoperative recovery milestones and explored preliminary trends in pain, anxiety, medication use, sleep, and length of stay. Twenty-nine adult lung transplant recipients (experimental n = 17, control n = 12) were enrolled between September 2021 and September 2023. Participants in the intervention group received music-based intervention sessions during spontaneous awakening trials, 24-48 hr following extubation, and within 72 hr of transfer from the intensive care unit to a step-down unit. Intervention components included patient-preferred live music, music-assisted relaxation, and optional receptive or active music engagement tailored to patient status. Intervention delivery was feasible across all three postoperative milestones, although challenges related to recruitment, retention, sleep measurement, and intervention standardization were identified. Exploratory analyses suggested lower observed pain scores and lower median acetaminophen use in the intervention group compared to standard care; however, no outcomes reached statistical significance. Anxiety outcomes did not differ between groups, and sleep measurement using consumer-grade wearable devices was not feasible. These findings support the feasibility of music-based interventions during postoperative lung transplant recovery and provide guidance for the design of future adequately powered trials using refined outcome measures, milestone-based intervention delivery, and improved implementation strategies.

### Predictors and clinical outcomes of pretreatment length in an outpatient Veterans Affairs dialectical behavior therapy program
**כתב עת:** 📄 Psychol Serv | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Duraney EJ et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 26

Objective: Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) pre-treatment is a standard component of DBT designed to prepare patients for treatment, identify treatment goals, and build commitment. Examination of DBT pre-treatment is of particular importance in naturalistic clinical settings, where clinical resources are limited, such as Veterans Affairs Medical Centers (VAMC), where DBT has become more widespread. Methods: Eligible veterans ( n = 89) were enrolled in an outpatient DBT program at a midwestern VAMC. Thirteen potential predictors (spanning demographic, healthcare utilization, and clinical factors) of DBT pre-treatment length, operationalized as pre-treatment duration and number of pre-treatment sessions, were examined. Partial least squares regression was used to identify latent components. The relationship between pre-treatment length and treatment outcomes was also examined. Results: A significant one-component solution explained 29.10% of the variance in pre-treatment duration. Borderline personality disorder symptoms, past suicide attempts, past inpatient days, and past psychiatric ER admissions were positively associated with pre-treatment duration. A significant one-component solution, consisting of the same predictors, with the exception of past suicide attempts, and the addition of race, sex, and psychiatric comorbidity, explained 18.10% of the variance in number of pre-treatment sessions. Neither pre-treatment duration nor number of pre-treatment sessions was related to treatment attendance, completion, or symptom severity. Conclusions: Indicators of clinical severity and complexity predict number of days and sessions spent in DBT pre-treatment. Non-White race, male sex, and psychiatric comorbidity predict greater number of DBT pre-treatment sessions. Deviation from the standard four session pre-treatment approach may be needed for some veterans.

Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) is a multicomponent treatment originally designed for individuals at high risk for suicide ( Linehan et al., 1991 ) as well as individuals with borderline personality disorder (BPD; Linehan, 1993 ). Since the introduction of DBT, it has been widely implemented across various treatment settings (e.g., DeCou et al., 2019 ; Kliem et al., 2010 ). Despite the widespread dissemination, portions of the treatment protocol remain understudied. Known collectively as “full model”, or “comprehensive” DBT, the multi-modal treatment consists of individual therapy, skills group, phone coaching, and therapist consultation team ( Linehan, 1993 ). Before beginning comprehensive treatment, patients enter a pre-treatment period designed to prepare patients for DBT and assess the appropriateness of the DBT modality. Despite pre-treatment constituting a formal and necessary component of DBT, little empirical research has been conducted examining the DBT pre-treatment period. The pre-treatment stage of DBT was first outlined in Linehan’s treatment manual ( Linehan, 1993 ) where she described “contracting strategies” for beginning DBT. There are five central goals of DBT pre-treatment: 1) identifying treatment goals; 2) determining if DBT is an appropriate treatment modality; 3) assessing suicidal behavior and nonsuicidal self-harm; 4) establishing and building commitment for treatment/reducing life-threatening behaviors; and 5) cultivating a strong therapeutic rapport ( Coyle et al., 2019 ). More succinctly, the aim of pre-treatment is to elicit the patient’s agreement to the specified treatment plan and treatment targets as well as optimize the patient’s commitment to this plan ( Swenson, 2016 ). Thus, pre-treatment builds the foundation for comprehensive treatment. Elucidating factors related to length of pre-treatment would provide clinicians with a framework for clinical decisions about if and when to extend pre-treatment. Additionally, understanding how length of pre-treatment impacts treatment outcomes would provide clinicians with important information regarding potential consequences of brief or prolonged pre-treatment. Furthermore, while pre-treatment is an essential and unique component of DBT, the term “pre-treatment” can be interpreted, from a resource management or administrative perspective, as indicating a patient is not yet receiving needed treatment. Critically, this perception can negatively impact access-to-care metrics which can cause downstream consequences for the degree to which the pre-treatment period is recognized and prioritized from an institutional standpoint. Thus, empirical research supporting the necessity of this component and elucidating empirically backed guidelines for the ideal duration of pre-treatment, informed by patient characteristics, is sorely needed. A specified portion of treatment focused on assessing life-threatening behaviors and building rapport is of critical importance given DBT targets individuals with complex presentations and high-risk behaviors (e.g., McCauley et al., 2018 ; DeCou et al., 2019 ). Additionally, pre-treatment provides clinicians with dedicated time to obtain and strengthen commitment utilizing commitment strategies which are a central component of DBT ( Linehan, 1993 ; Koerner, 2011 ). To our knowledge, there is no empirical research specifically examining the pre-treatment stage. However, broader DBT research can provide some insight into DBT’s effectiveness at achieving the chief goals of pre-treatment. For example, in a study comparing DBT to community treatment, DBT therapists reported achieving alignment in treatment goals earlier in treatment as well as greater treatment alliance ( Bedics et al., 2015 ). These findings suggest that the pre-treatment stage may provide a strong foundation for facilitating rapport early on in treatment—compared to other modalities without this specific treatment period. The recommended length of DBT pre-treatment is approximately 4 weekly sessions ( Swenson, 2016 ). However, it is currently unknown if there is an optimal dose of pre-treatment. While sufficient time in pre-treatment is likely advantageous, as it provides ample time to complete pre-treatment goals, it is possible that prolonged pre-treatment may have negative ramifications. For example, extending pre-treatment for individuals who are reluctant or avoidant to begin skills group may unintentionally reinforce avoidance by allowing the patient to continue in their preferred treatment modality (i.e., individual therapy only). Similarly, prolonged pre-treatment delays the start of comprehensive DBT treatment, which can be iatrogenic to patients who warrant the full model treatment, as well as tax provider resources. For naturalist treatment settings, particularly community-based settings, such as Veterans Health Administration, conserving provider resources needed to be able to meet the mental health treatment needs of a large population is vital. Given the high rates of suicidal behaviors among veteran populations (Veteran Affairs Report, 2024), DBT has become more widespread in these clinical settings. Currently, empirical evidence to inform pre-treatment clinical decision making within DBT programs in VA settings is lacking. To address this, the sample for the current study is drawn from a naturalistic DBT program in a Veteran Health Administration setting. Importantly, DBT has been implemented in numerous veteran samples (e.g., Goodman et al., 2016 ; Koons et al., 2001 ). Evidence suggests DBT reduces future mental health service utilization, including reducing the number and length of hospitalizations, among veterans ( Meyers et al., 2014 ). Thus, the tradeoff between longer pre-treatment, and thus longer overall treatment, may be worth the up-front time and resources—if effective DBT reduces future resource utilization.

### Roles or rules?
**כתב עת:** ⭐ Behavioral and Brain Sciences (Behav Brain Sci) *(IF: 20.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Johnson SGB | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 18

Jara-Ettinger and Dunham's institutional stance conceives of social behavior in terms of roles; economists instead define institutions in terms of rules. I argue that rules, rather than roles, are logically (and likely cognitively) primary because (i) rules are underdetermined by roles, (ii) many rules are more abstract than roles, and (iii) rules only work if they rarely need to be enforced.

🔗 [קישור למאמר ב-PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42755206/)

### Defining biological naturalism
**כתב עת:** ⭐ Behavioral and Brain Sciences (Behav Brain Sci) *(IF: 20.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Kleiner J | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 17

Biological naturalism, as explicated by Seth, is indispensable for a balanced and metaphysically neutral science of AI consciousness. However, most of the properties that Seth explores might not be unique to living systems. Therefore, I argue, a biological naturalist research programme in consciousness science requires an explicit definition of biological naturalism. I discuss how such a definition might be obtained.

### Social tinkering in designed environments
**כתב עת:** ⭐ Behavioral and Brain Sciences (Behav Brain Sci) *(IF: 20.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Burton JW et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 18

Human culture and cognition evolve in designed environments, like business organizations and online platforms, which are engineered to elicit some behaviors and suppress others. Emergent cultural complexity should thus be understood as a function of both bottom-up self-organization and properties of designed environments. Glossing over the role of top-down design risks misrepresenting the evolution of culture and cognition.

### What are institutions made of?
**כתב עת:** ⭐ Behavioral and Brain Sciences (Behav Brain Sci) *(IF: 20.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Jara-Ettinger J, Dunham Y | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 18

How do people understand a social world built from hundreds of interconnected social structures? The commentaries on our proposed answer, the institutional stance, clustered around three questions: (1) What is the conceptual structure within the stance and where does it come from? (2) How does the stance integrate with mentalizing, and to what extent are they separate systems? (3) What changes when we think of people not as observers of social life but as participants in it? The commentaries also highlight new aspects of social cognition we had not considered but which may fall within the stance's scope. Here we address these questions and challenges, clarify key aspects of our account, and offer friendly amendments and future empirical directions aimed at addressing important challenges raised in the comments.

### Adaptive regulation of confidence following metacognitive conflict: Evidence from behavior and computational modeling
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Xing J et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 14

It is well established that external feedback is effective not only for enhancing cognitive performance but also for regulating metacognitive monitoring. However, external feedback could be detrimental when it conflicts with one's internal feedback signal (i.e., the level of confidence we have in our decisions). Currently, however, it is unknown how discrepancies between internal and external feedback ("metacognitive conflict") influence cognitive and metacognitive performance. Here, we examined this question in a series of perceptual decision-making experiments in which participants reported both their level of confidence and received performance feedback. Metacognitive conflict occurs if participants have high confidence in their decision, but external feedback indicates that the decision was erroneous, or vice versa. Results revealed that metacognitive conflict triggers next-trial behavioral adjustments: Participants exhibit significantly lower confidence following metacognitive conflict. Furthermore, this behavioral adjustment was smaller in blocks with a high versus low proportion of metacognitive conflict trials, and confidence was generally reduced in blocks with a high proportion of metacognitive conflict. To better characterize the computational mechanisms underlying this effect, we deployed an evidence accumulation model with additional postdecision accumulation to quantify confidence. Model fits revealed that participants become more conservative in reporting confidence after experiencing metacognitive conflict, as evidenced by biased postdecision starting points. Jointly, our findings provide much-needed insight into the metacognitive consequences of metacognitive conflict. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

### School-Based Health Centers and Student Health Care Use, Need, and Health Outcomes
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Pediatrics (JAMA Pediatr) *(IF: 27.6)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Kranz AM et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 16

This cross-sectional study evaluates 10 years of survey data to determine the association of access to school-based health centers with health, unmet health care need, and health care receipt for high school students in Oregon.

Key Points Question Does having access to a school-based health center (SBHC) improve the physical and mental health outcomes of high school students? Findings This cross-sectional study using Oregon data found that SBHC access reduced suicidal thoughts, suicide attempts, and unmet emotional health care need among students in grade 11. No changes in physical health or unmet physical health care need were observed. Meaning SBHCs may help improve the mental health of high school students.

Importance School-based health centers (SBHCs) are health clinics located in schools that provide comprehensive primary medical care at low or no cost to students. SBHCs are well positioned to improve health outcomes and health care use by eliminating transportation and cost barriers and to reduce disparities by primarily serving communities with low household incomes and high health care needs. Objective To determine the association of SBHC access with health, unmet health care need, and health care receipt for high school students in Oregon. Design, Setting, and Participants This cross-sectional study used 10 years of student survey data during 2006 to 2023 linked to information on SBHC access in high schools in Oregon. Participants were students in 11th grade statewide who took the health survey. Using entropy-balanced staggered difference-in-differences regressions, we estimated the association of having an SBHC at a student’s school with their outcomes. Data analysis was performed from July to September 2026. Exposure School-level measure of whether an SBHC was present in a high school in Oregon. Main Outcomes and Measures Three measures of mental health and suicidality, 2 measures of physical health, 2 measures of unmet health care need, and 1 measure of medical care received. Results Among 87 871 students at 180 schools, 43 397 (49.7%) were female, 42 824 (49.0%) were male, and 1157 (1.3%) were another gender. A total of 14 933 students attended a school with an SBHC during the study period. SBHC access was associated with statistically significant improvements in student mental health, with decreases in suicidal thoughts (−2.7 percentage points [pp]; 95% CI, −4.5 to −0.7 pp) and suicide attempts (−1.4 pp; 95% CI, −2.3 to −0.5 pp) and decreases in reports of unmet emotional health care needs (−2.1 pp; 95% CI, −3.8 to −0.6 pp). No other statistically significant associations with SBHC access were observed. Heterogeneous treatment effects by gender, sexual orientation, race and ethnicity, and rurality were observed. Conclusions and Relevance This study found that SBHC access was associated with improved mental health among high school students. There are nearly 4000 SBHCs in the United States, and they are primarily in schools with a high share of students from low-income families. Expanding access to SBHCs may help reduce mental health disparities.

Introduction School-based health centers (SBHCs) are health centers located in schools that offer a range of physical, behavioral, and preventive health services at low or no cost. There are nearly 4000 SBHCs nationwide. SBHCs offer an opportunity to reduce disparities in health outcomes and access to care, as schools with SBHCs have a higher share of students from low-income households and Black and Hispanic students compared with schools without SBHCs. Much of the evidence about the impacts of SBHCs on health care use and health outcomes is based on studies examining correlations. Among more rigorous evaluations of SBHCs, there are mixed findings on the associations of receipt of health care and health outcomes, likely due to the variety of contexts and outcomes examined. Nationwide research suggests that SBHC openings reduce teen birth rates, but may not change high school dropout rates, and that the opening of an SBHC in a county increases the likelihood of an adolescent having a usual source of care, but does not change receipt of a mental health visit or improve overall health. Positive changes on students’ mental health following SBHC openings were observed in Tennessee and Oregon, with reductions in emergency department (ED) visits for mental health concerns and increased outpatient mental health visits among youth insured by Medicaid after the opening of an SBHC in a district in Tennessee and reductions in student-reported depression, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts among high school students after the expansion of mental health services in existing SBHCs in Oregon. However, a study of SBHCs offering mental health care in California found no changes in student-reported depression and suicidality. Nearly all of these studies looked at SBHC access at the school district or county level, which may understate the benefits that students attending schools with an on-site SBHC experience. The 2 studies that examined access to on-site SBHCs may have limited generalizability because one examined the expansion of services at existing SBHCs and the other examined a subtype of SBHCs that offer mental health care with or without primary care services. Our study extends previous work by examining SBHC access among students attending these schools and by examining a broad array of measures of physical and mental health. The objective of this study was to determine the changes in physical and mental health outcomes, unmet health care need, and receipt of care for high school students in Oregon after an SBHC opens. We used 10 years of survey data and a difference-in-differences study design to compare outcomes for students in grade 11 in schools with an SBHC to schools that did not have an SBHC during the study period. Methods This cross-sectional study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology ( STROBE ) reporting guidelines for cross-sectional studies and was determined exempt by RAND’s human participants protections committee with a waiver of informed consent. Data and Setting We use 10 years of survey data from the Oregon Healthy Teens survey (fielded annually from 2006-2009, biennially from 2011 to 2019) and the Student Health Survey (2023). Both are statewide surveys that provide information about the health and health behaviors of students in grade 11. These data were obtained from the Oregon Health Authority with school identifiers, allowing us to link on information about SBHCs. Information on Oregon’s SBHCs, including school location and open (and close) date, was obtained from the Oregon Health Authority SBHC program. Data analysis was performed from July to September 2026. Exposure Oregon certifies SBHCs and requires that they are open at least 3 days per week and at least 15 hours per week during the school year. SBHCs are required to have a primary care clinician, such as a physician, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant, working on-site at least 10 hours per week and at least 2 days per week. They are also required to have a qualified mental health professional, with at least a master’s degree in a mental health field, available on-site or through referral who can provide mental health care, substance use treatment, and other services supporting student emotional well-being. A student was considered exposed if they attended a school with a certified SBHC. Outcomes We examine 8 outcome questions that were asked in all survey waves. This includes 3 outcomes related to mental health and suicidality. Students were asked if during the past year they ever (1) felt so sad or hopeless almost every day for 2 weeks or more in a row that they stopped doing some usual activities and (2) seriously considered attempting suicide.

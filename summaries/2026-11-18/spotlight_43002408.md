### Satisfaction, Regret, and Harm After Hormone Therapy in Youth From a Multidisciplinary Gender Clinic
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Pediatrics (JAMA Pediatr) *(IF: 27.6)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Kuper L et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 9

Key Points Question What degrees of satisfaction, regret, and harm are reported by transgender and gender-diverse participants who initiated testosterone or estrogen therapy (ie, hormone therapy [HT]) as youth, and are these experiences associated with demographic or clinical characteristics? Findings In this cohort study of 372 participants who received care at a single multidisciplinary clinic, satisfaction with HT was high and regret and harm were rare. Decisional regret was slightly higher among youths not currently receiving HT and those identifying on the nonbinary spectrum, but it remained low in absolute terms. Meaning Results provide support for existing standards of care rather than broad restrictions on youths’ access to HT.

Importance Concerns about regret and harm have led to restrictions on transgender and gender-diverse youths’ access to testosterone or estrogen therapy (ie, hormone therapy [HT]), while empirical data on decisional regret among those initiating HT as youth remain limited. Objectives To examine satisfaction, regret, and perceived harm among individuals who initiated HT as youth and to identify demographic and clinical characteristics associated with regret or harm and current HT status (receiving vs not). Design, Setting, and Participants This cohort study evaluates self-report surveys collected from October 2023 to October 2025 among former patients who initiated HT as youth at a single multidisciplinary gender clinic in the US. Data analysis was conducted from December 2025 to August 2026. Exposure Initiation of estrogen or testosterone. Main Outcomes and Measures Decision Regret Scale (DRS) total score (range, 0-100; higher scores indicate greater regret); item-level satisfaction, regret, and perceived harm; and current HT status. Associations with demographic and clinical characteristics were examined using nonparametric, Welch t , Fisher exact, or Pearson χ 2 tests as appropriate, with parametric sensitivity analyses for the DRS total score. Results Of the 376 participants who initiated HT as youth (112 [29.8%] initiating estrogen and 264 [70.2%] initiating testosterone; current age, 14-28 years; mean [SD] age, 20.9 [2.9] years; time since HT initiation: range, 1.2 months to 10.7 years; mean [SD], 4.9 [2.5] years), 4 were missing DRS scores, all of whom were currently receiving HT. Among the 372 respondents with DRS scores, overall decisional regret was very low (mean [SD] DRS score, 4.3 [14.7]; median [IQR], 0 [0-0]); 360 participants (96.8%) endorsed that receiving HT was the right decision, 10 (2.7%) reported regret, and 7 (1.9%) reported perceived harm. DRS scores were higher among participants not currently receiving HT vs currently receiving HT and among those identifying on the nonbinary vs binary spectrum; parametric sensitivity analyses estimated mean differences of 15.95 points (95% CI, 8.50-23.39) and 6.07 points (95% CI, 2.15-9.99), respectively. Participants not currently receiving HT (65 participants [17.3%]) were more likely than those currently receiving HT (311 [82.7%]) to have received testosterone (odds ratio, 3.58; 95% CI, 1.65-7.78; P = .001) and identify on the nonbinary spectrum (odds ratio, 8.88; 95% CI, 4.76-16.58; P < .001), were older (mean difference, 1.04 years; 95% CI, 0.45-1.64 years; P = .001), and had longer time since initiating HT (mean difference, 1.00 year; 95% CI, 0.48-1.53 years; P < .001). Neither regret or harm nor current HT status was associated with age at HT initiation or prepubertal gender diversity. Conclusions and Relevance The findings of this cohort study complement the literature documenting mental health benefits of HT, providing further support for existing standards of care for transgender and gender-diverse youth.

This cohort study examines satisfaction, regret, and perceived harm among individuals who initiated hormone therapy as youth and identifies demographic and clinical characteristics associated with regret or harm and current hormone therapy status.

Introduction Current estimates suggest that approximately 3.3% of 13- to 17-year-olds in the US identify as transgender or gender diverse (TGD). However, only a small proportion receive hormone therapy (HT; ie, testosterone, estrogen), with insurance-based analyses indicating rates below 0.1% of the general youth population. More than a dozen short-term follow-up studies and several large cross-sectional and retrospective studies have consistently found improvements in mental health and quality of life among TGD youth receiving HT. Prior research from our clinic found significant reductions in body dissatisfaction, depression, and anxiety and improvement in psychosocial functioning in 2 cohorts of youth 1 year after initiating HT and/or puberty suppression. This previous research indirectly suggests that TGD youth may be satisfied with HT. However, concerns about potential regret and harm have intensified, leading to widespread efforts to prevent minors from accessing HT despite standards of care continuing to support its use. While more limited in scope, several initial studies have demonstrated very low levels of regret among youth, consistent with low regret levels documented among TGD adults. One of the first major longitudinal studies of youth receiving HT reported that no participants expressed regret regarding puberty suppression or HT an average of 6 years after starting treatment. Three other clinic-based studies of youth have documented regret rates between 0% and 2%. These findings are also consistent with a community-based sample of youth who socially transitioned in childhood and later accessed HT. While the rate of regret was slightly higher in this sample (5 of 119 respondents [4.2%]), nearly all respondents (115 of 119 [96.6%]) reported satisfaction with treatment. Preliminary research also suggests that rates of treatment discontinuation are low for those initiating HT as youth. van der Loos et al reported that only 16 of 720 participants (2.2%) who started puberty suppression as adolescents were no longer receiving HT at median (IQR) follow-up of 3.5 (1.5-6.6) years later for youth assigned female and 2.3 (1.2-4.8) years later for youth assigned male. In another clinic-based sample with median follow-up of 2.7 years, 57 of 1050 participants (5.4%) initiating HT as youth discontinued HT for at least 3 months, although 20 subsequently restarted and 210 were lost to follow-up. In a third sample using a US military insurance database, the 4-year continuation rate for those starting HT as minors was somewhat lower (74%), but it was still higher than for those who started HT as adults (64%). Most studies examining reasons for stopping treatment have been conducted with adults, and findings suggest that stopping treatment is influenced by a range of internal and external factors and thus is not synonymous with regret. Given recent increases in external barriers to accessing HT, rates of stopping treatment are likely increasing. The present study seeks to strengthen the research base regarding TGD youths’ experiences with HT, considering the substantial shifts in access to HT and concerns about the well-being of youth receiving this care. Building on our previous work, our first aim was to examine the degree of perceived satisfaction, regret, and harm among youth who initiated HT, including those who were currently receiving HT as well as those who were not. Our second aim was to examine whether demographic and clinical factors were associated with (1) youths’ reports of regret or harm and/or (2) current HT status (receiving vs not). Methods Participants and Procedure Participants in this cohort study included former patients who were seen by a US multidisciplinary program for TGD youth and received HT.

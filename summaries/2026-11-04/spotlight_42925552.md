### A systematic review of observational and report-based measures of caregiver sensitivity: A Latin American perspective
**כתב עת:** 📄 Infant Mental Health Journal (Infant Ment Health J) *(IF: 2.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** סקירה שיטתית | **מחברים:** Retiz O et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov

Abstract Sensitivity is a cornerstone of early childhood mental health; however, its measurement relies heavily on tools developed in English‐speaking, high‐resource contexts. This systematic review identified observational and report‐based instruments used to assess sensitivity in caregivers of children aged 0–6 years. It evaluated the methodological robustness and usability of these instruments within Latin American contexts. Following a systematic search in Web of Science, Scopus, PsycInfo, and Redalyc, 732 eligible studies were identified. Based on a frequency‐of‐use threshold, 16 instruments (14 observational and 2 report‐based) represented across 616 papers were selected for in‐depth evaluation. Measures were coded for theoretical alignment with Ainsworth's sensitivity definition, psychometric evidence, and usability—specifically training accessibility, administration complexity, and evidence of use with diverse Latin American populations (e.g., caregivers in rural settings or with lower educational levels). Results revealed that only a minority of measures adhered explicitly to Ainsworth's original definition. Most instruments prioritized warmth and positive affect, potentially leading to cross‐cultural underdetection in contexts where responsiveness is expressed through proximity and routine‐based care. While observational tools predominated, report‐based measures showed limited validation and minimal regional use. These findings highlight the urgent need for culturally grounded validation and the development of accessible training programs for the region.

1 INTRODUCTION According to Attachment Theory, attachment refers to the affective bond that develops between an infant and their primary caregiver(s) during the first years of life and becomes organized into relatively stable patterns of security or insecurity through repeated interactions (Ainsworth, 1967 ; Bowlby, 1969 ). Secure attachment reflects the child's expectation of caregiver availability and responsiveness in contexts of stress, supporting the use of the caregiver as a secure base for exploration (Ainsworth et al., 1978 ). Key Findings Sensitivity measures often treat warmth and positive affect as central, which can miss responsive caregiving expressed through proximity and routine‐based care in some Latin American and other non‐Western contexts. Many widely used observational tools require training and materials that are not available in Spanish, limiting feasibility and standardization in Latin American low‐resource settings. Report‐based sensitivity measures are brief and potentially scalable but currently lack evidence linking them to attachment outcomes and observational convergence. Statement of relevance to infant and early childhood mental health Accurate assessment of caregiver sensitivity is essential for identifying relational risk and tailoring early caregiving interventions central to infant and early childhood mental health. This review clarifies how sensitivity is operationalized across common instruments and highlights when measures may under‐detect responsiveness in contexts where caregiving is expressed through proximity and daily routines. By integrating psychometric indicators with usability and Spanish‐language training access, the study supports practitioners and researchers in selecting measures that better fit real‐world service and research settings. A substantial body of research has documented the long‐term implications of attachment security for socioemotional development and mental health. Secure attachment has been associated with greater emotional understanding and regulation, as well as more adaptive interpersonal functioning, whereas insecure attachment is linked to long‐term elevated risks of relational difficulties, externalizing behaviors, and mental health problems (Dagan et al., 2018 ; Deneault et al., 2023 ; Obeldobel et al., 2023 ). In this context, caregiver sensitivity has consistently been identified as a robust predictor of secure attachment (Madigan et al., 2024 ). Sensitivity is defined as the caregiver's capacity to perceive, interpret, and respond appropriately to the child's signals (Ainsworth et al., 1974 ). Through ongoing reciprocal exchanges, sensitive caregiving supports trust in caregiver availability and the development of secure internal working models, providing a relational foundation for socioemotional competence and mental health across development (Thompson, 2016 ). 1.1 Assessing sensitivity Given its foundational role in early socioemotional development, attachment research has increasingly emphasized the need for reliable and context‐sensitive measures of caregiving processes, particularly sensitivity (Halty & Berástegui, 2021 ). Observational measures are often treated as the reference approach for assessing sensitivity (Lotzin et al., 2015 ), as they allow direct evaluation of caregiver‐child interaction and capture sensitive behaviors that are often intuitive and automated rather than consciously planned (Mesman, 2010 ), making them less accessible through self‐report. Ainsworth's Maternal Sensitivity Scale (AMSS; Ainsworth et al., 1974 ) is the gold standard observational measure in the study of parental sensitivity, in that it operationalizes Ainsworth's original conceptualization of sensitivity as the caregiver's prompt, appropriate, and child‐attuned responding to signals (Bretherton, 2013 ). Building on this framework, numerous observational instruments have been developed, differing in their conceptualization and operationalization of sensitivity (Mesman & Emmen, 2013 ). While some measures adhere closely to Ainsworth's original definition by focusing on contingent and contextually appropriate responses to the child's signals, others incorporate additional behavioral indicators such as warmth, verbal engagement, or structured interactional tasks (Mesman & Emmen, 2013 ). As Mesman ( 2021 ) highlighted, these operational choices reflect implicit assumptions about how sensitivity is expressed and have important implications for the cultural flexibility and comparability of observational measures. Previous systematic reviews have synthesized observational measurements of parental sensitivity. Mesman and Emmen identified eight instruments most frequently used in the literature, all developed in English and primarily validated in North American or European contexts (Mesman & Emmen, 2013 ). A more recent review by Halty and Berástegui ( 2021 ) reported similar limitations in the linguistic and cultural scope of parental responsiveness measures, conceptualized as the behavioral manifestation of sensitivity. That review also underscored the need for more systematic evaluation of sensitivity instruments using objective, comparable criteria. To date, however, the field lacks an integrative assessment that jointly considers psychometric indicators and practical usability, including training accessibility and feasibility across diverse research settings. Importantly, methodological rigor and practical usability do not necessarily converge in the assessment of sensitivity. Instruments with theoretical and empirical support often require extensive training, lengthy administration procedures, and specialized resources (Fourment et al., 2022 ), limiting their feasibility in large‐scale studies and low‐resource settings. These constraints are particularly salient in contexts such as Latin America, where access to standardized attachment measures is often restricted by financial limitations, lack of institutional infrastructure, and the high cost of training for key observational instruments, which are frequently available only in English (Mesman et al., 2016a ).

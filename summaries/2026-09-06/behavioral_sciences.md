### Asking open-ended questions promotes personal goal attainment in integrative negotiations
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Di Stasi M et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Aug 31

Prior research suggests that asking more questions improves outcomes in cooperative conversations, and a vast wisdom literature espouses the power of asking questions during conflictual conversations, too: Negotiation experts suggest that questions invite counterparts to disclose information and, in doing so, put askers at an informational advantage. But is this advice necessary-and is it effective? We analyzed 61,057 speech turns from transcripts of 309 dyadic integrative negotiations and used natural language processing to estimate how often people ask questions, what types of questions they ask, and how well question-asking predicts personal goal attainment (Study 1;  n  = 618 MBA students). Open-ended questions were rare, occurring about once every 4 min (8% of turns). Yet, asking more open-ended questions was robustly associated with personal gains. Closed-ended questions and nonquestion statements were not reliably related to personal gains. We then tested causality in live chat negotiations: Participants who were instructed to ask open-ended questions achieved higher personal gains than those instructed to ask closed-ended questions or to only make statements (Study 2;  n  = 1,452 online participants), and those instructed to prepare open-ended questions before their interactions outperformed those instructed to prepare statements (Study 3;  n  = 372 online participants). In all studies, the benefits of open-ended questions were tied to increased information elicitation and greater value creation. Collectively, these findings offer empirical support for the widely accepted-but rarely tested-claim that negotiators (and conversationalists broadly) focus excessively on influencing (by making statements) at the expense of learning (by asking questions). ### The (anti-)conformity strategy space: How flexible is social learning when group membership and incentive alignment vary?
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** von Flüe L et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Aug 31

People often look to what most others are doing for guidance, yet the same majority can be informative in one setting and misleading in another. We examined how people interpret social frequency information when group membership and payoff alignment vary. In an incentivized experiment, demonstrators first learned through private feedback which option maximized their own payoff. Social learners received no outcome feedback and decided only after observing demonstrators' choices. This separation ensured that the social signal reflected demonstrators' private learning rather than mutual influence among simultaneously observing learners. We varied whether demonstrators were ingroup or outgroup members and whether their incentives were aligned or misaligned with the social learner's. The same majority could therefore point to the best option in one context and to the worst option in another. The design thus tested whether people interpreted the same signal differently across contexts. Performance was highest when demonstrators were aligned with the learner's incentives, especially when those demonstrators were also ingroup members, even though group membership was induced by minimal cues. Cognitive load reduced memory performance but did not measurably alter the use of social information. We repeated the design in Switzerland and Kenya to study how findings extend across distinct participant pools. Individual strategies were heterogeneous in both sites but more dispersed in Kenya. These findings show that people do not treat the same social frequency signal as interchangeable across contexts defined by group membership and incentive alignment, and they highlight the value of studying social learning across distinct participant pools. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

### Male mice are particularly vulnerable to cognitive impairment following mild traumatic brain injury
**כתב עת:** 📄 Behavioral Neuroscience (Behav Neurosci) *(IF: 3.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Neal JT et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 3

Traumatic brain injuries (TBIs) result from impact to or rapid displacement of the brain and can lead to various neurological deficits involving working memory, decision making, and anxiety. While large-scale effects of brain damage are well-described for more severe TBIs, less is known about the extent and duration of cognitive deficits at the mild level. Interval timing can provide a helpful window into cognition in mice and humans. Interval-timing behavior is impaired in a wide range of neuropsychiatric disease states, but less is known about whether it is impaired by mild TBI (mTBI). Furthermore, novel object recognition and the Barnes maze tests are valuable assays for evaluating spatial learning, working memory, and anxietylike behavior in mice. Here, we employed a weight-drop model of mTBI to investigate cognitive and behavioral changes resulting from mTBI treatment. mTBI mice were not consistently impaired in either interval timing or novel object recognition, but they demonstrated impaired spatial memory in the Barnes maze. Interestingly, between-sex comparisons revealed impairments in male mTBI mice in the interval-timing task, suggesting that male and female mice may be differently affected by mTBIs. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

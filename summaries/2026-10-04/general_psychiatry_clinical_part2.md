### Mental Health and Substance Use Disorder Expenditures in the US
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Psychiatry *(IF: 25.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Blanco C et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 30

Key Points Question What were US mental health and substance use disorder expenditures in 2021-2023, and how were they distributed across disorders, populations, and services? Findings In this cross-sectional study of the 2021-2023 Medical Expenditure Panel Survey, annualized mental health care expenditures totaled $174 billion, representing 7% of all medical expenditures. Mood and anxiety disorders accounted for the largest aggregate expenditures, while expenditures per treated individual were highest for substance use disorders, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder; ambulatory care accounted for most spending. Meaning Mental health expenditures vary widely across disorders, but they remain low relative to the societal burden of mental disorders.

Importance Understanding spending on mental health could help inform policymakers on decisions about where to direct mental health care resources and help evaluate whether spending aligns with public health priorities. Despite this, the magnitude and distribution of mental health expenditures in the US remain poorly characterized. Objective To estimate aggregate mental health expenditures by sociodemographic characteristics, type of insurance, mental health disorders, and type of service. Design, Setting, and Participants This was a cross-sectional analysis of the 2021-2023 Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS), an annual, nationally representative survey of the civilian noninstitutionalized US population. Analyses focused on individuals who made at least 1 outpatient mental health visit in 2021, 2022, or 2023. Data analysis was conducted from August 2025 to January 2026. Exposure Outpatient mental health visits. Main Outcomes and Measures Overall and per-100-person expenditures stratified by sociodemographic characteristics and diagnostic categories. Results The analysis included all survey participants (N = 67 532 person-year observations; mean [SD] age, 39.8 [0.25] years; 35 608 [50.7%] female) and identified those with reported outpatient mental health visits in 2021, 2022, and 2023 (n = 15 407; mean [SD] age, 45.0 [0.36] years; 9500 [60.0%] female). The estimated annualized aggregate total health care expenditures as measured in the MEPS in 2021-2023 were $2.5 trillion (95% CI, $2.3 trillion to $2.7 trillion), with $174 billion (95% CI, $152 billion to $196 billion) in mental health expenditures representing 7.0% (95% CI, 6.4%-7.7%) of all medical expenditures. This represented an annual mean of $523 (95% CI, $474-$571) per person overall and $2473 (95% CI, $2266-$2679) per person among those with any treated mental health disorder. Mood disorders and anxiety and fear-related disorders accounted for the highest aggregate expenditures ($74 billion [95% CI, $62 billion to $86 billion] and $69 billion [95% CI, $58 billion to $81 billion], respectively). Expenditures per treated individual were highest for substance use disorders ($9569 [95% CI, $6943-$12 195]), schizophrenia ($9370 [95% CI, $6685-$12 055]), and bipolar disorders ($7382 [95% CI, $6027-$8737]). Overall, expenditures on ambulatory services accounted for $107 billion (95% CI, $93 billion to $121 billion), while expenditures on medication accounted for $36 billion (95% CI, $31 billion to $40 billion) and those for home health care and inpatient treatment accounted for $20 billion (95% CI, $11 billion to $29 billion) and $12 billion (95% CI, $8 billion to $15 billion), respectively. Conclusions and Relevance This cross-sectional study found that expenditures on mental health disorders in the US vary widely at the per-person and population levels, but they are consistently low relative to the societal burden of mental disorders. Research that leads to more effective treatments, decreases provision of low-value care, and supports better matching of patients’ impairment with the appropriate level of care could help optimize treatment access while controlling expenditures.

This cross-sectional study evaluates US mental health and substance use disorder expenditures in 2021-2023 and how were they distributed across disorders, populations, and services.

### National Trends in Benzodiazepine Dispensing
**כתב עת:** ⭐ The American Journal of Psychiatry (Am J Psychiatry) *(IF: 18.1)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Cruz N et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 30

OBJECTIVE: Benzodiazepines (BZDs) are commonly prescribed medications in the United States. Up-to-date BZD dispensing trends can inform whether and where improvements in prescribing practices are needed. The objective of this study was to evaluate national changes in BZD dispensing from 2013 to 2024 by patient age and prescriber type.

METHODS: A repeated, cross-sectional analysis using the IQVIA Longitudinal Prescription dataset was conducted to examine trends in BZD prescription dispensing between 2013 and 2024 (a total of 1,100,233,787 prescription fills). BZD prescription fill counts, prevalence, and mean total dose dispensed per BZD prescription were reported annually, stratified by patient age and prescriber type.

RESULTS: From 2013 to 2024, the annual U.S. prevalence of BZDs dispensed declined by 26.7%, from 7.5% to 5.5%. Net decreases in prevalence occurred across age groups, with the largest relative reduction among young adults ages 18-29 (-44.8%). Primary care physicians dispensed the most BZD prescriptions, followed by advanced practice providers and psychiatrists/mental health specialists. There were sharp relative declines in the number of BZDs dispensed by pain medicine specialists (-62.5%), primary care physicians (-44.3%), and psychiatrists/mental health specialists (-38.3%), with an increase observed among advanced practice providers (+101.5%). The mean total dose dispensed per BZD prescription decreased across all patient ages and prescriber types, with large declines in doses prescribed by pain medicine specialists (-65.3%).

CONCLUSIONS: National declines in BZD prescription fills and total dose dispensed over the past decade may indicate greater caution or selectiveness in BZD dispensing, potentially related to safety considerations and drug labeling changes. Shifts in dispensing patterns among select patient and prescriber populations warrant ongoing monitoring.

### Shared Transcriptional Features of Layer 3 Pyramidal Neurons Projecting to the Monkey Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex
**כתב עת:** ⭐ The American Journal of Psychiatry (Am J Psychiatry) *(IF: 18.1)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Arion D et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 30

OBJECTIVE: Layer 3 (L3) pyramidal neurons projecting to the primate dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) convey essential information for core cognitive processes. Alterations in these neurons could contribute to cognitive deficits in schizophrenia. This study was designed to determine whether DLPFC-projecting L3 pyramidal neurons have a distinctive transcriptional profile.

METHODS: Laser microdissection was used to capture Nissl-stained and retrogradely labeled L3 pyramidal neurons that project to the DLPFC from the superior temporal cortex or posterior cingulate cortex of eight (four female) young adult (54-60 months of age) macaque monkeys. All samples were subjected to RNA sequencing. The results were compared to 1) data from L3 pyramidal neurons that project to the DLPFC from the posterior parietal cortex (lateral intraparietal cortex/area 7a [LIP/7a]) or contralateral DLPFC and 2) new single-nucleus RNA-sequencing data from multiple cortical regions of two female, young adult (77 and 96 months of age) monkeys and from similar publicly available human data.

RESULTS: Numerous genes (N=2,876) were differentially expressed between Nissl-stained L3 pyramidal neurons from the superior temporal cortex and posterior cingulate cortex. In contrast, only 527 genes were differentially expressed between DLPFC-projecting L3 pyramidal neurons from these regions. Transcriptional profiles were concordant between genes enriched in DLPFC-projecting L3 pyramidal neurons from the superior temporal cortex and posterior cingulate cortex and those enriched in DLPFC-projecting L3 pyramidal neurons from LIP/7a and contralateral DLPFC. The 91 genes enriched in samples from all four regions represent a transcriptional profile of DLPFC-projecting L3 pyramidal neurons. This profile was detected in specific clusters in both new monkey data and published human single-nucleus RNA-sequencing data from cortical regions that project to the DLPFC, but not from primary visual cortex (V1), which lacks these projections.

CONCLUSIONS: These findings indicate that DLPFC-projecting L3 pyramidal neurons can be identified by a shared transcriptional profile, regardless of cortical region. The transcriptional identification of these and other L3 pyramidal neurons with specific cortical projections will provide the means to determine which specific subtype or subtypes of pyramidal neurons contribute to cognitive impairments in schizophrenia.

### Irritability in the Mix: An Exploratory Study of the Role of Irritability in Characterising Mood Disorders
**כתב עת:** 📄 Bipolar Disord | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Bell E et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov

ABSTRACT Objectives To examine the relationship between irritability and mixed states in bipolar disorder to determine how subjective irritability and mixed symptoms of mania and depression manifest in patients with bipolar disorder over a period of 18 months. Methods This exploratory analysis examined longitudinal data from a randomised controlled trial of a psychological intervention for patients with bipolar disorder. Scores of subjective irritability and mood morbidity were analysed from data sampled over ten timepoints over the trial from 82 patients. Results Significant associations were found between the presence of irritability and mixed symptoms ( F (1, 684) = 20.468, p < 0.001). This relationship remained significant throughout the duration of the study and the administration of a psychological treatment which reduced both ratings of mood and irritability. Notably, when mixed symptoms are absent, irritability nevertheless remained for nearly half the time. Conclusions Irritability and mixed symptoms are phenomenologically linked. The persistence of this link, regardless of the existence of mixed symptoms, suggests that irritability permeates bipolar disorder and is core to its phenomenology. Future studies should interrogate the tethering of mixed symptoms and irritability in the context of bipolar disorder as this may lead to a deeper understanding of the core characteristics of the illness—both clinically and in terms of its neurobiology.

1 Introduction 1.1 Irritability as a Psychiatric Phenomenon Within psychiatry, irritability is a transdiagnostic phenomenon and a common clinical complaint among patients [ 1 , 2 , 3 ]. It is mentioned in over 30 different disorders within the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders—5th Edition Text Revision (DSM‐5‐TR) [ 4 ], serving as a feature and/or a diagnostic criterion. This ubiquity has led some to advocate for irritability disorders in adulthood to be considered a category of their own [ 5 ]. Indeed, irritability is so commonplace that its relationship to mental illnesses has been described as analogous to the relationship between fever and physical illnesses in general medicine [ 6 ]. This is because it is non‐specific, and its manifestation does not necessarily point to an underlying cause or mechanism. At the same time, its presence is often a strong signifier of illness and in practice it suggests that a person may require medical attention—hence it is an important indicator of illness. Although definitions of irritability vary, and there is no consensus [ 7 ], a recent working definition is that irritability is a mood characterised by a heightened sensitivity to stimuli coupled with a lowered threshold for responding with anger or aggression to typically less vexing stimuli [ 7 , 8 ]. It is often seen in the context of unmet needs (e.g., hunger, sleep), illness or somatic complaints (e.g., hormonal fluctuations) [ 9 , 10 ] and in phases of life in which the capacity to regulate one's emotions and moods is compromised (e.g., young childhood, dementia and Huntington's disease) [ 11 , 12 , 13 ]. Nevertheless, its nature within the context of psychiatric disorders remains largely unmapped. One hypothesis is that, rather than being a symptom of such a broad array of conditions with purportedly distinct aetiologies, it is an epiphenomenon that signals when there is a problem per se, but remains non‐specific to any underlying cause. In other words, irritability manifests when there is an underlying problem, but its manifestation is not specific to the underlying mechanisms that drive the problem, and irritability may instead be a secondary by‐product of this process. This possibility is worthy of further exploration, and just as fever is a useful clinical indicator, irritability too may serve as a broad signal for an underlying psychiatric condition. In this way, it may alert both patients and clinicians to an insidious problem before it crystallises and manifests acute symptoms. 1.2 Irritability in Mood Disorders Within our current major diagnostic taxonomies, the DSM‐5‐TR and the International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision (ICD‐11) symptoms of depression and mania have been separated and conceptualised as groups of symptoms from two diametrically opposed poles of mood [ 4 , 14 ]. However, irritability defies this separation in that it is listed within both sets of symptoms—as a symptom of mania in adults, and as a symptom of depression in children and adolescents. In addition, within DSM manic episode diagnostic criteria, if the prevailing mood during a manic episode is irritable (as opposed to euphoric), then there is a higher threshold required before a patient can be diagnosed with mania (four symptoms as opposed to the usual three) [ 4 ]. This distinct requirement and separation of irritability from other symptoms of mania perhaps indicates a tacit acknowledgment of irritability's lack of specificity. And whilst it is common enough in the context of bipolar disorder to be listed as a ‘symptom’ of mania, it is not specific enough to be counted as a mood that is ‘equal’ to euphoria. The higher threshold required of irritability is puzzling, as irritability is considered so central to manic symptomatology that it was listed as a key clinical feature (along with elevated mood and grandiosity, hyperactivity, pressured speech) in psychiatric textbooks published in the first half of the 20th century [ 15 ]. The prevalence of irritability among clinical presentations of mania has meant that its co‐occurrence with depressive symptoms is usually classified as a mixed presentation or episode. Indeed, in mixed depression, that is depressive episodes with so‐called ‘contra‐polar’ symptoms, irritability is considered one of the most prevalent (manifesting in approximately 30%–70% of presentations [ 16 , 17 ]) and is therefore classified as a key symptom of mixed states [ 16 , 17 , 18 ]. Thus, whilst irritability is in many ways non‐specific, it is at the same time characteristic of bipolar disorder presentations [ 19 ]. Therefore, the exact nature of irritability and its relationship to bipolar disorder as we currently understand it presents a quandary worthy of further exploration and characterisation. 1.3 The Status of ‘Mixity’ in Bipolar Disorder Whilst current diagnostic taxonomies remain largely wedded to the separation of manic and depressive symptoms, it is well‐established that clinically, many patients experience mixed symptoms (co‐occurring depressive and manic symptoms; also known as mixity [ 20 ]), with recent estimates ranging from 12%–28% of patients with a mood disorder experiencing mixed features [ 21 ]. The ability to classify mixed presentations as an ‘episode’ in and of themselves is a key point of distinction between DSM‐5‐TR and ICD‐11. In the latter, an episode of illness can be classified as a mixed episode if there are several ‘prominent’ symptoms from both depression and mania co‐occurring or alternating rapidly [ 14 ]. In DSM however, since the 5th edition in 2013, mood episodes can only be coded as depressed or manic, with contrapolar symptoms being coded as mixed ‘features’, thus minimising their importance [ 4 , 22 ]. Further, in DSM‐5, irritability cannot be counted as a manic/hypomanic symptom towards a mixed features specifier for a depressive episode. ### Holding collaboration together: a community response using a violence threat and suicide prevention protocol
**כתב עת:** 📄 Front Public Health | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Friesen S et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

Coordinated, multi-sectoral community responses to youth mental health represent a promising yet undertheorized approach to addressing the service fragmentation that characterizes collaborative work across education, health, policing, and community services. This article presents qualitative findings from a larger sequential mixed-methods study of Strathcona County’s Violence, Trauma, and Suicide Prevention (VTSP) Protocol. The VTSP Protocol is a coordinated community framework designed to address youth mental health, community safety, and wellbeing in Alberta, Canada. The larger study combined social network analysis with qualitative inquiry; this article reports on the qualitative strand, which comprised 12 semi-structured interviews with key stakeholders purposively sampled from across the VTSP network. Thematic analysis of interview transcripts identified four interwoven themes that together describe the relational, communicative, and capacity-building labours through which the protocol is sustained: leadership as relational infrastructure, shared processes and communication structures, cross-sectoral relationships and joint purpose, and capacity building and shared language. Across all four themes, collaboration emerged not as a stable institutional achievement but as a fragile accomplishment, strong yet precarious, requiring continual renewal rather than resting on structural stability. The findings are theoretically grounded in Ecological Systems Theory and Relational Coordination Theory. Implications for the design, sustainability, and transferability of coordinated community response models in other communities and school authorities are discussed.

1 Introduction On February 10, 2026, five students and an educator were killed at Tumbler Ridge Secondary School in northeastern British Columbia in one of the deadliest school shootings in Canadian history, second only to the École Polytechnique massacre of 1989 ( 1 , 2 ). The perpetrator, an 18-year-old who had dropped out of school 4 years earlier, had a documented history of mental health concerns and had been the subject of multiple prior police contacts related to mental health ( 2 , 3 ). In the days following the tragedy, British Columbia Premier David Eby publicly stated that he would be seeking information from the provincial health system about what interactions it may have had with the perpetrator, a question that laid bare one of the most consequential failures in Canadian community safety: the absence of coordinated, sustained communication among the organizations responsible for identifying and supporting young people at risk ( 4 ). Tumbler Ridge is not an isolated case. The 2016 La Loche school shooting in Saskatchewan, in which a 17-year-old with known cognitive and social difficulties killed four people, raised the same fundamental question: what might have been different if schools, health services, police, and community organizations had been working together, sharing information through common protocols, and taking coordinated action before a crisis became a catastrophe ( 1 ). These events, rare by international standards but devastating in their impact, are powerful reminders that the systems responsible for youth mental health, community safety, and violence prevention are often fragmented precisely when integration is most urgently needed. The urgency that these tragedies reveal is inseparable from a broader pattern of deteriorating youth mental health across Canada. Data from the 2023 Canadian Health Survey on Children and Youth indicate that the proportion of young people aged 16 to 21 rating their mental health as fair or poor more than doubled between 2019 and 2023, rising from 12 to 26% ( 5 ). Longitudinal surveillance data document sustained increases in diagnosed mood and anxiety disorders, suicidality, and mental health service utilization among youth aged 12 to 24 between 2011 and 2018 ( 6 ), and Canadian Institute for Health Information ( 7 ) data indicate that mental health disorders account for approximately 5% of all emergency department visits among children and youth aged 5 to 24. The burden is not evenly distributed. According to the 2022 Mental Health and Access to Care Survey, 56% of 2SLGBTQ + youth met diagnostic criteria for a mental health or substance use disorder, and Indigenous youth experience depression and suicide at rates 5 to 7 times higher than those of non-Indigenous peers ( 8 ). For these young people, early identification and coordinated support are not merely beneficial; they are potentially lifesaving. Despite the scale and urgency of this challenge, the systems responsible for addressing youth mental health remain deeply fragmented. Schools, health services, policing, and community organizations each carry distinct mandates, operate under separate legislation and governance structures, and frequently use incompatible professional languages and referral pathways. Surveys of educators consistently identify the lack of coordination between schools and community mental health services as a primary barrier to effective student support ( 9 , 10 ). Research from Sweden, examining cross-boundary collaboration in youth mental health systems, concluded that there is little scientific evidence on how these collaborations are applied or sustained, and that significant gaps persist between the needs of young people and the quality and accessibility of available services ( 11 ). In the Canadian context, these gaps are compounded by workforce shortages, uneven regional resourcing, and the absence of a national framework for coordinating cross-sector responses to youth mental health ( 10 ). The result is a system in which information about a young person in crisis rarely travels across the organizational boundaries that divide education from health, health from policing, and policing from community services. The consequences of that fragmentation are not abstract. What makes coordinated, multi-sector community responses both compelling and rare is precisely the organizational complexity they must overcome. Bringing together education, health, policing, and community services around a shared protocol, common language, and sustained relational infrastructure requires deliberate design, ongoing resourcing, and the cultivation of trust across boundaries that ordinarily separate these sectors. In practice, most communities do not have such systems in place. Where they do exist, they typically involve a small number of organizations operating under limited agreements. The Strathcona County Violence, Trauma, and Suicide Prevention (VTSP) Protocol is an exceptional case: a coordinated community framework in which 16 organizations from across education, health, policing, and community services have worked together, using the Violence Threat Risk Assessment (VTRA) and Threat Evaluation of Situations (TES) processes as shared methodology and language, to address youth mental health, community safety, and wellbeing. Operationally, the protocol is sustained through regular cross-agency meetings, shared processes and forms, designated community leads, and ongoing VTRA and TES training; when coordinated action is required, community leads help gather information, bring relevant partners together, and guide the collaborative process. The protocol was built, in part, in direct response to a traumatic community event, and it has received national recognition as a promising model for other communities and school authorities in Alberta and across Canada. Its existence represents an organizational and relational achievement that the vast majority of Canadian communities have not yet attained.

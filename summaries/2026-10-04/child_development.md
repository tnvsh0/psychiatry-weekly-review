### Quantifying the health and financial burden of adverse childhood experiences in Wales, England and English regions in 2023: a pooled analysis of 10 population surveys
**כתב עת:** ⭐ BMJ Open *(IF: 105.7)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Hughes K et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 28

Abstract Objectives To update estimates of the health and financial burden of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) in England and Wales and generate estimates for English regions. Design Population attributable fractions (PAFs) for ACEs were calculated for risks and causes of ill-health using data from 10 randomly stratified cross-sectional ACE studies. PAFs were applied to disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for England, Wales and English regions for 2023, with financial costs estimated using UK statistical life year values (societal willingness to pay). Setting Households in England and Wales. Participants 28 449 residents aged ≥18 years. Outcome measures PAFs for ACE exposure categories (1 ACE, 2–3 ACEs and ≥4 ACEs) for four health risks (smoking, high alcohol use, drug use and high body mass index (BMI)) and seven causes of ill health (violence, mental illness, cancer, type 2 diabetes, heart disease, respiratory disease and stroke). Annual estimated DALYs and financial costs attributable to ACEs. Results Relative risks for all outcomes increased with ACEs, with risk ratios for ≥4 ACEs ranging from 1.063 (95% CI 1.000 to 1.129, p=0.048) for high BMI to 9.147 (95% CI 7.062 to 11.847, p<0.001) for drug use. For health risks, PAFs for ACEs were highest for drug use (Wales 60.3% and England 56.7%), while ACE-attributable costs were highest for smoking (Wales £1.8 billion and England £22.7 billion). For causes of ill health, PAFs for ACEs were highest for violence (Wales 49.2% and England 46.2%), and costs were highest for mental illness (Wales £1.8 billion and England £37.8 billion). Across all outcomes (accounting for duplication by excluding DALYs for causes linked to the four health risks), total ACE-attributable costs were £7.8 billion for Wales and £115.6 billion for England; costs for English regions ranged from £6.3 billion (North East England) to £18.0 billion (South East England). Conclusions Findings highlight the substantial financial burden of ACEs across England and Wales and provide important information to inform regional activity. There is an urgent and economically justified need for action to prevent ACEs and support those affected by them to reduce the health and economic burden of poor-quality childhoods.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY This study uses additional and more recent adverse childhood experience (ACE) studies to update existing estimates of the health and financial burden of ACEs in England and Wales using an increased sample size. Additional analyses now provide estimates of the health and financial burden of ACEs across different English regions, allowing more local interpretation and application of findings. Financial costs were estimated using established statistical life-years approaches, which are based on societal willingness to pay for reductions in the risk of ill health and mortality and therefore include intangible costs. ACE data were retrospectively reported and therefore may be affected by recall bias, while general household surveys are also likely to exclude some of those who have experienced the greatest harm related to ACEs (eg, homelessness, incarceration or premature death). We used cross-sectional data and therefore cannot infer causality; thus, our estimates reflect the health and financial burden associated with, but not definitively wholly attributable to, ACEs. Introduction Exposure to chronic stress in childhood can disrupt children’s developing neurological and physiological systems and embed vulnerability to poorer health and well-being. 1 2 Accordingly, a large body of research has identified how adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), such as child maltreatment, witnessing domestic violence and household member substance misuse, increase the risks of poorer health across the life course. 3 4 ACEs show dose-responsive relationships with both behavioural risk factors for ill health, such as tobacco, alcohol and drug use, 5 6 and major causes of ill health, such as mental illness, cancer and respiratory and cardiovascular diseases. 7 – 9 Such effects mean the health and associated financial costs of ACEs are substantial. For example, annual costs attributable to ACEs for leading health risks and causes in North America and Europe have been estimated to be equivalent to around 3% of each geography’s gross domestic product (GDP). 10 Similar estimates across 28 European nations found costs equivalent to between 1% and 6% of national GDP. 11 In England and Wales, a study using data from the 2017 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study estimated the annual health costs attributable to ACEs at £42.8 billion (£40.6 billion in England and £2.2 billion in Wales). 12 The study combined data from five general population ACE studies in England and Wales and, in line with other estimates, used a human capital approach to value the health burden of ACEs, based on the cost of lost productivity due to people being removed from the labour force through morbidity or premature mortality. However, the health and social impacts of ACEs affect far more than economic productivity. Thus, an alternative method of valuing the burden of ACEs is through statistical life methods, which focus on the social value of—or society’s willingness to pay for—small reductions in the risk of mortality. 13 14 A recent study using this approach in the USA estimated the annual economic burden of adult health conditions attributable to ACEs at US$13.9 trillion, substantially higher than equivalent estimates using GDP per capita. 15 Understanding the financial burden of ACEs is imperative for informing early intervention and response systems and assessing the potential economic gains of preventing ACEs for future generations. Since 2017, major events such as the COVID-19 pandemic and the rising costs of living linked to global conflict have affected population health, with studies identifying ACEs as a vulnerability for greater harm. 16 – 19 Here, we update estimates of the costs of ACEs in England and Wales using a statistical life methods approach, data from five further ACE studies and data from the 2023 GBD. We also, for the first time, provide estimates of ACE-attributable costs for the nine administrative regions that make up England. ### Proteomic insights into childhood adversity: Exploring biological mechanisms and cognitive outcomes in the CARDIA study
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Brain Behav Immun Health *(IF: 14.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Rose DK et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), including abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction, are among the most consequential adverse social experiences of early life and carry elevated risk of chronic disease and cognitive impairment in adulthood through biological pathways that are poorly understood. We examined the mediation of ACE-related plasma proteins, including immune signaling markers, in the association between childhood adversity and cognition in midlife using data from the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) cohort. Cognitive performance across verbal memory, processing speed, executive function, and global cognition was assessed with validated neuropsychological batteries. Among 2003 participants (mean [SD] age: 55 [3.6] years; 56% female; 42% Black), adjusted linear regression analyses showed that 35 proteins were significantly associated with at least one ACE ( p < 0.001, a priori discovery threshold). Of these, two proteins were associated with cognition after adjusting for age, sex, race, and years of education, and correction for multiple comparisons (Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate [FDR], q < 0.05). Higher MCTS1, a regulator of translation re-initiation and cell cycle progression, was associated with worse processing speed (β = −0.088, q = 0.002), and higher AINX, a neuron-specific intermediate filament protein, was associated with worse executive function (β = −0.069, q = 0.025). Adjusted mediation analyses did not identify any significant pathways that may have explained the effects of ACEs on cognition. These findings suggest that ACEs are associated with alterations in specific circulating proteins, including MCTS1 and AINX, that are independently associated with cognitive performance in midlife. Although mediation analyses did not support these proteins as significant intermediaries association ACEs with cognition after correction for multiple comparisons, their convergent identification across independent analyses suggests they may be worth prioritizing as candidate biomarkers for future study.

Highlights • Childhood adversity associates with distinct plasma protein signatures in midlife. • MCTS1 and AINX are the most robust of 12 cognition-associated candidate proteins. • No proteomic marker mediated childhood adversity-cognition associations. • Proteomic signatures may identify adults at risk for adversity-related cognitive change.

1 Introduction Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), including abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction, are pervasive and fundamental disruptors of one's social environment and health across the lifespan ( Danese and McEwen, 2011 ; Nelson et al., 2020 ; Felitti et al., 1998 ). ACEs are associated with increased risk for many chronic medical conditions (e.g., cardiovascular disease, diabetes, psychiatric disorders, cognitive impairment), which highlights the capacity of early-life adversity to embed biologically and alter developmental and physiological trajectories well into adulthood ( Danese and McEwen, 2011 ; Daskalakis et al., 2015 ; Danese and Widom ; Danese et al., 2009 ). Childhood adversity does not appear to affect cognition uniformly. A systematic review found that specific ACE subtypes, including physical neglect and physical or sexual abuse, differentially influenced distinct cognitive domains such as executive function, attention, working memory, and verbal and visual memory, in non-clinical populations ( Rosa et al., 2023a ). This domain specificity suggests that different forms of adversity may act through distinct mechanisms. One hypothesized pathway through which ACEs exert lasting effects is via chronic dysregulation of immune processes ( Bourassa et al., 2021 ; Lin et al., 2015 ; Danese et al., 2007 ). Social psychoneuroimmunology situates this pathway within a broader bidirectional framework: adverse social experiences alter immune function, and disrupted immune signaling, in turn, affects the social-cognitive capacities (e.g., executive function, processing speed) that people rely on for social perception and everyday interpersonal functioning ( Muscatell, 2021 ). Childhood adversity is a particularly potent social stressor within this framework, given that early immune alterations may compound across development and manifest as impaired social cognition well into midlife ( Nusslock and Miller, 2015 ; Chen et al., 2021 ). Prior research, however, has typically relied on canonical inflammatory markers ( Danese et al., 2007 ; Baumeister et al., 2016 ). A meta-analysis found only a small overall association between childhood stress and inflammation, and no evidence that this association differs by adversity type or specific inflammatory marker ( Chiang et al., 2022 ). This constrains inference such that when only immune proteins are assayed, only immune pathways can be recovered. Thus, immune markers may lack the resolution to distinguish how different forms of adversity affect biology. This limits our understanding of the broader proteomic landscape through which ACEs may influence health, particularly cognitive function. Advances in high-throughput proteomics now allow comprehensive, multiplexed measurement of thousands of circulating proteins, including a substantial complement of immune and inflammatory markers alongside proteins spanning metabolic regulation, cellular stress response, and other interconnected systems ( Candia et al., 2022 ). These non-immune systems point toward other routes from early adversity to cognition: cellular stress response and proteostasis pathways govern neuronal management and recovery from damage over time, metabolic regulation directly influences the energetic and structural resources available to support brain aging, and proteins involved in translational control and cytoskeletal integrity maintain the synaptic and axonal architecture underlying memory and executive function ( Beste et al., 2019 ; Hetz, 2021 ; Na et al., 2025 ). This offers an unprecedented opportunity to investigate the biological correlates of ACEs and their downstream effects on cognition. Few studies have applied this approach to sociodemographically diverse, community-based cohorts, and the specific protein-level signatures associated with ACEs (particularly those linked to cognition) are largely unknown. Further, most large-scale population studies have not examined ACE–protein associations in midlife, which is a critical period for both dementia risk detection and intervention. The Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) cohort, which recently incorporated an extensive proteomic panel comprising >6600 plasma proteins measured by the SomaScan assay, offers a unique opportunity to address these gaps. Given the biological plausibility and clinical relevance of protein-level alterations as potential mechanistic links between ACEs and cognition, we sought to identify plasma proteins broadly associated with different ACEs reported by older and midlife adults and to examine their associations with cognition. Specifically, our study assessed: (1) proteome-wide associations between five ACEs (emotional neglect, emotional abuse, physical abuse, household substance use, and household structure and supervision) and plasma protein levels; (2) associations between these ACEs and cognition; (3) associations between these ACE-related proteins and cognitive performance; and (4) the mediating role of these ACE-related proteins in ACE–cognition associations. We hypothesized that higher ACE exposure would associate with multiple biological proteins beyond immune markers, and that these ACE-related proteins would mediate effects on cognitive performance. ### Perceived family instability, involvement in interparental conflict, and depression in adolescents
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Development and Psychopathology (Dev Psychopathol) *(IF: 5.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Warmuth KA et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 30

Family instability and involvement in interparental conflict have long been identified as risk factors for adolescent depression; however, adolescent  perceptions  may be an underexamined measure of family instability which could produce cascading effects toward depressive symptoms. Altered perceptions due to cognitive maturation and puberty may make adolescents particularly attuned to deviations in the stability of the family system. Moreover, their involvement in parents' conflicts may further amplify their vulnerability to these stressful environmental experiences and increase their likelihood of depression. Therefore, perceived family instability, involvement in interparental conflict, and depressive symptoms were examined in 296 adolescents (50.3% female, mostly White) across three annual waves starting at age 13 years. Using autoregressive latent trajectory modeling, between-person results showed that adolescents with higher initial levels of perceived family instability tended to show higher initial depression levels, and some adolescents' involvement in their parents' disputes increased faster than others over this three-year period. Within-person results indicated that adolescents experiencing higher-than-usual perceived family instability at age 14 exhibited significantly higher depressive symptoms at age 15, pointing to the unfolding emotional cost of instability for early-to-middle adolescents. Findings emphasize modeling enduring vulnerabilities and intra-individual fluctuations in family and emotional processes during adolescence.

### Transitions in preschool children's internalizing and externalizing symptoms before and during the COVID-19 pandemic: The role of family disruptions in Singapore
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Development and Psychopathology (Dev Psychopathol) *(IF: 5.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Wang X, Fu Z | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 30

The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted family routines, economic security, and caregiving contexts, with potential consequences for children's mental health. However, less is known about heterogeneity in preschool children's changes in internalizing and externalizing symptoms from before to during the pandemic and about how pandemic-related family disruptions predicted these transitions. This study examined symptom transitions among 2,601 preschool children from the Singapore Longitudinal Early Development Study, using pre-pandemic data from 2018-2019 and during-pandemic data from 2021 ( M  age = 4.92 years,  SD  = 1.18; 50.17% girls). Latent profile analysis identified three groups at both waves: low, mild, and severe symptoms. Random intercept latent transition analysis revealed four transition patterns: stable low symptoms (63.40%), worsening symptoms (22.30%), stable mild symptoms (6.23%), and improved symptoms (7.73%). Compared with the stable low symptoms group, older children and those from families negatively impacted by the pandemic in work and family function were more likely to experience stable mild or worsening symptoms. Ethnic differences were also observed, and pandemic-related financial stress was associated with both worsening and improved symptom patterns. These findings highlight heterogeneous symptom transitions among preschool children and underscore the importance of considering demographic characteristics and family-level disruptions when supporting children's mental health following large-scale crises.

### The long shadow of social withdrawal: The impact of social withdrawal in adolescence on adult developmental outcomes - The TRAILS study
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Development and Psychopathology (Dev Psychopathol) *(IF: 5.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Veenstra R et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 30

Social withdrawal in late childhood and adolescence is linked to long-term developmental challenges, yet its unique impact on adult adaptive functioning remains understudied. This TRAILS study ( N  = 1,798 in the population cohort,  M  ( SD ) age at T1: 11.1 (0.6), 49% boys, 86.5% ethnically Dutch,  M  ( SD ) age at T7: 28.9 (0.6);  N  = 405 in the high-risk cohort,  M  ( SD ) age at T1: 11.1 (0.5), 66% boys, 97.6% ethnically Dutch,  M  ( SD ) age at T7: 29.6 (0.7)) examined whether social withdrawal during the ages of 11 to 16 predicted the achievement of developmental milestones by age 29, including romantic competence, residential autonomy, friendship satisfaction, work competence, and financial competence. Abstract The perinatal period is marked by heightened vulnerability to maternal depression and anxiety symptoms, which have been associated with early child socio‐emotional development, including difficult temperament. This longitudinal study investigated whether prenatal maternal depression and anxiety symptoms predicted infant difficult temperament at 12 months through parallel mediation by postnatal maternal symptoms and perceived partner support. Given emerging evidence on sex differences in the associations between prenatal maternal distress and early socio‐emotional development, exploratory multi‐group analyses by child sex were also conducted. Pregnant individuals were recruited (October 2020–September 2022) for the Resilience and Perinatal Stress during the Pandemic (RESPPA) study in Quebec, Canada. Measures included the EPDS, GAD‐7, ICQ, and perceived partner support assessed via self‐reported online questionnaires. Path analyses ( N = 1422) were conducted using data from the third pregnancy trimester (T1), three months postpartum (T2), and 12 months postpartum (T3). Separate models were estimated for depression and anxiety symptoms. Greater prenatal depression symptoms predicted higher infant difficult temperament scores, with postnatal depression symptoms and perceived partner support fully mediating this association. Findings highlight postnatal maternal depression and anxiety symptoms and perceived partner support as potential screening and intervention targets to promote infant development.

This period is marked by numerous changes, including hormonal, physical, psychological, financial, and social transitions, which can contribute to the onset or recurrence of mental health challenges (Biaggi et al., 2016 ; Smith et al., 2011 ). Maternal mental health problems are considered to be of global public health concern (Howard & Khalifeh, 2020 ; World Health Organization, 2024 ). Depression and anxiety symptoms have been found to be the most frequent mental health difficulties reported by women during the perinatal period (Alipour et al., 2012 ; Mateus et al., 2022 ). A Canadian study found that one in four women reported symptoms consistent with postpartum depression or postpartum anxiety at five to thirteen months postpartum (Gheorghe et al., 2021 ). While the perinatal period is already a time of increased vulnerability, the COVID‐19 pandemic likely exacerbated mental health difficulties. In particular, studies reported higher rates of depression and anxiety symptoms among pregnant women following the onset of the pandemic (Abu Sabbah et al., 2022 ; Caffieri et al., 2024 ; Tomfohr‐Madsen et al., 2021 ). A review including 25 systematic reviews and meta‐analyses conducted during the COVID‐19 pandemic across different countries estimated the pooled prevalence of prenatal and postnatal depression symptoms at 29% and 26%, respectively (Caffieri et al., 2024 ). ### Argentinean validation of the Early Trauma Inventory-Self-Report (ETI-SR) in an adult sample
**כתב עת:** 📄 Psychol Trauma | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Elgier AM et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 1

OBJECTIVE: Adverse childhood experiences affect long-term mental and physical health. In Argentina, the lack of validated tools limits accurate assessment of early trauma and hinders prevention strategies. This study adapted and validated the Early Trauma Inventory-Self-Report Short Form for Argentine adults and provided an initial standardized estimate of early trauma prevalence.

METHOD: A total of 418 Argentine adults ( M  = 30.6,  SD  = 10.1; 74.4% female) completed the Early Trauma Inventory-Self-Report Short Form through web-based data collection. Confirmatory factor analyses were conducted using the weighted least squares estimator to evaluate competing models. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω coefficients. External validity was examined through correlations with age and educational level.

RESULTS: The bifactor model demonstrated the best fit to the data, supporting the presence of both a general trauma factor and specific trauma domains. Internal consistency indices were acceptable for most subscales, although lower values were observed for the general trauma dimension. Positive associations were found among trauma dimensions, and physical trauma showed significant relationships with age and educational level. High prevalence rates of early trauma were observed, with most participants reporting at least one adverse experience.

CONCLUSIONS: The Early Trauma Inventory-Self-Report shows adequate psychometric properties for use in Argentina, providing preliminary support for its validity in this context. These findings contribute to addressing an important limitation in health measurement and support the development of standardized assessments for early trauma in Argentina. Further research using more representative samples is needed to confirm these results. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

Traditionally, such training has been developed and delivered by clinical experts or academics with specialised training, excluding those with lived experience expertise. Limited research has explored the involvement of lived experience experts in professional learning. Methods This study evaluated the quality and impact of a trauma‐informed practice training package for community health, mental health and drug health professionals that was co‐designed and co‐facilitated by women with lived experience of child sexual abuse. In total, 97 survey responses and 10 qualitative interviews were included in the study. Descriptive and inferential statistics were generated to measure changes to knowledge and confidence as a result of the training and to examine which aspects of the training were most valuable. Thematic analysis of data was conducted to explore how lived experience co‐facilitation supported learning. Results There was an increase in both the proportion and mean scores of participants who reported feeling confident in understanding trauma informed practice immediately after the training. Paired sample t ‐tests indicate these increases were statistically significant ( p < 0.001). Hearing directly from a trainer with lived experience was the most valued aspect of the training and was given the highest rating of ‘excellent’ by the highest proportion of participants (85%) compared to all other aspects of the training. Five key qualitative themes were identified, demonstrating how: (1) the depth and richness of lived experience perspective supported learning, (2) humanised trauma‐informed practice beyond theory, (3) encouraged engagement through openness, (4) facilitated the translation of learning into practice, and (5) informed improvements to workplace practices. Conclusion Future trauma‐informed practice training for healthcare professionals can utilise lessons learned in this study to enact participatory co‐design approaches and leverage the strengths of lived experience expertise for professional learning. 1 Introduction 1.1 Health Impacts of Child Sexual Abuse Child sexual abuse (CSA) is a common and egregious human rights violation globally and within Australia where this study was conducted. CSA encompasses acts that involve or expose a child to sexual processes beyond their developmental understanding or contrary to accepted community standards and law [ 1 ]. Globally, approximately one in five girls and women and one in seven boys and men experience sexualized violence and abuse during childhood [ 2 ]. In Australia, a landmark prevalence study reported that one in three girls and one in seven boys are subjected to CSA [ 3 ]. Victim‐survivors are at substantially elevated risk of mental and physical health harms across the lifespan, including suicide and suicidal ideation, self‐harm, substance dependence and chronic physical conditions [ 3 ]. It is noteworthy however, that not all individuals subjected to sexualized abuse in childhood go on to develop persistent, long‐term symptoms. It is therefore important for health professionals to operationalise trauma‐informed approaches to understand the healthcare needs of those who have been subjected to CSA. In Australia, the Royal Commission into Institutionalised Responses to Child Sexual Abuse (2017) found that many victim‐survivors experienced the health system as re‐traumatising, particularly mental health services. They reported inconsistent access to trauma‐specific care and clinicians who lacked understanding of the long‐term impacts of childhood sexual abuse [ 4 ]. This is consistent with academic literature which draws attention to how systemic shortcomings often compound harm [ 5 ]. Despite localised efforts in implementing trauma‐informed practice (TIP) within health systems, overall health systems have been widely described as undermining the dignity of system users, with those who access health systems frequently reporting feeling marginalised in their encounters with health professionals [ 6 , 7 ]. High levels of disempowerment, stigmatisation and coercion within mental health settings have been widely documented [ 8 , 9 ]. Accordingly, the Royal Commission recommended that health services adopt trauma‐informed and victim‐survivor‐centred approaches across all levels of care (recommendation 9.2) and that the health workforce receive mandatory education and training on the impacts of CSA and TIP (recommendations 9.6, 9.7). 1.2 TIP in Healthcare TIP in healthcare recognises the pervasive impact of trauma on physical, psychological, and social wellbeing and how it can shape engagement with health services, integrating this understanding into all aspects of care delivery. Rather than focusing solely on symptoms or diagnoses, TIP reframes clinical encounters to prioritise safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, and empowerment [ 10 ] whilst attending to a person's culture, history and gender. As such, TIP shifts the focus from pathologizing behaviours (‘What is wrong with you?’) to understanding a person's life experiences (‘What happened to you?’). TIP implementation encompasses both organisational and clinical practices. Examples of organisational practices include engaging service users in planning and service design, TIP training and workforce development, and policies and strategies for preventing secondary traumatic stress in staff. Examples of clinical practices include screening and assessing for trauma, involving patients in the treatment process and providing or referring to trauma‐informed treatment services [ 11 ]. Evidence suggests that trauma‐informed approaches can improve service engagement, therapeutic relationships, consumer satisfaction, and staff wellbeing, particularly in mental health, drug health and community healthcare settings [ 12 , 13 ]. As a result, TIP is increasingly viewed as a foundational component of quality and ethical healthcare creating substantial demand for workforce upskilling [ 14 ]. Evaluations of workforce TIP training consistently demonstrate benefits, including improved trauma knowledge, increased confidence in responding to disclosures, and greater awareness of referral pathways [ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ].

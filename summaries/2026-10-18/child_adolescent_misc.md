### Functional Listening Difficulties in Children With ADHD: Associations With Language Abilities
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Brain Behav *(IF: 14.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מחקר תצפיתי | **מחברים:** Sariyer Temelli MN et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct

ABSTRACT Objective Listening difficulties are commonly reported in children with attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and are often interpreted as manifestations of inattention. However, it remains unclear whether functional listening difficulties are related to broader language abilities or whether they differ across ADHD subtypes. The present study examined the association between functional listening difficulties and language abilities in children with ADHD and explored whether these relationships vary between predominantly inattentive and combined subtypes. Methods This cross‐sectional observational study included 125 children diagnosed with ADHD, categorized into predominantly inattentive ( n = 32) and combined ( n = 93) ADHD subtypes. Functional listening was assessed using the Children's Home Inventory for Listening Difficulties (C.H.I.L.D.), with higher scores indicating better functional listening, and language abilities were assessed using the Turkish version of the Test of Early Language Development (TELD‐3:T). Between‐group differences were examined using Mann–Whitney U tests. The primary analysis was a hierarchical linear regression examining the adjusted association between total C.H.I.L.D. score and language performance, controlling for age, gender, and ADHD subtype. A C.H.I.L.D.‐by‐ADHD‐subtype interaction was examined in a secondary analysis. Results There was no statistical evidence of a difference between ADHD subtypes in C.H.I.L.D. or language scores (all p > 0.05). In the adjusted regression, higher total C.H.I.L.D. scores were associated with better language performance ( B = 6.66, β = 0.594, p < 0.001, 95% CI [4.996, 8.315]). Exploratory correlations between total C.H.I.L.D. and language scores were positive in the predominantly inattentive type ( r = 0.72), combined type ( r = 0.56), and total sample ( r = 0.61; all p < 0.001). The C.H.I.L.D.‐by‐ADHD‐subtype interaction was not significant ( B = 0.63, β = 0.031, p = 0.72, 95% CI [−2.919, 4.177]). Conclusions Better functional listening was associated with stronger language performance in children with ADHD. There was no statistical evidence that this association differed between ADHD subtypes. These findings highlight the relevance of considering both functional listening and language performance in the clinical assessment of children with ADHD.

1 Introduction Listening plays a central role in children's everyday communication and learning. Effective listening requires the ability to attend to, process, and interpret spoken information in dynamic real‐world environments. In contrast to laboratory‐based listening comprehension tasks that primarily evaluate linguistic understanding, functional listening refers to how effectively individuals manage auditory information in everyday contexts. Functional listening reflects the interaction of multiple processes, including auditory perception, attentional regulation, executive control, and language processing. In clinical settings, caregiver‐reported listening difficulties are frequently used to capture children's listening performance across real‐world environments such as classrooms, social interactions, and noisy situations. Importantly, functional listening should not be considered equivalent to language ability. While language assessments evaluate linguistic knowledge and processing capacity, functional listening reflects how effectively individuals apply these abilities in everyday auditory environments that place demands on attention and executive control. Attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent patterns of inattention and/or hyperactivity–impulsivity that interfere with functioning across settings (American Psychiatric Association [APA] 2022 ). Contemporary frameworks conceptualize ADHD as involving alterations in distributed neural systems responsible for executive control and attentional regulation (Faraone and Larsson 2019 ). Neurodevelopmental studies indicate atypical maturation within frontostriatal and frontoparietal networks in individuals with ADHD, including differences in the prefrontal cortex involved in working memory, inhibitory control, and top‐down attentional regulation (Cortese et al. 2012 ; Shaw et al. 2007 ). Consistent with these findings, meta‐analytic research has demonstrated significant impairments in working memory and inhibitory control among children with ADHD (Martinussen et al. 2005 ; Willcutt et al. 2005 ). These executive vulnerabilities may affect a range of cognitively demanding activities, including listening and spoken language processing. Listening comprehension requires the rapid integration of phonological, lexical, and syntactic information while maintaining spoken input in working memory (Baddeley 2003 ; Just and Carpenter 1992 ). Successful listening also depends on attentional control mechanisms that allow individuals to sustain focus on relevant speech while suppressing competing auditory stimuli. Because spoken language unfolds rapidly and cannot be revisited in the same way as written text, efficient coordination between attention, working memory, and linguistic processing is essential for effective comprehension. Difficulties in attentional regulation or executive control may, therefore, interfere with real‐time language processing and lead to challenges in everyday listening situations. These mechanisms are particularly relevant for children with ADHD. Emerging evidence indicates that children with ADHD demonstrate reduced spoken‐word recognition accuracy and slower processing efficiency in challenging listening conditions, such as environments with competing speech (Lemel et al. 2023 ). Neuroimaging studies further suggest atypical connectivity between auditory cortices and frontoparietal executive networks during listening tasks, indicating potential difficulties in coordinating speech perception with top‐down attentional control. Together, these findings suggest that listening difficulties in ADHD may reflect broader interactions between attentional regulation and language processing systems. Despite increasing recognition of these interactions, empirical research examining listening abilities in ADHD remains limited. Many studies have investigated language abilities, academic outcomes, or attentional symptoms separately, making it difficult to determine how listening difficulties relate to broader language functioning in this population. Furthermore, listening outcomes are often measured using tasks that primarily assess linguistic comprehension rather than functional listening performance in real‐world environments. This distinction is important because listening in everyday contexts requires children to manage auditory information while simultaneously coping with attentional demands, environmental distractions, and competing stimuli. Another unresolved issue concerns whether listening difficulties differ across ADHD subtypes. ADHD is typically classified into predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive‐impulsive, and combined subtypes based on symptom patterns (APA 2022 ). However, neurocognitive evidence suggests that executive control and attentional regulation systems are affected across subtypes (Cortese et al. 2012 ; Faraone and Larsson 2019 ). Consequently, functional listening difficulties may not necessarily differ between ADHD subtypes but may instead reflect shared cognitive mechanisms influencing language processing and attentional control. Understanding how functional listening relates to language abilities within ADHD populations may have important implications for clinical assessment and intervention. ### How the Brain Interprets Pain in Autistic People: Does Sex Matter?
**כתב עת:** 📄 Eur J Pain | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Granovsky Y et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct

ABSTRACT Background While psychophysical studies suggest heightened pain sensitivity in individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), the relationship between subjective pain experience and cortical neurophysiological responses and whether this relationship is sex‐dependent, remains poorly understood. Methods This cross‐sectional two‐group comparison study included 50 adults with Level 1 ASD and 52 age‐, sex‐ and IQ‐matched typically developing controls (TDC). Pain ratings and cortical contact heat‐evoked potentials (CHEPs) were recorded in response to 52°C phasic heat stimuli. ANOVA and regression models assessed the effects of group, sex and pain ratings on CHEP amplitudes. Results Compared to TDC, autistic participants reported higher pain scores ( p = 0.004) and had lower N 2 amplitudes ( p = 0.02). A significant Group × Sex interaction ( p < 0.001) was associated with the lowest N 2 amplitudes in autistic females ( p < 0.05). Regardless of group, in females, higher N 2 amplitudes were associated with higher pain scores ( p = 0.042; model p = 0.032), whereas this association was not significant in males ( p > 0.05). Within‐group correlation analyses in females showed a significant pain–N 2 amplitude relationship in TDC ( r = 0.734; p = 0.004) but not in autistic participants ( r = 0.371; p = 0.192). Across groups and sexes, higher N 2 amplitude values were associated with greater attention‐to‐detail autistic traits ( p = 0.009). Conclusions Our findings suggest a dissociation between subjective pain experience and cortical responses to nociceptive stimulation in autism, particularly among females, consistent with different salience‐related or attentional pain processing. These results underscore the importance of considering sex and autism when interpreting pain‐evoked cortical responses and challenge the assumption that pain‐evoked potentials provide universally objective measures of pain. Significance This study identifies sex‐related differences in the relationship between subjective pain ratings and cortical responses to nociceptive stimulation in autistic and typically developing individuals. Autistic females showed particularly attenuated N 2 amplitudes and a weaker correspondence between pain ratings and cortical responses, suggesting different salience‐related or attentional processing of nociceptive stimuli. These findings highlight the importance of considering sex and autistic traits when interpreting pain‐evoked cortical responses in autism.

### Electrodermal Activity as a Metric of Arousal in Autistic Children Prior to Dental Treatment
**כתב עת:** 📄 Sensors (Basel) | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Isralowitz EB et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Sep 29

Several prominent theories have asserted atypical arousal as a biological feature of autism. However, there is mixed evidence regarding atypical arousal patterns, particularly resting-state arousal, in autistic children, with research increasingly using psychophysiological measures, such as electrodermal activity (EDA), to help elucidate arousal levels in individuals who might not otherwise be able to identify such states. This study analyzed extant EDA data from 135 autistic children (6-12 years) collected during a baseline rest period in a dental environment to determine the relationships between tonic (mean skin conductance level; SCL) and phasic (frequency of non-specific skin conductance responses; NS-SCRs) arousal and participant characteristics, including sensory over-responsiveness, autism traits, expressive language, and general anxiety. EDA data were collected using pre-gelled electrodes placed on the distal phalanges of the participant's non-dominant hand using the BIOPAC MP150 system. Data preprocessing and epoch analyses were conducted using BIOPAC AcqKnowledge software. Conditional linear and, for the outcome variable anxiety, quadratic growth models were used to assess the temporal (within-participant across observations) relationship between EDA and behavioral variables. The results revealed a significant time-by-autism trait interaction for SCL, as well as linear and quadratic relationships between anxiety and NS-SCRs. No significant relationships were found between EDA arousal parameters and sensory over-responsiveness or expressive language abilities. A significant covariance relationship between SCL and NS-SCR frequency was also observed. Collectively, these results support previous characterizations of unique, within-autism-population arousal profiles related to autism traits, anxiety, and tonic-to-phasic covariance patterns.

### Phenotypic Complexity and Diagnostic Utility of Exome Sequencing in ASD: Real-World Insights from Colombia
**כתב עת:** 📄 Int J Mol Sci | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Rodríguez Gutiérrez DA et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 7

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a genetically heterogeneous condition; however, genomic data from Latin American populations remain limited. We investigated the molecular and phenotypic landscape of ASD in 916 Colombian patients who underwent whole-exome sequencing (WES) between 2022 and 2025. Clinical and genomic data were retrospectively analyzed, and phenotypes were standardized using Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms. Variants were classified per ACMG/ClinGen guidelines, and we assessed phenotypic burden, predictors of molecular diagnosis, copy number variants (CNVs), and functional convergence of candidate genes. A molecular diagnosis was identified in 112 patients (12.2%), including 82 with variants in ASD-associated genes or regions and 30 with findings related to other clinical features. We identified 16 novel pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants, along with 10 CNVs. Among trio-based patients, 70.9% of molecular diagnoses were de novo. Higher phenotypic burden was associated with molecular diagnosis, and global developmental delay was the strongest predictor after correction for multiple testing. Functional enrichment analysis of candidate genes showed convergence on pathways related to neuronal development, synaptic function, and ion transport. These findings highlight the genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity of ASD and support the value of integrating standardized genomic and phenotypic characterization in Colombian populations.

### Ophthalmologic manifestations in children with autism spectrum disorder
**כתב עת:** 📄 Arq Bras Oftalmol | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Pereira L et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

PURPOSE: To determine the frequency and clinical characteristics of ophthalmologic manifestations in children aged ≤16 years with autism spectrum disorder who were evaluated at a private academic healthcare network and a public tertiary hospital.

METHODS: This retrospective descriptive study was conducted through a review of the medical records of children aged <16 years with autism spectrum disorder who were examined between 2023 and 2024. Demographic characteristics, visual function testability, refractive errors, strabismus, and prescribed treatments were analyzed. Dynamic retinoscopy was used to objectively assess the binocular accommodative response. Statistical analyses included 95% confidence intervals (Wilson score method) and exploratory multivariate logistic regression.

RESULTS: A total of 144 children were included; 79.2% were male, and the mean age was 6.3±2.7 years. Refractive errors were identified in 81.9% of children, of whom 63.2% had clinically significant errors requiring optical correction. Strabismus was diagnosed in 32.6% of cases, with exotropia being the most prevalent type. Stereopsis was successfully assessed in 72.2% of children. Notably, dynamic retinoscopy demonstrated perfect agreement (κ=1.000, p<0.001) with the presence of clinically significant refractive errors. Multivariate regression showed that increasing age was strongly associated with successful visual acuity testing, whereas neurological comorbidities and greater autism spectrum disorder severity significantly increased the odds of non-testability. Among children who continued follow-up, 63.9% experienced significant improvement in visual acuity. Although optical correction was generally well tolerated, adherence to occlusion therapy was notably low (6.8%), and 42.4% of the cohort was lost to follow-up.

CONCLUSIONS: Ophthalmologic abnormalities, particularly refractive errors and strabismus, are highly prevalent among children with autism spectrum disorder. Given the limited reliability of subjective visual assessment in this population, objective measures, particularly dynamic retinoscopy and cycloplegic refraction, are essential for accurate diagnosis. The substantial loss to follow-up further highlights the need for adapted clinical strategies to ensure continuity of ophthalmologic care in this population.

Large data repositories provide a valuable opportunity to combine multiple independent studies, creating large samples that can address questions not achievable within a single study. However, independent studies often use different instruments to measure the same behavior or psychological construct, such as cognitive ability, social functioning, or psychopathology. Investigators often combine samples that include different instruments under the assumption that instruments assessing the same construct capture the same underlying phenomenon. Yet this assumption of instrument interchangeability may be wrong. Collapsing different instruments into a single variable could introduce bias. Here, we established a quantitative method to demonstrate compatibility among multiple instruments with moderation analyses. We illustrate this method in domains of cognitive ability, anxiety symptoms, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder symptoms as exemplars that might interact with outcomes of interest (autistic features instruments). A total of 1,352 participants were included from autistic and non-autistic samples across 14 independent studies. Our findings suggest that within this sample, some commonly used instruments within a domain can be acceptably combined, whereas others demonstrate discrepant relationships with outcomes of interest. More importantly, we offer a generalizable approach for scientists seeking to leverage existing datasets for secondary analyses. Introduction A primary challenge to mental health research is identifying the precise relationship between brain imaging data and clinical phenotypes. Most studies are limited by small samples (Button et al., 2013 ; Yarkoni, 2009 ). Complex brain and behavior relationships are likely small effects that require thousands of participants to identify reliable and meaningful results (Marek et al., 2022 ). One path to address the gap is completing coordinated, large-scale multi-site studies, such as the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study (Casey et al., 2018 ) or a very large single-site study like the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (Calkins et al., 2015 ; Satterthwaite et al., 2014 ; Satterthwaite et al., 2016 ) that collect cognitive and mental health phenotype data and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans at a scale that is not typically available for most studies. These approaches are effective for studying neurocognitive development but require a significant investment from a funder and study coordination by project leaders. An alternative and cost-effective approach to generating a large-scale dataset is aggregating data from multiple small-scale, single-site studies. Research in the field of autism has produced two well-known efforts of such data aggregation: The National Database for Autism Research (Hall et al., 2012 ) and the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (Di Martino et al., 2014 ). The biggest challenge in post-hoc data aggregation is harmonizing behavioral and cognitive data from multiple independent studies that were conducted without knowledge of the future aggregation. Data harmonization, as defined by Cheng et al., ( 2024 ), refers to “the practice of combining different datasets to maximize their comparability or compatibility” (pp. 1). While attention has been given to combining or harmonizing MRI data (Fortier et al., 2016 ; Fortin et al., 2017 , Shen et al., 2025 ), behavioral and cognitive phenotypic data are often assumed to be comparable across studies with little to no quantitative assessment demonstrating their comparability. A common strategy is to combine different instruments that are believed to assess the same construct. For example, investigators may choose to administer different measures to estimate intellectual ability within their studies, such as the Differential Abilities Scales, 2nd Edition, Wechsler Intelligence Scales for Children, 4th Edition (now 5th Edition), or Wechsler Abbreviated Scales of Intelligence, 2nd Edition (Elliott, 2007 ; Wechsler, 2003 , 2011 ). When merging data across studies with these various cognitive instruments, scientists may collapse the Full-Scale IQ scores, 2- and 4-subtest estimate Full-Scale IQ scores, and the General Conceptual Ability scores from these measures into a singular “Full-Scale IQ” variable. While this approach is common (Bertollo & Yerys, 2019 ; Cola et al., 2022 ; Granader et al., 2014 ; Pugliese et al., 2015 ; Ratto et al., 2018 ; Yerys et al., 2019 ), there is limited empirical data supporting it as an appropriate strategy other than psychometric testing showing correlations between cognitive assessments among children hovering around r = 0.8 (Sattler, 2018 ). One concern with this collapsing approach is that, even if the instruments are moderately correlated, they may relate differently to one’s outcome of interest. For example, if various intellectual ability instruments have demonstrably distinct relationships to measures of social function in autistic individuals, then employing this collapsing approach in control variables for brain–behavior analyses related to social function may distort the outcomes. If our goal with big data is to elucidate relationships between brain and behavior measures, then we would not want these relationships to be biased by our collapsing approach. This concern exists for collapsing both primary behavior outcome measures (e.g., social function measures), as well as commonly controlled variables that interact with outcome measures (i.e., cognitive ability). Prior studies addressed this harmonization issue through advanced statistical modeling with item-level data or meta-analytic tools (van den Heuvel et al., 2020 ; Penberthy et al., 2005 ). The item-level analyses are elegant in their use of item-response theory and factor analysis to derive harmonized latent traits of a construct (e.g., memory). However, item-level data are not always available in aggregated datasets such as the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (Di Martino et al., 2014 ). Unfortunately, many tools do not sample all 18 diagnostic symptoms, such as the Child Behavior Checklist (Achenbach et al., 2001 ). Thus, there is a critical need to develop analytic tools that harmonize phenotypic measures for which only aggregate data and scores are available, or the scales are not easily aligned at the level of individual items. ### Syndromic Autism in Helsmoortel-Van der Aa Syndrome: From Clinical Suspicion to Genetic Diagnosis
**כתב עת:** 📄 Dev Neurobiol | **סוג מחקר:** תיאור מקרה | **מחברים:** Kılıçaslan F et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct

Syndromic autism refers to autism spectrum disorder (ASD) occurring as part of an identifiable genetic syndrome. Helsmoortel-Van der Aa syndrome (HVDAS/ADNP syndrome) is a rare autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in the ADNP gene. We report a 10-year-old boy with ASD and intellectual disability who exhibited delayed motor and language development, characteristic facial dysmorphism, early tooth eruption, endocrine abnormalities, electroencephalography (EEG) abnormalities, and urogenital findings. Following unremarkable karyotyping and chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), whole-exome sequencing identified a heterozygous pathogenic de novo frameshift variant, c.1239_1240del, p.(Gln414Valfs*25) in ADNP. This case emphasizes the importance of considering a syndromic etiology in children with ASD accompanied by developmental delay, dysmorphic features, neurological findings, or multisystem involvement, and supports comprehensive genetic evaluation when first-tier testing is unrevealing.

### Gut Microbiota Composition in Helicobacter pylori-Positive Adolescents With Autism Spectrum Disorder: An Exploratory Compositional Analysis
**כתב עת:** 📄 APMIS | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Kakiuchi T et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct

Alterations in the gut microbiota have been implicated in autism spectrum disorder (ASD), but reported microbial profiles remain inconsistent, potentially because of heterogeneity in factors affecting gut microbial composition. Because Helicobacter pylori infection is associated with alterations in the gut microbiota, we investigated whether ASD was associated with gut microbial composition in a cohort of H. pylori-positive adolescents. This cross-sectional study included seven adolescents with ASD and 80 controls before H. pylori eradication therapy. Gut microbiota was analyzed using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, with alpha diversity, centered log-ratio-transformed genus-level abundance, and overall community composition based on Aitchison distance evaluated between groups. No significant differences were observed in alpha diversity or overall microbial community composition. At the genus level, 82 genera met the prevalence criterion for individual testing, and no association remained significant after false-discovery-rate correction. The effect estimate for Roseburia was not statistically significant in either unadjusted or adjusted analyses. These findings do not support marked differences in overall gut microbiota composition associated with ASD within this H. pylori-positive cohort; however, the small ASD sample and substantial age imbalance limit the precision and interpretation of the findings.

### Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder polygenic risk score and incidence of cognitive impairment in older adults: A prospective multicohort study
**כתב עת:** 📄 Alzheimers Dement | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Leffa DT et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct

Abstract INTRODUCTION Preliminary evidence suggests a link between attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and later‐life cognitive impairment. Whether genetic risk for ADHD, estimated using an ADHD polygenic risk score (ADHD‐PRS), is associated with incident cognitive impairment (mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's disease dementia) remains unknown. METHODS We analyzed 7761 cognitively unimpaired (CU) older adults with whole‐genome sequencing from eight cohorts participating in the Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project (ADSP). RESULTS During follow‐up, 1347 participants developed cognitive impairment. Ten‐year cumulative incidence was 33.8% in the highest ADHD‐PRS tertile versus 23.4% in the lowest. In Cox models, higher ADHD‐PRS remained associated with greater risk of cognitive impairment after adjustment for education, apolipoprotein E ( APOE) ε4 status, or vascular risk. PRSs for other major psychiatric disorders were not associated with cognitive impairment in the same analytic framework. In a subsample with biomarker data, the association was not modified by baseline amyloid‐β status. DISCUSSION These findings support ADHD‐PRS as a novel correlate of cognitive impairment and vulnerability in aging.

Highlights Genetic risk for ADHD was estimated using PRSs. Across eight cohorts comprising 7761 participants, higher ADHD‐PRS was associated with greater risk of incident cognitive impairment. This association remained after additional adjustment for education, APOE ε4 status, or vascular risk. PRSs for other major psychiatric disorders were not associated with cognitive impairment in the same analytic framework. Baseline Aβ status did not modify the association between ADHD‐PRS and cognitive impairment.

1 BACKGROUND Dementia affects more than 55 million people worldwide, yet the full landscape of factors contributing to late‐life cognitive decline remains incompletely understood. 1 Attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that affects approximately 5% of school‐aged children and can persist throughout the lifespan, with prevalence estimates of ∼2.5% in young adults and ∼1% among adults older than 60 years. 2 Emerging evidence suggests that ADHD is associated with a higher risk of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia, 1 and a large population‐based study reported elevated risk for AD among relatives of individuals with ADHD, suggesting a potential shared genetic risk. 11 However, studies examining ADHD and later‐life cognitive impairment remain susceptible to residual confounding, including socioeconomic factors, health behaviors, and comorbid psychiatric and medical conditions. 12 Furthermore, most studies rely solely on electronic health records to ascertain dementia and ADHD diagnosis, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 10 which may introduce bias due to differences in healthcare access, clinician recognition, and documentation practices. Crucially, existing cohorts of individuals diagnosed with ADHD in childhood have not yet reached an age where the risk of MCI or dementia can be adequately assessed, 13 limiting the feasibility of rigorous longitudinal evaluation. These limitations have prevented firm conclusions from being drawn about whether ADHD represents a genuine risk factor for cognitive decline. To complement this literature, genetic analyses have been proposed as an alternative approach. ADHD is highly heritable, 14 with common genetic variants contributing significantly to risk. 15 The ADHD polygenic risk score (ADHD‐PRS), which aggregates the effects of common variants identified in genome‐wide association studies (GWASs), has been associated with ADHD diagnosis and related traits. 16 Therefore, ADHD‐PRS may provide a useful tool to investigate the relationship between ADHD genetic risk and late‐life cognitive outcomes. Recent studies have begun to explore whether higher ADHD‐PRS is associated with increased risk for cognitive decline in older age. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 However, most are focused on cognitive test performance, 18 , 19 , 20 and limitations in sample size and follow‐up duration restricted their ability to examine progression to clinical MCI or dementia. To address these gaps, we aimed to evaluate whether genetic risk for ADHD, estimated with an ADHD‐PRS, is associated with the risk of progression from cognitively unimpaired (CU) status to MCI or AD dementia. We further performed sensitivity analyses to explore the role of potential confounders previously associated with both ADHD and dementia, including vascular risk factors, 1 , 2 assessed possible ADHD specificity by examining PRS for other psychiatric disorders, tested whether baseline amyloid beta (Aβ) status modifies this association, and evaluated the longitudinal relationship between ADHD‐PRS and cognitive performance. RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review : The authors reviewed the literature using traditional sources, including PubMed. A growing number of studies have examined ADHD in relation to later‐life cognitive impairment. Relevant works are appropriately cited. Interpretation : Our results indicate that ADHD‐PRS, an estimate of genetic risk for ADHD, is associated with vulnerability to later‐life cognitive impairment. The absence of association with other psychiatric PRSs in the same models suggests a degree of specificity to ADHD. Clinically, the results suggest that individuals with higher ADHD genetic risk and those with diagnosed ADHD represent subgroups that may benefit from earlier/more frequent cognitive monitoring and targeted risk‐factor mitigation.

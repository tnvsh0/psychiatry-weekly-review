### Caring Letters and Suicide Mortality Among US Veterans Who Contacted the Veterans Crisis Line: A Prespecified Secondary Analysis
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Psychiatry *(IF: 25.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Reger MA et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 14

Key Points Question Is the US Department of Veterans Affairs Veterans Crisis Line (VCL) caring letters (CL) intervention associated with reduced suicide mortality, and are there differences in patient outcomes by signatory? Findings In this prespecified secondary analysis of a randomized clinical trial of 227 502 veterans who contacted the VCL, 87 926 received CL from a clinician or a peer veteran signatory, and 139 576 did not. CL receipt was not associated with suicide mortality, and no differences in outcomes were identified by signatory. Meaning These findings suggest that the CL intervention may not reduce suicide mortality among veterans at high suicide risk who contact the VCL.

This secondary analysis of a randomized clinical trial examines the association between caring letters receipt and suicide mortality among veterans who contacted the US Department of Veterans Affairs Veterans Crisis Line and whether outcomes differ by clinician or peer veteran signatory.

Importance Caring letters (CL) is one of the only suicide prevention interventions to ever reduce suicide mortality in a randomized clinical trial, but the results have not been replicated. The US Department of Veterans Affairs Veterans Crisis Line (VCL) CL intervention was previously evaluated, but suicide mortality data were not available at that time. Objectives To examine the association of the VCL CL intervention with suicide mortality among US veterans, and to determine whether there are differences in outcomes by signatory. Design, Setting, and Participants This prespecified secondary analysis of a randomized clinical trial includes a randomized parallel trial of US veterans, combined with an observational comparison of no CL receipt with any CL receipt. Veterans Health Administration (VHA) patients (aged ≥18 years) who contacted the VCL between June 11, 2020, and June 10, 2021, were randomized to receive CL from either a VA clinician or a peer veteran signatory. Veterans who contacted the VCL between June 11, 2018, and June 10, 2021, and did not receive the intervention were included as an observational historical control group. Suicide mortality analyses were completed between March and September 2025. Intervention Veterans received 9 CL messages over a period of 1 year from either a clinician or peer veteran signatory. The signatory was randomly assigned. Main Outcomes and Measures The main outcome for these analyses was suicide mortality among veterans in the 12 months following the index VCL contact. Suicide mortality was compared using χ 2 tests and time-to-event models. Results This study included 227 502 VHA patients (mean [SD] age, 53.6 [16.9] years; 84.4% men) who contacted the VCL. A total of 87 926 veterans received the CL intervention; the remaining 139 576 comprised the historical control group. A total of 625 suicides (0.27%) occurred during the study period. The suicide mortality rate was similar between the CL and historical control groups (0.29% and 0.27%, respectively; P = .31). Within the CL group, outcomes did not differ between peer letter and clinician letter recipients (0.21% and 0.22%; P = .95). Conclusions and Relevance In this analysis, among VHA patients who contacted the VCL, CL receipt was not associated with suicide mortality, and no differences in outcomes were identified by signatory. Future research should explore the potential benefits of increased health care utilization, a finding reported in the parent trial. Trial Registration isrctn.org Identifier: ISRCTN27551361

### Effectiveness of rTMS vs. Pharmacotherapy in Late-Life Treatment-Resistant Depression: An Observational Study of Individual Participant Data From Two Randomized Clinical Trials
**כתב עת:** ⭐ The American Journal of Psychiatry (Am J Psychiatry) *(IF: 18.1)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Adler E et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 14

OBJECTIVE: The authors compared the effectiveness and tolerability of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) versus pharmacotherapy augmentation and switching strategies in older adults with late-life treatment-resistant depression (LL-TRD).

METHODS: This observational study used individual participant data from 758 participants age 60 or older with LL-TRD from two randomized controlled trials: an rTMS trial of once-daily bilateral treatment for 4-6 weeks, and a pharmacotherapy trial comparing aripiprazole augmentation, bupropion augmentation, and switch to bupropion. Restriction, propensity score weighting, and regression adjustment were used to account for differences in trial design and eligibility criteria. The primary outcome was the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) 10 weeks after treatment initiation.

RESULTS: After accounting for differences in participant characteristics, rTMS was associated with lower MADRS scores than aripiprazole augmentation (difference: 4.16 points, 95% CI=2.32, 6.00), bupropion augmentation (difference: 6.08 points, 95% CI=4.06, 8.09), and switch to bupropion (difference: 6.36 points, 95% CI=4.55, 8.17). rTMS was also associated with a higher response rate (defined as reduction ≥50% in MADRS score from baseline to week 10) than aripiprazole augmentation (adjusted odds ratio [AOR]=3.83, 95% CI=1.08, 13.56), bupropion augmentation (AOR=4.87, 95% CI=1.15, 20.68), and switch to bupropion (AOR=4.02, 95% CI=1.12, 14.47). Participants who received rTMS were significantly more likely to experience headache than those who received pharmacotherapy.

CONCLUSIONS: This analysis suggests that rTMS for LL-TRD may be superior to pharmacotherapy and supports the use of rTMS earlier in the treatment algorithms for LL-TRD.

### The effect of psychoeducation-based motivational interviewing on schizophrenia-related knowledge and hope among individuals with schizophrenia: A single-blind randomized controlled trial
**כתב עת:** 📄 Arch Psychiatr Nurs | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Taşkıner B, Aycanoğlu C | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct

BACKGROUND: A lack of schizophrenia-related knowledge and low levels of hope among individuals with schizophrenia can negatively affect treatment adherence, personal recovery, and long-term clinical outcomes. Although psychoeducation and motivational interviewing have demonstrated positive effects in these areas, randomized controlled trials evaluating their combined effects on schizophrenia-related knowledge and hope remain limited.

OBJECTIVE: This study aimed to examine the effects of psychoeducation-based motivational interviewing on schizophrenia-related knowledge and hope in individuals with schizophrenia.

METHODS: This single-blind randomized controlled trial included 44 individuals with schizophrenia receiving care at a Community Mental Health Center in Turkey. Participants were randomly assigned to the intervention group (n = 21) and the control group (n = 23). The intervention group received a psychoeducation-based motivational interviewing program consisting of one preparatory session and five main sessions, whereas the control group received routine care. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using the Demographic Information Form, Schizophrenia Knowledge Scale, and Schizophrenia Hope Scale at three time points: baseline, post-intervention, and one-month follow-up. Data were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA).

RESULTS: At baseline, no significant differences were observed between the groups in schizophrenia-related knowledge or hope (p > .05). Following the intervention, the experimental group demonstrated significant improvements in schizophrenia-related knowledge (F = 176.060, p < .001, η 2  = 0.898) and hope (F = 99.782, p < .001, η 2  = 0.833), and these improvements were maintained at the one-month follow-up.

CONCLUSION: Psychoeducation-based motivational interviewing is an effective psychiatric nursing intervention for improving schizophrenia-related knowledge and hope among individuals with schizophrenia. Integrating this person-centered, recovery-oriented approach into routine mental health services may enhance active participation in treatment and support personal recovery.

### Key Decision Points for Personalization and Customization in Self-Guided Digital Suicide Prevention: Qualitative Interview Study
**כתב עת:** 📄 JMIR Form Res | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Quan Z et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 14

Abstract Background Young adults in the United States experience high rates of suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STB) but low engagement with traditional care, highlighting the need for accessible and scalable digital interventions. Self-guided digital safety planning interventions (SPIs) can expand access to support but introduce important decisions about what content to deliver, when systems should intervene, and how users retain control over system actions. Personalization, defined as system-initiated adaptation, and customization, defined as user-initiated control, may offer complementary approaches to these decisions. However, how they should be coordinated to preserve autonomy and reduce burden during periods of distress remains unclear. Objective This study aims to identify key decision points in self-guided digital SPIs and characterize what, when, how, and by whom (system or user) safety planning functions should be controlled. We further examined how personalization and customization can jointly support autonomy, reduce burden, and promote safety. Methods Nine young adults (ages 18‐24) with lived experience of suicidal thoughts and behaviors were recruited from an asynchronous remote community comprising individuals who had engaged with the Mental Health America (MHA) website. Participants completed semistructured individual interviews via Zoom. Three coders analyzed interview transcripts using codebook thematic analysis. One coder open-coded all transcripts to develop a preliminary codebook. Two additional coders then applied the codebook to overlapping transcripts, after which all coders revised the codebook and resolved discrepancies by consensus before coding the remaining transcripts. Themes were interpreted using the 5W1H (Who, What, Where, When, Why, and How) framework as an organizing lens for identifying and structuring decision points. Results Participants identified 3 classes of decision points for self-guided digital SPIs: (1) system-level decisions about what content and actions are automatically provided; (2) user-level decisions about when and at what pace safety planning unfolds; and (3) shared decisions requiring coordination between users and the system. Participants favored system personalization of decision aids, coping suggestions, brainstorming prompts, example messages for contacting trusted others, affirmations, and relevant resources. In contrast, participants wanted control over the timing and pacing of safety planning and the representation and organization of their safety plans. Participants also emphasized that systems should obtain explicit permission before taking safety-relevant actions, such as contacting trusted others or connecting users with crisis counselors in response to increases in detected risk. Conclusions Personalization and customization can function as complementary mechanisms for allocating decision authority within self-guided digital SPIs. The findings support a model of permissioned automation in which systems reduce cognitive and emotional burden through adaptive support while users retain control over safety-relevant actions. This decision-point framework provides a foundation for designing and evaluating self-guided suicide prevention technologies that balance adaptation, autonomy, and safety.

Introduction A widening discrepancy between the elevated suicide risk among young adults and the low use of mental health services indicates a critical need for research and interventions. Suicide is one of the leading causes of death in the United States. In 2024, 14.3 million (5.5%) of adults (aged 18 or older) had serious thoughts of suicide within the past year, and 2.2 million attempted suicide [ 1 ]. Suicide prevention interventions are often delivered through formal mental health care settings; however, over 41% of adults who endorse a need for mental health treatment do not receive treatment [ 1 ]. These data suggest that traditional service delivery models are not sufficiently reaching this population. The safety planning intervention (SPI) is considered a best practice [ 2 , 3 ] in behavioral health settings when working with individuals who have elevated suicide-related thoughts or behaviors. The SPI was developed as a brief and collaborative clinician-delivered intervention [ 4 ] that aims to support patients in coping with suicidal thoughts and resisting suicidal behaviors by creating a highly personalized list of internal and external coping resources [ 5 ]. The SPI often comprises 7 steps: (1) identifying warning signs, (2) coping strategies, (3) people who can provide distraction, (4) trusted contacts to help in a crisis, (5) professional resources, (6) lethal means safety, and (7) reasons for living. It also includes follow-up contact from providers to support connections to care and support using one’s safety plan [ 4 ]. However, young adults face structural and attitudinal barriers, such as cost, stigma, and limited access to providers, that prevent them from engaging in formal mental health treatment [ 6 , 7 ]. Moreover, this clinician-delivered intervention may face demand barriers, such as young adults’ strong preferences for self-reliance and autonomy [ 8 , 9 ]. Digital SPIs offer a potential pathway to circumvent these barriers by offering low-burden, accessible, and scalable interventions via mobile platforms [ 10 , 11 ]. As digital versions of SPIs have expanded, it is important to distinguish between collaborative SPIs completed jointly with clinicians through telehealth platforms and self-guided SPIs completed independently with structured system directions and guidance [ 12 ]. Emerging randomized controlled trials comparing in-person and digital SPIs suggest that some self-guided and interactive digital SPIs are acceptable, feasible, and yield effective safety plans that rival clinician-delivered safety plans [ 12 , 13 ]. Digital SPIs have been developed across various platforms and populations, with many offering self-guided formats designed to help individuals manage suicidal crises [ 14 - 18 ]. These self-guided safety plans demonstrate acceptability and show promise for increasing the accessibility of SPIs. Within self-guided digital SPIs, there are decision points that must be considered as part of their design, specifically how users are expected to engage with safety planning tools and the level of decisional authority they can retain throughout the planning process. We organize these decision points using the 5W1H (Who, What, Where, When, Why, and How) framework, a structure used in human-centered design work to identify and organize decision points in complex behavioral interventions [ 19 ]. In this study, what , when , and how organize the primary decision-point domains, while who , where , and why function as contextual conditions that cut across those domains, grounding the needs-assessment logic that shapes how each decision point should be operationalized. The current study adopts a clear conceptual distinction between personalization and customization as 2 mechanisms through which control over functionality and decision support can be implemented in a self-guided digital SPI. Personalization typically involves the system automatically tailoring content to users based on user data, while customization involves users actively choosing and modifying the interface or content [ 20 , 21 ]. In this study, personalization refers to system-initiated adaptation, in which the intervention automatically tailors features of, or content within, a system, while customization refers to user-initiated control that defines when, what, and how users can actively make choices about their experience using a system, increasing user autonomy and engagement [ 20 , 22 ].

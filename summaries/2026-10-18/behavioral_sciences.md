### Artists Can Design Memory, and AI Can Predict It
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Psychological Science (Psychol Sci) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Davis TM et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 13

Can artists deliberately design artworks to be remembered-or forgotten? Across three experiments, we tested whether visual-memory outcomes can be shaped through artistic design and how they are predicted by artists, laypeople, and artificial intelligence. We launched a nationwide art contest in which artists submitted works intended to be memorable or forgettable. Using recognition tasks with adults based in the United States (119 from Amazon Mechanical Turk, 892 from Prolific, and 62 visitors from a Chicago gallery), we found that artists achieved their intended memory outcomes. However, their success was asymmetric: Memorability was more readily increased than reduced. A neural network, ResMem, consistently outperformed human observers and artists in predicting which artworks would be remembered. Data-driven analyses identified psychologically interpretable features that explained nearly half the variance in memory and aligned with ResMem's predictions, but not with human judgments. Together, findings suggest that visual memorability can be engineered by humans, though asymmetrically, and is better captured by computational models.

### Does Global Slowing Explain Age Effects in Inhibitory Control?
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Psychological Science (Psychol Sci) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Sellers J et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 14

A long-standing debate in the study of cognitive aging centers around the issue of whether aging results in a selective deficit in inhibitory control beyond general slowing of cognitive processing. Previous studies have obtained mixed findings, in part because the typical free-response-time paradigm lacks the specificity required to disentangle component processes underlying inhibitory control. Here, we apply a forced-response paradigm and computational model to test whether aging results in overall speed differences or a selective deficit in inhibitory control. Across two experiments, over 200 older adults and younger adults completed a forced-response arrow-flanker task. We found strong evidence that older adults demonstrate a global slowing of processing without any selective deficit in inhibitory control in this task. Furthermore, we demonstrate that global slowing is sufficient to explain the mixed findings in previous free-response-time studies. Overall, these results argue against a selective inhibitory-control deficit in older adults.

### Naturally disengaging control to reveal habits
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Oh S, Collins AGE | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct

Habits are an essential part of everyday decision-making, yet the mechanisms underlying human habit formation and expression are difficult to study in laboratory settings, where participants typically engage strong goal-directed (GD) control that can mask habitual tendencies. Recent approaches have successfully elicited habits in long experiments by limiting the amount of GD control participants can exert (e.g., through forced response times). We extend this approach by designing a short behavioral paradigm that aims to induce and reveal habits without extrinsically limiting GD control, instead incorporating task features that we hypothesized would encourage participants to spontaneously relax GD control: a hierarchical multi-step trial structure, opportunities for self-correction, and frequent switches between extensively and minimally practiced behaviors. Across seven experiments, we demonstrate that this approach induces habitual control in a relatively brief paradigm. Specifically, overtraining increases slips of action toward the extensively-practiced context and reduces slips toward the minimally-practiced one, indicating behavioral inflexibility at early response times, while slower responses remain dominated by GD control. Systematic manipulation of task features across experiments revealed that allowing multiple response attempts was particularly important for reliably observing this overtraining effect; a hierarchical trial structure may further facilitate habit expression, though this remains inconclusive. More broadly, our paradigm provides a practical tool for investigating how habits form and interact with goal-directed control.

Introduction A habit is a stable pattern of behavior that has become routine and effortless, even automatic and difficult to change, through repetition ( Du et al., 2022 ). Habits are highly consequential: a considerable portion of our day-to-day behaviors are habitual ( Wood et al., 2002 ), and when adaptive, habitual control can be effective and efficient, limiting the need for costly cognitive control and freeing up cognitive resources. Conversely, because they are highly automatized, habits can also be associated with behavioral inflexibility and maladaptive decisions, and are thought to play a role in multiple psychiatric disorders, such as obsessive-compulsive disorder ( Gillan et al., 2011 ), addiction ( Everitt & Robbins, 2016 ), and anorexia nervosa ( Uniacke et al., 2018 ). Habits become evident in scenarios where they conflict with one’s current goals, yet are carried out nonetheless due to repetition-induced automaticity. As such, habits are typically characterized in contrast to goal-directed (GD) behavior, which is guided by explicit representations of action–outcome contingencies and current goals ( Balleine & Dickinson, 1998 ; Dickinson, 1985 ; Dickinson & Balleine, 1995 ). Studies using outcome devaluation and related paradigms in rodents have shown that a behavior that, early in learning, is directed toward achieving a particular outcome gradually comes to be performed automatically, even when the environment changes such that the behavior no longer reliably results in the desired outcome ( Adams, 1982 ; Dickinson, 1985 ; Dickinson & Balleine, 1995 ; Killcross & Coutureau, 2003 ). These paradigms have been combined with lesion studies to demonstrate that habitual and GD processes rely on partly dissociable neural networks: GD learning can be disrupted while leaving habit formation intact by damaging certain brain areas such as the dorsomedial striatum, and vice versa by damaging other brain areas such as the dorsolateral striatum( Balleine & Dickinson, 1998 ; Killcross & Coutureau, 2003 ; Lingawi et al., 2016 ; Yin et al., 2004 , 2005 ). The success of these classic approaches in explicating different forms of behavioral control in non-human animals has inspired efforts to translate them to humans. These efforts have met with mixed success ( de Wit et al., 2018 ; Gera et al., 2023 ; Pool et al., 2022 ; Tricomi et al., 2009 ), suggesting that existing experimental designs may be inadequate for reliably inducing and/or revealing habits in humans. In general, tasks developed to study habits in humans often fail to exhibit what is widely considered a key marker of habits: sensitivity to overtraining. That is, increased inflexibility with extended practice is considered critical for establishing that inflexible behavior reflects gradually-formed habits rather than weak GD control ( de Wit et al., 2018 ; Gera et al., 2023 ; Pool et al., 2022 ; Watson et al., 2022 ). Furthermore, putative markers of habitual behavior on these tasks tend to correlate with individual measures related to GD control, rather than with other measures purported to assess habitual tendencies. For example, devaluation sensitivity on habit tasks correlates with indices of model-based (but not model-free) control derived from sequential decision-making tasks ( Friedel et al., 2014 ; Gillan et al., 2015 ; Sjoerds et al., 2016 ), and performance in a lab-based slips-of-action task correlates with self-reported attentional control but not with self-reported slips of action (errors where a well-practiced action is executed despite being inappropriate) in a real-world slips-of-action task ( Linnebank et al., 2018 ). Finally, a recent comprehensive validation study found poor convergent validity across commonly used habit tasks, and no significant correlations between task-derived measures and self-reported real-life habits ( Nebe et al., 2024 ). Together, these findings suggest that many existing task paradigms are more sensitive to individual differences in GD control processes than to processes supporting habit formation or habitual control. Thus, the challenge in demonstrating habit formation in human laboratory tasks may lie partly in finding ways to reduce GD control so as to allow habits to emerge ( Buabang et al., 2024 ). Consistent with this view, one study found that over the course of training over multiple days, participants’ overt responses become no more devaluation-insensitive, but that their responses to devalued stimuli become slower, suggesting that habitual impulses are present in overtrained participants but can be suppressed with deliberate GD control ( Luque et al., 2020 ). Other work has shown that acute stress amplifies devaluation-insensitive responding in humans ( Schwabe & Wolf, 2009 , 2010 ), and another study successfully demonstrated overtraining-induced habits in humans using a forced response time paradigm ( Hardwick et al., 2019 ). Revealing habits by extrinsically limiting the amount of GD control participants can exert has ecological relevance: people are especially prone to performing unwanted habits when they are distracted, fatigued, or pressed for time. However, in naturalistic settings, habits also often emerge without such constraints, when GD control momentarily lapses, allowing habits to slip through. For instance, one might habitually turn left at a familiar intersection as when driving to work, despite intending to turn right toward the doctor’s office; the familiarity of the route reduces vigilance, allowing attention to wander at the critical moment. Such lapses reflect a momentary failure to engage control, not an inability to do so due to a time limit or acute stress. ### From stigma to trust: A paradigm shift for understanding perceptions of individuals with bipolar disorder
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Bitterly TB et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct

Individuals with bipolar disorder (BD) face professional and personal challenges such as underemployment. Navigating these challenges involves the dilemma of whether to disclose their illness or conceal it and risk presenting symptoms. Examinations of these challenges are often anchored in stigma, which has attributed negative employment outcomes for individuals with BD to adverse reactions from managers following disclosure. Across three experiments, including a managerial sample and a novel chatbot interaction, and eight supplemental studies (N = 8,112), the present research proposes and tests a trust-based attributional model. Mania, the hallmark symptom of BD, signals activation-based unpredictability, which serially erodes the perceived trustworthiness of an employee, ultimately compromising a manager's trust and willingness to retain the employee. The current findings demonstrate that disclosure is not uniformly harmful and that stigma operates as a distal moderator. An employee disclosing in the absence of mania can appear less predictable, and managers high in preexisting stigma toward BD generally view employees as less predictable. However, when manic symptoms are visible, disclosure buffers against their negative impact by providing an explanation for the erratic behavior. Together, this research reveals that moving beyond stigma-centric accounts clarifies managerial perceptions of employees with BD, with disclosure and stigma shaping this trust-based process. ### "A girl is the champion!": Well-intentioned gendered messages can backfire
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Bailey AH et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct

Initiatives aimed at promoting gender equity in majority-male domains often emphasize gender to highlight women's achievements (e.g., "Space4Women," "all-women spacewalk"). Does such gendered messaging help achieve equity goals? Two theoretical accounts offer competing predictions. An informational account suggests that referencing a gender alongside a domain should strengthen beliefs that the referenced gender participates and succeeds. A pragmatic account, however, predicts that explicitly marking gender may backfire by signaling that the referenced gender is atypical in the domain-otherwise, why mention it? We tested these two possibilities with U.S. children (ages 4-10; N = 326) and adults (N = 788). Participants learned about fictional activities described with either gendered messaging (e.g., "A girl/boy is the Gazzer champion") or control messaging (e.g., "Joan/Rob is the Gazzer champion"). Gendered messaging backfired compared to control messaging on average, causing participants to infer lower participation by the referenced gender and, among adults, lower ability. Gendered messaging also undermined participants' interest in the activities. A meta-analysis across experiments revealed small-to-moderate effects that were larger for adults than children, consistent with developmental changes in pragmatic reasoning. Finally, backfire effects disappeared for messaging about fictional groups, suggesting that background knowledge about the referenced group may be needed to trigger pragmatic inferences. Together, these findings advance theoretical accounts of pragmatic reasoning while indicating that well-intentioned gendered messaging may paradoxically undermine equity goals. A more effective alternative may be to clearly feature women in a domain without explicitly framing it as being "for them."

### Individual differences in motor and auditory activation in second language speech learning: Roles of domain-general auditory processing
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Saito-van Poeteren K et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct

While existing models emphasize the critical roles of auditory and motor systems in speech perception, it remains unclear how individual learners differentially rely on these systems and how their relative weighting relates to language learning. This study investigates the neural mechanisms through which individual differences in domain-general auditory processing influence reliance on these networks during second language (L2) phonetic learning. Focusing on the acquisition of English [æ] and [ʌ] by 207 Japanese learners, we examined participants with varying auditory precision profiles using both behavioral and functional magnetic resonance imaging analyses as they performed discrimination tasks for speech and nonverbal sounds. The findings revealed a pattern consistent with a triangular relationship among learners' auditory precision, brain activation in motor-related regions, and L2 speech development. Learners with lower auditory precision tended to show less advanced L2 vowel proficiency and exhibited higher levels of sensorimotor activation during discrimination of English [æ] and [ʌ], but not during discrimination of nonspeech counterparts with comparable second formant variation. In light of theoretical accounts of adult L2 speech learning (Best & Tyler, 2007), one possible interpretation is that greater reliance on articulatory processing among less auditory-precise and less experienced or proficient L2 learners reflects the processing of L2 sounds through first language articulatory routines. In contrast, learners with higher auditory precision attained not only more advanced L2 vowel proficiency but also lower levels of sensorimotor activity, comparable to that observed during nonverbal processing, suggesting more balanced spectrotemporal and articulatory analyses of sounds, which optimize both the process and outcomes of L2 speech learning.

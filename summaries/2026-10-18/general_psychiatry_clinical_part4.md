### Stimulant Persistence in Adult ADHD: A Retrospective Analysis of a Large Clinical Cohort
**כתב עת:** 📄 Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica (Acta Psychiatr Scand) | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Wettstein R et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 15

INTRODUCTION: Network meta-analyses suggest dexamphetamines (dAMP) may yield more favourable short-term outcomes than methylphenidate (MPH) in adults with ADHD, yet evidence on long-term comparative persistence remains scarce. This study compared 12-month treatment persistence between MPH and dAMP in a large multicentre cohort and explored associations with ADHD clinical presentations and objectively measured core symptoms.

METHODS: Stimulant persistence was defined by the primary outcome, 'time-to-transition', the interval from initiation to switching stimulant classes, transitioning to non-stimulants or complete discontinuation. Patients were censored at 12 months or at last clinical contact. A multivariable mixed-effects Cox proportional hazard model with a random intercept for clinicians was used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and assess the relative impact of stimulant class on persistence, including its interactions with DSM-based clinical presentations and objective indices.

RESULTS: Among 12,995 participants (49% female), 62% initiated dAMP and 38% MPH. Median time-to-transition was 33.1 weeks for dAMP versus 15.0 weeks for MPH. MPH was associated with a 45% increased hazard for non-persistence compared to dAMP (HR = 1.45, 95% CI [1.36-1.55]). Regarding clinical and objective factors, hyperactive/impulsive presentation (HR = 1.14, p = 0.025) and objective impulsivity (HR = 1.05, p = 0.002) were independently associated with non-persistence. A significant interaction was observed between stimulant class and objective hyperactivity (p = 0.003), indicating that dAMP's persistence advantage became more pronounced with increasing motor activity (HR = 1.09). Nevertheless, these associations were secondary to the overarching effect of the stimulant class itself.

CONCLUSION: Treatment with dAMP demonstrates significantly superior 12-month persistence compared to MPH in adults with ADHD. While clinical and objective measures of impulsivity and hyperactivity identify patients at higher risk for treatment non-persistence, dAMP's advantage remains consistent across all presentations. These findings suggest that prioritizing dAMP may enhance long-term treatment persistence in adult ADHD clinical practice.

### Artificial Intelligence in suicide prevention: promise, peril, and clinical implications for clinical practice
**כתב עת:** 📄 Dialogues Clin Neurosci | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Nobile B, Courtet P | **תאריך:** 2027 Dec

Abstract The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into psychiatry is progressing rapidly, with promising yet underexplored applications in suicide prevention. While most existing studies have focused on suicide risk prediction, AI could be applied more broadly across the suicide prevention continuum. This review explores AI’s potential in improving the detection of suicidal ideation (SI), enhancing clinician training, and expanding patient access to psychotherapeutic support. Studies suggest that AI models may identify SI more sensitively than clinicians by detecting subtle linguistic or semantic cues. Furthermore, AI-based virtual patients have been shown to improve clinicians’ confidence and accuracy in suicide risk assessments, particularly when used alongside traditional training methods. On the patient side, CBT-inspired AI tools offer scalable, on-demand support, but most remain unregulated and clinically unvalidated. Despite their perceived empathy and availability, such tools pose ethical and clinical risks. Many patients use AI tools without professional guidance, a trend clinicians often underestimate. This disconnect calls for urgent regulatory oversight and professional engagement. Without clear clinical validation, AI-based tools may evolve outside medical frameworks, exposing vulnerable individuals to preventable harm. Psychiatry must not only adapt but also lead, ensuring that innovation is harnessed responsibly to serve patients with empathy, safety, and scientific rigor.

Introduction The use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) is spreading rapidly across developed countries, including among patients with mental disorders. In clinical neuroscience, AI can be viewed as a set of computational tools that mimic certain aspects of human cognition—such as information processing, language comprehension, and risk evaluation. When applied to psychiatry, AI models can process and interpret complex data to identify mental health risks, assist in clinical diagnosis, or personalise interventions. Importantly, AI does not replicate human understanding or empathy, but it can detect subtle markers in data that may escape human attention, such as linguistic patterns associated with suicidal ideation. In a recent U.S. survey among adults with a mental disorder who had used an AI large language model (LLM), such as ChatGPT, within the past year, nearly half reported using it as a form of psychological support (Rubin 2026 ). Even more strikingly, two-thirds of these individuals stated that interacting with AI improved their mental health condition. In younger populations, AI use for mental health purposes is also substantial. A recent survey of adolescents and young adults (ages 12–21) found that approximately 13% used AI for mental health advice, with this percentage increasing significantly among those over 18. Among AI users, over 65% used it monthly for mental health-related advice, and more than 90% found it very helpful (McBain et al. 2025 ). These numbers highlight that a significant proportion of individuals with mental health conditions are using AI tools without any clinical supervision. Alarmingly, most psychiatrists are unaware of this widespread usage—or outright deny it—despite its potential impact on both patients and clinical practice (Frances, 2026 ). When used correctly, AI could offer substantial benefits in mental health care. However, unsupervised, or inappropriate use may lead to serious and even life-threatening consequences. This issue is particularly critical in the field of suicidal behaviours (i.e., suicide and suicide attempts). A recent tragic case involved a suicidal adolescent who disclosed his suicidal thoughts to an AI-based LLM and eventually died by hanging, reportedly after the AI explained to him how to tie the knot (Nagata et al. 2026 ). Despite such catastrophic events, AI may still hold promise in improving care for suicidal individuals (Lee et al. 2021 ). Numerous studies have shown promising results in predicting suicide risk using LLMs and machine learning (ML) algorithms, based on electronic health records or social media data (Arowosegbe and Oyelade 2023 ; Levkovich and Omar 2024 ; Badian et al. 2023 ; Mansoor and Ansari 2024 ; Du et al. 2018 ; Wu et al. 2023 ; Sherekar and Mehta 2025 ). While improving the prediction of suicidal behaviour is undeniably important, many other potential applications of AI in suicide prevention remain underexplored. Indeed, the implementation of predictive models in real-life clinical practice is still far from reality. In contrast, some AI-based tools could be applied more immediately to support suicide prevention, such as: (i) detecting suicidal ideation in clinical settings through analysis of clinical interviews; (ii) improving clinician training; and (iii) supporting patients in coping with suicidal thoughts. While these applications may offer real benefits, it is essential to consider the potential risks of relying too heavily on AI, especially in the context of vulnerable individuals. Accordingly, the aim of this review is to examine these three domains of direct AI application in suicide prevention, while critically discussing their potential benefits and associated risks and to address the broader challenges of integrating AI safely into suicide prevention practices. Clinical judgment and Artificial Intelligence: Complementarity, not substitution Suicidal behaviours can be conceptualised as existing along a continuum, ranging from passive suicidal ideation (SI) to active ideation, suicide attempts, and ultimately death by suicide. Identifying patients experiencing SI is thus the crucial first step in this chain, offering an opportunity to intervene early and prevent progression to more severe outcomes (Nobile et al. 2022 ; Courtet and Baca-García 2024 ). This is not only essential from a suicide prevention standpoint, but also because SI is itself associated with worse psychiatric prognosis, including treatment resistance and recurrence in mood disorders (Nobile et al. 2024 ; Bergfeld et al. 2018 ). Yet, detecting SI remains a substantial clinical challenge. Studies have revealed a concerning level of discordance—up to 75%—between clinicians’ evaluations and patients’ self-reports regarding suicidal thoughts (Nobile et al. 2023 ; Nobile et al. 2024 ; Gao et al. 2015 ). In most cases, patients reported SI that clinicians failed to detect. However, this discrepancy cannot be attributed solely to limitations in clinical judgement. A significant body of evidence suggests that some patients, particularly adolescents and individuals from diverse or vulnerable backgrounds, may not disclose suicidal thoughts during clinical encounters due to stigma, fear of hospitalisation, uncertainty, or a weak therapeutic alliance (Uhl et al. 2023 ). This is far from a trivial oversight: approximately 56% of individuals who die by suicide have consulted a healthcare professional within the four weeks preceding their death (O’Rourke et al. 2023 ). Moreover, patients who only endorse SI via self-report questionnaires, but not during clinical interviews, appear to carry a suicide attempt risk equivalent to those whose SI is confirmed by clinicians (Nobile et al. 2024 ; Uhl et al. 2023 ). These findings highlight both the limitations of clinical judgement alone and the challenges related to patient disclosure underscoring the need for enhanced tools to detect SI more reliably. ### Low-threshold secondary and tertiary suicide prevention measures for individuals with suicid-related thoughts and behaviors: a scoping review
**כתב עת:** 📄 Front Public Health | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Spahn C et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

Introduction Suicide rates remain persistently high in many regions worldwide, including Germany. The 2024 German National Suicide Prevention Strategy emphasizes the strengthening of evidence-based approaches. In this context, a comprehensive synthesis of low-threshold secondary and tertiary suicide prevention measures for adults is required. Methods This scoping review set out to address this need and followed established methodological guidance and the PRISMA-ScR reporting guidelines. PubMed and Web of Science were searched on September 1, 2025, for studies published from January 1, 2005, onwards. To be eligible for inclusion, studies needed to examine low-threshold secondary or tertiary suicide prevention measures targeting adults with suicide-related thoughts and behaviors (STBs). Interventions focusing on primary medical care, psychotherapy, pharmacotherapy, gatekeepers and means restriction were excluded. Results Out of 1,897 records, 24 studies met the inclusion criteria. Most studies investigated voice-based ( n = 9; 37.5%) and text-based ( n = 7; 29.2%) crisis interventions within suicide prevention lifelines. These interventions consistently demonstrated reductions in suicidal ideation and psychological distress, with one exception: a moderated peer-to-peer forum. Outreach messages ( n = 3; 12.5%) and web-based educational (and counseling) measures ( n = 5; 20.8%) showed promising but more heterogeneous findings. Outcomes indicated potential benefits, though the evidence was less consistent. Discussion The findings suggest that voice- and text-based crisis interventions are supported by a growing evidence base, primarily demonstrating short-term effects. Web-based educational (and counseling) measures and outreach messages, while heterogeneous, show potential for individuals with STBs through the use of emerging technologies. Future research should examine mechanisms of action, long-term outcomes, and employ randomized controlled trial designs (where feasible) to strengthen the evidence base. Introduction Suicid-related thoughts and behaviors (STBs) constitute a major global public health concern ( 1 ). Over the past decades, various suicide prevention initiatives, improved access to care, and societal developments have contributed to a notable decline in suicide rates in many countries ( 1 , 2 ). Nevertheless, this decline has not progressed in recent years to the extent anticipated under the WHO Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) ( 3 ). In Germany, following a marked reduction between 1980 and 2000, suicide rates have remained largely stable over the past 25 years, ranging between 9,000 and 10,000 cases annually ( 2 ). In 2024, the German Federal Ministry of Health published a National Suicide Prevention Strategy, with the aim of strengthening evidence-based suicide prevention in Germany ( 4 ). As part of this initiative, recommendations are needed that specifiy which secondary and tertiary preventive measures are most suitable to support the strategy. Against this backdrop, the present scoping review was conducted to synthesize the available evidence on low-threshold secondary and tertiary suicide prevention measures, to identify effective measures, and to provide evidence for the German National Suicide Prevention Strategy. In the final report of the German National Suicide Prevention Programme, Fiedler et al. ( 5 ) define low-threshold suicide prevention as encompassing “professional, semi-professional, and lay-supported services” that provide “barrier-free access for help-seeking individuals across the full spectrum of suicidal ideation and behaviour” and aim to reduce suicidality while providing psychosocial support. According to the authors, such services should be easily accessible, confidential, ideally free of charge, and available without waiting periods. Secondary and tertiary suicide prevention measures differ from primary prevention in that they target individuals who already experience or have experienced STBs. Primary prevention, in contrast, focuses on the general population or at-risk groups without manifest symptoms, aiming to prevent the emergence of STBs. Secondary measures relate to the early management of suicidal ideation, for example through crisis intervention, whereas tertiary measures focus on relapse prevention, such as the prevention of repeating suicide attempts ( 6 ). In practice and research, secondary and tertiary measures are often difficult to distinguish and are therefore considered jointly in this review ( 7 ). In practice, a wide range of suicide prevention measures are implemented, and a variety of secondary and tertiary suicide prevention strategies have been analyzed in the research literature. Over the past 10–15 years, research on these measures has increased markedly. Several scoping and systematic reviews have attempted to provide an overview of the various areas of suicide prevention and the available evidence. Some reviews have focused on measures for specific populations [e.g., men ( 8 , 9 ), students ( 10 ), older adults ( 11 )], or geographic regions [e.g., rural areas ( 12 )], while others have examined specific intervention types, such as web-based suicide prevention ( 13 – 15 ). Miller et al. ( 16 ) primarily examined the mechanisms underlying the effectiveness of Lifelines in the United States. The focus of Miller et al. ( 16 ) is not primarily on the effects of the intervention on STBs, and their analysis is limited to a single region. A recent review published in 2025 assessed the effectiveness of gatekeeper programs ( 17 ). Among other outcomes, changes in the behavior of trained individuals were observed (e.g., whether they intervened more frequently or in different ways). In summary, existing reviews have largely focused on specific interventions, target populations, or geographic regions, or have addressed aspects other than the effectiveness of interventions in relation to STBs or psychological distress. To date, no comprehensive overview exists regarding the effects of low-threshold secondary and tertiary suicide prevention measures on STBs in adults. This gap is addressed by the present review. Where previous research intersects with our research question, it has been taken into account. For this reason, gatekeeper programs will not be addressed in our review. Excluded from the present review are also suicide prevention measures with a main focus on medical care (e.g., outpatient and inpatient care), pharmacotherapy, and psychotherapeutic treatment, as strong evidence already exists in these domains. Some reviews covering multiple suicide prevention strategies have demonstrated effectiveness primarily for these measures. Mann et al. ( 18 ) showed that primary care training, psychiatric treatment and follow-up, as well as pharmacotherapy, are effective in reducing STBs. Additional studies provide comprehensive evidence for psychotherapeutic interventions ( 19 – 22 ). These measures are also reflected in clinical recommendations, including those of the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence ( 23 ). A particularly suicide-focused psychotherapy appears to be a potentially effective approach ( 24 ). Zalsmann et al. ( 25 ) reviewed evidence for seven types of suicide prevention measures (public and physician education, media strategies, screening, restriction of access to means, treatment, and internet or hotline support) and also found support for pharmacological and psychotherapeutic interventions, school-based awareness programs, and means restriction.

### Identifying expanded adverse childhood experiences and examining their relationship with health outcomes: a systematic review protocol
**כתב עת:** ⭐ BMJ Open *(IF: 105.7)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Forke CM et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 28

Abstract Introduction Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are associated with poor health outcomes, with higher cumulative exposure conveying more risk. Although earlier ACE studies focus on household-level adversities, growing evidence explores traumatic childhood experiences originating outside the home (expanded ACEs). Expanded ACEs may co-occur with household-level ACEs, causing misclassification bias if not measured. A comprehensive list of expanded ACEs has not been identified, and no unified framework exists to consider their impacts. This systematic review will identify expanded ACEs published in peer-reviewed literature and examine their associations with physical and mental health outcomes and behaviours by demographic subgroups, offering the first unified framework for expanded ACEs’ impacts on health. Methods and analysis Six databases were systematically searched in February 2026 for global, peer-reviewed studies published in English since database inception: PubMed/Medline, Embase, CINAHL, PsycInfo, CENTRAL and Scopus. Eligibility includes studies involving humans without restriction to date, setting, population or location; studies with ACEs originating outside the home as the exposure; and studies with outcomes including physical or mental health outcomes anchored to International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision or Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition codes or behaviours. Researchers (RAs) screen titles/abstracts using pre-established eligibility criteria on training batches of 30–50 articles with faculty oversight. Once agreement consistently reaches 80%, RAs may use a hybrid screening process that prioritises independent, dual screening, relying on single-screening with fidelity checks to stay within budget and timeline, while maintaining rigour. Title/abstract screening is followed by full-text review and extraction. Study quality will be assessed using validated tools, with limitations reported. Data will be summarised via quantitative synthesis or narrative summary as appropriate. Ethics and dissemination The University of Pennsylvania #26-7542 determined this work is not human subjects research. Dissemination will occur through peer-reviewed presentations and publications. PROSPERO registration number CRD420261432925.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY This review systematically catalogues the expanded adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) that appear in the literature. The review will report effect sizes between expanded ACEs and physical and mental health outcomes and behaviours. Subgroup analyses will identify relationships across demographic subgroups by age, race and sex. Articles are limited to those published in English. Grey literature is excluded. Introduction In a landmark study published in 1998, researchers from Kaiser Permanente and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) demonstrated the negative life course impact of childhood exposure to abuse, neglect and various household stressors, termed adverse childhood experiences (ACEs). 1 ACEs are defined broadly by the CDC as ‘potentially traumatic events that occur in childhood (age 18 years and younger)’. 2 In the more than 25 years following publication of the original Kaiser-CDC ACE study, the association between childhood exposure to adversities and poor health outcomes (health risk behaviours and mental and physical health conditions) has been repeatedly substantiated, recognising that higher cumulative exposure conveys more risk. 3 – 8 The original Kaiser-CDC ACE study 1 focused predominantly on traumatic events occurring within the home or within the context of families (ie, household ACEs) and included participants who were mostly white, upper-middle class and medically insured. Subsequent research measured adversity in other geographic and demographic subgroups and specifically measured adversities occurring outside the home, seeking a more wholistic understanding of childhood adversity. 9 Between 2013 and 2015, two independent research teams reported on childhood traumatic events occurring outside the home (ie, expanded ACEs): The Philadelphia ACE Task Force and The Crimes Against Children Research Center. Findings from these studies uncovered important connections between health outcomes and stressful encounters occurring outside the home (ie, neighbourhood violence, racial discrimination, peer victimisation). 10 – 14 Since their publication 10 years ago, these papers have been heavily referenced with more than 4300 combined citations, indicating strong interest in expanded ACEs. Substantial additional evidence now exists to show that various community ACEs are associated with health outcomes. 15 – 20 With considerable overlap between expanded ACEs and household-level ACEs, 10 recent work has highlighted the importance of measuring household and community ACEs in tandem to avoid misclassification bias and overestimated effect sizes for household-level adversities. 21 Prior work highlights the need to better understand the types of community-level ACEs youth experience and their relationship to health outcomes. While there has been rapid expansion of studies exploring expanded ACEs, a comprehensive list of expanded ACEs has not been identified, nor is there a unified framework to consider their impacts on health, creating a significant barrier to advancing this work. Studies continuously reflect the importance of cumulative trauma on health outcomes, and expanded ACEs comprise a critical part of that equation. The proposed study has the potential to improve trauma-informed research and practice by collating a list of expanded ACEs and describing their associations with physical and mental health outcomes and behaviours. Our objectives are to (1) complete a systematic review of existing peer-reviewed, primary research to identify what ACEs originating outside the home have been studied; (2) examine the associations between ACEs originating outside the home with physical and mental health outcomes and behaviours; and (3) identify if these relationships differ across specific demographic subgroups, such as age, race, sex and socioeconomic indicators. Methods and analysis Protocol and registration This protocol was created using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Protocols checklist ( online supplemental appendix A ). 22 The protocol is registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO Registration CRD420261432925). 23 Timeline The study start date is February 2026; given the iterative nature of systematic reviews, the projected completion date may change as the review progresses. Therefore, we refer readers to the living PROSPERO record for the most current timeline, which will be updated throughout the review process. Review team Three members of the research team have ACE content expertise (CMF, RW, PFC), and two have methods expertise in systematic reviews (JL and YZ-I). A team of research assistants (RAs) will receive orientation to the study topic and methods to provide foundational knowledge about conventional ACEs, expanded ACEs and systematic reviews. After the initial orientation, RAs will receive more in-depth training on conducting systematic reviews and Covidence to ensure rigour of the review. ### Sensory hyper- and hyporeactivity at age 3 as transdiagnostic prospective markers of neurodevelopmental, emotional, and behavioral difficulties at age 8: A population-based cohort study in the Norwegian Mother, Father, and Child Cohort Study (MoBa)
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Development and Psychopathology (Dev Psychopathol) *(IF: 5.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Larsen K et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 6

Atypical sensory reactivity is a core feature of autism but has mostly been studied in small, clinical, cross-sectional samples. We examined whether early sensory atypicalities are transdiagnostic or unique to autism and whether sensory modality and persistence matter. This study is based on the Norwegian Mother, Father, and Child Cohort Study (MoBa), a Norwegian population-based cohort in which race/ethnicity is not recorded. In a subsample ( N  = 113,785; 51.0% boys, 49.0% girls; child age at the age-8 assessment M = 97.6 months, SD = 2.0; analytic samples  n  ≈ 31,000-33,000), three parent-reported markers at age 3 (auditory hypersensitivity, reduced pain sensitivity, and unusual sensory interest) predicted seven age-8 outcomes: autism traits, ADHD inattention and hyperactivity, oppositional and conduct problems, anxiety, and depressed mood. In robust regressions and a three-wave persistence analysis, sensory atypicality predicted all domains (standardized β). Autism traits were most strongly predicted ( β  = 0.18 composite; 0.42 persistent), yet not uniquely (autism-ADHD difference = 0.05 SD). Auditory hypersensitivity was related to internalizing problems, reduced pain sensitivity to externalizing problems, and lower anxiety. Cumulative load showed a graded dose-response. Effects were small, indicating population-level rather than individual predictive value.

### Prenatal Vitamin C supplementation mediates the association between prenatal distress and infant negative affect: A prospective cohort study
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Developmental Psychology (Dev Psychol) *(IF: 5.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Wood EK et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 2

Negative affect (NA) is a temperament trait reflecting the propensity to feel and express negative emotions. High NA in infancy is an early risk factor for future psychopathology and clinical diagnoses. Prenatal maternal depression, anxiety, and stress are linked to elevated infant NA, suggesting developmental programming effects. Given its anti-oxidative and stress-regulating properties, we investigated whether prenatal vitamin C mediates the association between maternal prenatal distress and infant NA. Pregnant participants ( N =302; 73.2% White, non-Hispanic; M age =31.94 years, SD =4.23 years) reported their prenatal vitamin C supplementation and completed three 24-hour dietary recalls from which dietary vitamin C intake was derived. Participants also completed assessments for prenatal depression (Center for Epidemiological Studies-Depression [CES-D], Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale [EPDS]), anxiety (State-Trait Anxiety Inventory [STAI], Beck Anxiety Inventory [BAI]), and stress (Perceived Stress Scale [PSS]). Six months following delivery, infant NA was assessed via the Infant Behavior Questionnaire-Revised. Confirmatory factor analysis supported a latent prenatal maternal distress variable, using prenatal CES-D, EPDS, STAI, BAI, and PSS as indicators. Higher prenatal maternal distress predicted higher infant NA at six months ( β =0.21, p =.001). Adjusting for covariates, structural equation modeling showed that prenatal vitamin C supplementation, but not dietary vitamin C intake, mediated the association between maternal prenatal distress and infant NA (indirect β =.02, bootstrapped 95% CI [0.01, 0.05]). As such, lower vitamin C intake during pregnancy may influence fetal neurodevelopment via altering the intrauterine environment. These findings point to prenatal vitamin C supplementation’s potential as a modifiable and scalable intervention to promote healthy early socioemotional development.

Pregnancy is a time of increased vulnerability for mental health conditions ( Howard & Khalifeh, 2020 ). Mounting evidence suggests that in utero exposures to elevated maternal distress (anxiety, depression, and stress) exerts programming effects on infant temperament ( Beijers et al., 2010 ; Blair et al., 2011 ; McGrath et al., 2008 ; Thiel et al., 2021 ), predisposing infants to future psychopathology, with effects persisting into adolescence and beyond ( Rogers et al., 2020 ). High negative affect (NA), or temperamental tendencies for elevated sadness and irritability ( Gartstein & Rothbart, 2003 ), is an early risk marker for adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes and later mental health concerns ( Grant et al., 2009 ; Kostyrka-Allchorne et al., 2020 ). It is of great interest, therefore, to identify factors involved in the mechanism by which prenatal maternal distress influences the developing brain. Adequate maternal nutrient intake during pregnancy is critical for fetal development ( Christian et al., 2015 ). Stress is a well-established contributor to unhealthy dietary patterns in the general population ( Hill et al., 2022 ). Prenatal depression, stress, and anxiety are similarly linked to poor dietary quality in pregnancy ( Avalos et al., 2020 ; Baskin et al., 2015 ). Preclinical evidence ( Mitchell et al., 2022 ; Sullivan et al., 2010 ) and emerging evidence from human studies ( Brunst et al., 2014 ; Gustafsson et al., 2016 ; Wood et al., 2025 ), links maternal perinatal nutrition to infant behavioral health and temperament, with diets high in fats and simple sugars, or with low omega-3:omega-6 ratios, associated with offspring psychopathology risk, including greater NA. While maternal prenatal nutrition is increasingly considered as a modifiable factor in programming infant temperament ( Gartstein & Skinner, 2018 ), key nutritional targets remain poorly defined. Here, we examine the association between maternal prenatal vitamin C intake and infant NA. Vitamin C, L-ascorbic acid, is an essential, water-soluble micronutrient with critical roles supporting fetal development. Pregnancy involves increased vitamin C demands, reflected in higher recommended daily allowances (85 mg/day vs. 75 mg/day for non-pregnant adults) ( Institute of Medicine (US) Panel on Dietary Antioxidants and Related Compounds, 2000 ). Maternal vitamin C intake is reflected in maternal plasma concentrations of vitamin C ( Scaife et al., 2006 ), with newborns reportedly exhibiting higher cord blood concentrations of vitamin C compared to the vitamin C concentrations in maternal circulation, impacts of maternal vitamin C intake on the fetal environment. Indeed, vitamin C is preferentially transferred to the fetus under typical conditions; however, this does not occur in instances of maternal vitamin C insufficiency ( Schjoldager et al., 2013 ). Because humans cannot endogenously synthesize vitamin C, the fetus relies completely on adequate maternal vitamin C supply to meet developmental demands, including protection from oxidative stress ( Padayatty et al., 2003 ), facilitation of collagen synthesis and iron metabolism ( Lane & Richardson, 2014 ), and placental support ( Schjoldager et al., 2015 ). Vitamin C has immunomodulatory properties ( Carr & Maggini, 2017 ; Jafarnejad et al., 2018 ), which may be relevant to fetal development because, while inflammatory signaling is a normal and necessary part of pregnancy, excessive or dysregulated maternal inflammation has been linked to altered fetal neurodevelopment and later child behavioral outcomes, including elevated infant NA ( Gumusoglu & Stevens, 2019 ; Gustafsson et al., 2019 ). Importantly, vitamin C is highly concentrated in the brain ( Rice & Russo-Menna, 1997 ), and vitamin C transport into neural tissue is actively regulated, suggesting specific functional roles in central nervous system development ( Hansen et al., 2014 ). Preclinical work further suggests that vitamin C supports neurodevelopmental processes relevant to neuronal differentiation and myelination, including dopaminergic differentiation in fetal midbrain stem cells and oligodendrocyte differentiation and remyelination in experimental models ( Guo et al., 2018 ; He et al., 2015 ). Vitamin C also supports redox homeostasis ( Lykkesfeldt & Moos, 2005 ), catecholaminergic neurotransmitter system modulation ( May et al., 2013 ), and iron- and collagen-related processes relevant to vascular and tissue integrity ( Lane & Richardson, 2014 ). Preclinical work further supports the role of vitamin C exposure may influence fetal brain development, with vitamin C transporter knockout mice exhibiting perinatal mortality, cerebral hemorrhage, and increased neural oxidative stress ( Harrison et al., 2010 ; Sotiriou et al., 2002 ), while guinea pig models show that prenatal vitamin C deficiency increases markers of oxidative stress in the fetal brain during late gestation ( Paidi et al., 2014 ) and reduces postnatal hippocampal volume and disrupts hippocampal development ( Tveden-Nyborg et al., 2012 ). Early postnatal vitamin C deficiency reduces hippocampal neuron counts and impairs spatial memory ( Tveden-Nyborg et al., 2009 ). Although direct human evidence linking prenatal vitamin C intake specifically to infant NA is limited, available human and preclinical literatures support vitamin C as a biologically plausible prenatal exposure relevant to fetal brain development and infant behavioral outcomes, including NA. ### Adverse Childhood Experiences and Adolescent Substance Use: School Connectedness as a Moderator of Risk
**כתב עת:** 📄 J Child Adolesc Trauma | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Loes C | **תאריך:** 2026 Dec

Although the link between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and adolescent substance use is well established, it remains unclear how factors such as school connectedness might moderate this relationship. This study addressed this gap by examining whether the relationship between ACEs and substance use varies by levels of school connectedness among a large sample of adolescents. Data from 23,078 students in grades 9 through 12, collected via the 2022 Florida Youth Substance Abuse Survey, were analyzed using negative binomial regression models adjusting for a range of potential confounding influences. Results indicated that each additional ACE was associated with a 17% higher incidence rate of substance use (IRR = 1.17,  p  < .001). Greater school connectedness was strongly protective (IRR = 0.71,  p  < .001), which corresponds to a 29% reduction in the expected rate of substance use. Although the positive interaction term (IRR = 1.04,  p  < .001) indicates slight relative attenuation, school connectedness remained significantly associated with lower predicted substance use across all levels of ACE exposure.

### Incorporating Childhood Identity-Based Discrimination into the Adverse Childhood Experiences Framework
**כתב עת:** 📄 J Child Adolesc Trauma | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Powers EA et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Dec

Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) are prevalent stressors that can have lasting negative impacts on well-being. However, ACEs research has historically excluded childhood discrimination, which is an important contributor to health disparities. The goal of this study was to identify the frequency of discrimination-related ACEs in childhood and examine their effects on young adult mental health, accounting for other ACEs. This study recruited 160 18-25-year-olds who identified as members of US racial or ethnic minority groups (n = 99), as members of the LGBTQIA+ community (n = 107), or as both (n = 46). Participants were recruited to complete an online survey both through a subject pool enrolled in college-level Psychology courses as well as through community and online flyering in the Northeast US. The anonymous survey assessed history of Sexual and Gender Minority ACEs (SGM-ACEs), Racial and Ethnic Minority ACEs (REM-ACEs), traditionally assessed ACEs, and current depression and anxiety. In several models, the inclusion of SGM-ACEs alongside traditionally assessed ACEs explained significant variance in outcomes. Results demonstrated that experiencing more SGM-ACEs uniquely predicted higher anxiety (b = 1.39, SE = 0.39, β = 0.40, p < 0.001) as well as depression (b = 1.69, SE = 0.47, β = 0.41, p < 0.001), accounting for the effects of traditionally assessed ACEs and socioeconomic status. REM-ACEs were not uniquely associated with mental health. These findings highlight how larger cultural, systemic, and political influences need to be better accounted for in ACEs measures and can contribute to long-term adjustment.

### Resiliency as a Protective Factor Against the Negative Impact of Adverse Childhood Experiences on Suicidality
**כתב עת:** 📄 J Child Adolesc Trauma | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Thompson M et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Dec

Suicide is a leading cause of death in the United States. Suicide risk is higher among those who have experienced more adverse childhood events (ACEs). Yet the impact of ACEs on suicide risk could potentially be buffered by individual-level psychological characteristics. The purpose of this study was to test whether resiliency moderated the association between ACEs and suicide attempts. Using prospective data from a large, nationally representative sample of 8,671 respondents surveyed over 30 years, we found that although each additional ACE increased the odds for making a suicide attempt by 56%, these odds were significantly lower when resilience was high. Post hoc probing of the significant interaction term indicated that when ACEs were high, but resiliency was low, the probability that a participant had made a suicide attempt was 4.4%, compared to 1.6% when ACEs were high, but resiliency was also high. Our findings on the importance of resiliency in promoting mental health indicate the need for programs designed to promote resiliency across different settings.

Suicide as a Public Health Problem Suicide is a significant public health problem and has been a leading cause of death in the United States for the past 70years (2008 (Vital and Health Statistics; CDC, 2026 ). Rates of suicide increased 37% between 2000 and 2018, then decreased 5% between 2018 and 2020 before increasing again to their peak in 2022, with an age-adjusted rate per 100,000 of 14.2 (CDC, 2025 ). In 2023, there was a suicide death every 11 min in the United States, resulting in over 49,000 deaths (CDC, 2025 ). Suicide deaths represent the tip of the iceberg. Data from 2023 indicate that in addition to the 49,316 suicides, there were 1.5 million attempts, 3.7 million who made a suicide plan, and 12.8 million people who had serious thoughts about suicide (CDC, 2026 ). Thus, for every death by suicide, there are approximately 31 suicide attempts and 234 serious suicide ideations. One of the most significant predictors of suicide is a prior attempt (Coon et al., 2024 ). Although most people (approximately 90%) who attempt suicide do not eventually die by suicide (Owens et al., 2002 ), over half of those who die by suicide have made a prior attempt (Ribeiro et al., 2016 ). These findings reveal the importance of understanding risk and protective factors for nonfatal suicidal attempts. Risk Factors of Suicide Behaviors An extensive body of literature has identified many risk factors for suicidal behaviors, including mental health issues such as depression and anxiety, as well as stressful and traumatic life events (Franklin et al., 2017 ; Werbart et al., 2021 ). Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) have also specifically been linked to an increased risk for suicidal behaviors (Choi et al., 2017 ; Dube et al., 2001 ; Ng et al., 2018 ; Thompson & Kingree, 2022 ). The various types of ACEs that have been shown to increase risk for suicidal behaviors include experiencing physical, sexual, and/or emotional abuse and neglect (Devries et al., 2014 ; Miller et al., 2013 ; Thompson & Kingree, 2022 ), having a parent with alcohol problems (Thompson et al., 2017 ; Thompson & Kingree, 2022 ), having a parental in jail during childhood (Dube et al., 2001 ; Thompson & Kingree, 2022 ), losing a parent to death (Guldin et al., 2015 ; Rostila et al., 2016 ; Thompson & Kingree, 2022 ), as well as having a family history of suicide (Brent et al., 2015 ; Feigelman et al., 2016 ; Thompson & Kingree, 2022 ). Further, research indicates that the accumulation of ACEs is associated with an increased risk for suicide attempts, such that those who experienced three or more ACEs were three times more likely to have attempted suicide than those who did not experience any ACEs (Thompson et al., 2019 ). Resiliency as a Protective Factor Research has revealed several important risk factors for suicidality, including ACES, yet less research has focused on protective factors. One protective factor that this study seeks to investigate is resiliency. The term resilience refers to the ability to recover from or adjust easily to misfortune or change (Merriam- Webster, n.d. ). This definition implies that resiliency can be conceptualized as a buffer against traumatic life experiences, thereby reducing their negative impact. A meta-analysis of 60 studies on the role of resiliency indicated that it was associated with better mental health outcomes, such as depression and anxiety (Hu et al., 2015 ). Although less attention has focused on the moderating or stress-buffering role of resilience in the presence of adversity, studies suggest that an individual’s level of resiliency can impact how they respond to adverse or traumatic events (Johnson et al., 2011 ). That is, a traumatic event may not have the same impact on everyone who experiences it. Rather, an individual’s degree of resiliency can buffer against some of the negative consequences of an adverse experience. In the Hu et al. ( 2015 ) meta-analysis, the effect sizes between resilience and mental health were significantly stronger for those experiencing adversity compared to those not experiencing adversities. The purpose of our study was to determine if the prospective association between ACEs and suicide attempts was moderated by resiliency. We hypothesized that higher levels of ACEs would be prospectively associated with a higher likelihood of suicide attempts, and that suicide risk would be attenuated when resiliency was high. We used a nationally representative sample to generalize the results to the US population. We also took advantage of a longitudinal research design, such that we could examine the long-term protective impact of resiliency in the face of childhood trauma. Although the primary (i.e., universal) prevention of ACEs is ideal, this study can help inform secondary (i.e., selective) and tertiary (i.e., treatment) prevention efforts to reduce the negative sequelae of ACEs. Method Data Source and Sampling Procedures We used restricted data from the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health, Harris, 2018 ; Harris et al., 2019 ). The Add Health study has followed a nationally representative sample of adolescents for approximately 30 years, from adolescence through emerging adulthood, to middle adulthood. The Add Health study was approved by the University of North Carolina School of Public Health Institutional Review Board using guidelines based on the Code of Federal Regulations on the Protection of Human Subjects 45CFR46. Local IRB approval for the secondary analysis of the restricted data was also obtained. Add Health participants provided written informed consent for participation in all aspects of the study. Participants were surveyed for the first time in 1994–1995, and then re-surveyed five additional times, 1, 7, 14, 22, and 30 years later, for a total of six waves of data collection. Details about study design, sampling, and survey procedures, and response rates are available on the Add Health website ( http://www.cpc.unc.edu/addhealth ). The Add Health research design entailed multistage sampling and clustering of students withing schools. Thus, we needed to account for this in order to obtain unbiased variance estimates and to generalize our findings to the population. Thus, we restricted our sample to individuals with valid sampling weights to ensure our findings were nationally representative. The weight variable we used was recommended for longitudinal data with Wave 1 in-home survey participants who also completed surveys at Waves 4, 5, and 6 (Chen & Harris, 2020 ). This resulted in an analytic sample of 8,671 participants. The sample was evenly divided by sex at birth, with 50% males and 50% females. At Wave 5, participants were 33–43 years old, and at Wave 6, they were 39–51 years old.

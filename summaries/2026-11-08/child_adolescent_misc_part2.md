### The Cognitive Functions of Youth With Obesity: A Meta-Analysis
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Brain Behav *(IF: 14.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מטה-אנליזה | **מחברים:** Lin YC et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov

ABSTRACT Background This meta‐analysis examined the associations between obesity and performance across seven cognitive domains (inhibition, cognitive flexibility, working memory, verbal fluency, planning, attention, and intelligence quotient [IQ]) in youth under 18 years. Developmental stage (school‐aged children vs. adolescents) and sample sex composition examined as moderators. Evidence on executive function in youth with comorbid obesity and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) was synthesized qualitatively. Methods Following PRISMA guidelines, 22 studies comparing youth with obesity to normal‐weight peers were included. Effect sizes (Hedges’ g ) were pooled using a random‐effects model. Subgroup analyses compared school‐aged children and adolescents; meta‐regression examined age and proportion of female participants as study‐level moderators. Results Youth with obesity performed significantly worse than normal‐weight peers on inhibition ( g + = −0.193), cognitive flexibility ( g + = −0.499), working memory ( g + = −0.652), verbal fluency ( g + = −0.420), attention ( g + = −0.526), and IQ ( g + = −0.288); planning could not be estimated. School‐aged children showed deficits on inhibition, working memory, and attention; adolescents on working memory and attention only. Between‐subgroup tests were non‐significant for all domains ( p ≥ 0.26), so developmental differences should be interpreted with caution. Meta‐regression showed that a higher proportion of female participants in a study sample, a study‐level characteristic rather than an individual‐level effect, was associated with larger working memory deficits ( p = 0.002); age did not moderate any domain. Two ADHD‐comorbidity studies received qualitative synthesis only. Discussion Obesity is associated with poorer cognitive performance in youth across several domains, with sex composition of the study sample emerging as a moderator of the working memory finding; differences between school‐aged children and adolescents were not significant and should be interpreted cautiously. Substantial heterogeneity, predominantly observational designs, inconsistent BMI criteria, and limited ADHD‐stratified data constrain causal inference. Future studies should standardize weight definitions, report age‐ and ADHD‐stratified data, and consider bidirectional pathways.

1 Introduction Childhood and adolescent obesity has become an increasingly important global health concern. According to the World Health Organization, more than 390 million children and adolescents aged 5–19 years were overweight in 2022, and within this age group the prevalence of overweight increased from 8% in 1990 to 20% in 2022, while obesity rose from 2% to 8% over the same period (World Health Organization 2024 ). Overweight and obesity among young people have increased across both developed and developing countries (Ng et al. 2014 ), and are associated with elevated rates of hypertension, flatfoot, anxiety, and depression in children and adolescents (Zhang et al. 2024 ). Beyond these physical and psychological consequences, obesity may also be related to how children and adolescents think, regulate their behavior, and control their impulses. Executive functions such as inhibition, working memory, and cognitive flexibility support learning, classroom behavior, decision‐making, and everyday self‐control, and are considered foundational to healthy development (Diamond 2013 ); attention follows a partly distinct developmental course governed by alerting, orienting, and executive‐attention networks (Posner and Petersen 1990 ), and general intellectual ability (IQ) draws on this same prefrontal‐dependent architecture while also indexing broader academic and occupational outcomes (Nisbett et al. 2012 ). Several distinct mechanisms could plausibly link obesity to weaker performance across these domains: systemic low‐grade inflammation associated with excess adiposity (Samoilova et al. 2024 ), sleep‐disordered breathing and other sleep disturbances common in pediatric obesity (Pearce, Mackey, et al. 2018 ), structural and functional differences in prefrontal and reward‐related circuitry (Moreno‐López et al. 2012 ; Ronan et al. 2020 ), shared genetic liability affecting dopaminergic reward and self‐regulatory systems (Herzog et al. 2024 ), and shared environmental disadvantage, such as socioeconomic deprivation that affects both diet and cognitive stimulation (Li et al. 2023 ). Because prefrontal‐dependent cognitive systems are still maturing across childhood and adolescence, this developmental window is also when obesity‐prevention and cognitive‐support interventions may have the greatest long‐term benefit—the central stake motivating the present synthesis. Despite this rationale, the empirical literature linking obesity to cognitive performance in youth has already been synthesized several times, with converging but incomplete conclusions. Liang et al. ( 2014 ), Reinert et al. ( 2013 ), Yang et al. ( 2018 ), and Favieri et al. ( 2019 ) each report small‐to‐moderate obesity‐related deficits concentrated in inhibition and working memory, alongside considerable inconsistency across primary studies and domains; of these, only Yang et al. ( 2018 ) conducted a formal meta‐analysis, while the others synthesized the evidence narratively. Most recently, Ásgeirsdóttir et al. ( 2026 ) conducted a preregistered meta‐analysis restricted to adolescents with obesity and found a small, domain‐general executive‐function deficit ( g = −0.314) that did not differ significantly across specific domains, with age, BMI range, and the proportion of female participants in each sample moderating effect size. These reviews leave gaps that motivate the present synthesis: most combine overweight and obesity into a single category, obscuring any dose‐related pattern; none directly contrasts school‐aged children and adolescents within a single set of analyses; and, with the partial exception of Ásgeirsdóttir et al. ( 2026 ), none extends beyond core executive‐function domains to attention and IQ. Primary studies further illustrate why pooling, rather than additional narrative review, is needed: significant deficits associated with obesity have been reported for inhibition (Blanco‐Gómez et al. 2015 ; Kamijo et al. 2014 ; Kittel et al. 2017 ), cognitive flexibility (Alatorre‐Cruz et al. 2021 ; Blanco‐Gómez et al. 2015 ; Bozkurt et al. 2017 ; Maayan et al. 2011 ; Özer et al. 2024 ; Yau et al. 2014 ), working memory (Alatorre‐Cruz et al. 2021 ; Bozkurt et al. 2017 ; Kamijo, Khan, et al. 2012 ; Maayan et al. 2011 ; Özer et al. 2024 ), verbal fluency (Maayan et al. 2011 ), planning (Qavam et al. 2015 ), attention (Bozkurt et al. 2017 ; Chojnacki et al. 2018 ; Fields et al. 2013 ; Kittel et al. 2017 ; Logan et al. 2021 ; Maayan et al. 2011 ; Meo et al. 2019 ; Özer et al. 2024 ; Parisi et al. 2010 ), and IQ (Krombholz 2013 ; Maayan et al. 2011 ; Pearce, Mackey, et al. 2018 )—while other studies of the very same domains report no significant group differences: inhibition (Kamijo, Pontifex, et al. 2012 ), cognitive flexibility (Meo et al. 2019 ), working memory (Bauer and Manning 2016 ; Goldschmidt et al. 2018 ), planning (Qavam et al. 2015 ), attention (Li et al. 2008 ; Moss et al. 2023 ), and IQ (Akın et al. 2017 ; Bozkurt et al. 2017 ; Fields et al. 2013 ; Kamijo, Khan, et al. 2012 ; Logan et al. 2021 ; Parisi et al. 2010 ; Yau et al. 2014 ). ### School-Based Interventions for Children with Behavioral Difficulties: Inconsistent Implementation of Evidence-Based Practices
**כתב עת:** 📄 Evid Based Pract Child Adolesc Ment Health *(IF: 3.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Steenweg EM et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

BACKGROUND: Children with behavioral difficulties, including those with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and their teachers frequently experience challenges at school, leading to school-based interventions.

OBJECTIVE: This exploratory study evaluated the utilization and evidence-base of school-based interventions for behavioral difficulties among school mental health professionals in Dutch schools.

METHOD: A survey was conducted among 102 school mental health professionals to assess their utilization of school-based interventions, attitudes toward evidence-based practice, knowledge about behavioral problems, and educational background. The evidence-base of reported interventions was evaluated, and associations of professionals' attitudes, knowledge, and educational background with the use of evidence-based interventions were analyzed. Interventions were identified based on a previous study of regularly used interventions in Dutch schools.

RESULTS: Large heterogeneity in evidence-based practice was found: 18.6% of school mental health professionals only used evidence-based interventions, while 38.2% used at least one evidence-based intervention alongside non-evidence-based ones. However, 26.5% used interventions without sufficient empirical support, and 16.7% indicated to not use any intervention for children with behavioral difficulties at all. About half of the currently used lack empirical support and evidence-based interventions specifically developed for behavioral difficulties were rarely used. No predictors were significantly associated with the use of evidence-based interventions.

CONCLUSIONS: These findings underscore the need for a better implementation of effective school-based interventions for children with behavioral difficulties. A multifaceted approach is required, including research into promising interventions, deimplementation of interventions that lack sufficient evidence, and enhanced support for implementing evidence-based interventions in schools.

### Identification and multidimensional characterization of two distinct autism spectrum disorder subtypes
**כתב עת:** 📄 Transl Psychiatry | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Zhang H et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Aug 7

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) shows high variability in brain structure and behavior, complicating identification of biologically meaningful subtypes and their neuroanatomical and molecular bases. To address this, we analyzed structural MRI data from 1055 ASD and 1164 typically developing (TD) individuals. Gray matter volume (GMV) values from regions showing significant ASD–TD differences were extracted in ASD participants and used for supervised affinity propagation (AP) clustering. Subtypes were then characterized via individual differential structural covariance networks (IDSCN), followed by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) based on spatial transcriptomic data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) to identify molecular pathways associated with subtype-specific spatial GMV patterns. This integrative analysis identified two ASD subtypes with distinct neuroanatomical and molecular features. Subtype 1 showed widespread GMV reductions in social, limbic, and salience networks, reduced structural covariance, and enrichment in genes linked to neurodevelopment, neuronal differentiation, and synaptic organization. In contrast, Subtype 2 exhibited GMV increases in similar regions, stronger covariance, and enrichment in pathways involving cytoplasmic translation, mitochondrial organization, and synaptic signaling. These structural and molecular differences were also associated with variations in clinical symptom severity. Our findings reveal two ASD subtypes with distinct cortical morphometry, network patterns, and associated molecular signatures, emphasizing multiple neurodevelopmental paths and the value of biologically informed subtyping for precise diagnosis and personalized treatment.

Introduction Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by challenges in social communication and interaction, as well as the presence of restricted and repetitive patterns of behaviors, interests, or activities [ 1 , 2 ]. Moreover, the diverse genetic, environmental, and neurodevelopmental origins of ASD give rise to distinct subgroups and developmental patterns, leading to considerable variability in behavioral manifestations and communication abilities [ 3 ]. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) recognizes ASD’s phenotypic heterogeneity, and mounting evidence supports variability at neuroimaging and genetic levels as well [ 4 ]. ASD is increasingly understood as a highly heterogeneous neurodevelopmental condition, characterized by substantial inter-individual variability in both behavioral profiles and underlying neurobiology [ 5 , 6 ]. Neuroimaging studies have identified widespread, though variable, differences in brain structure particularly in gray matter among individuals on the ASD spectrum. Lenroot and Yeung reviewed early neuroimaging research that sought to characterize subgroups within the ASD spectrum based on individual differences in behavior and brain organization. They emphasized the value of dimensional approaches for capturing the diversity of autistic neurobiology beyond traditional diagnostic categories [ 7 ]. Halliday et al. conducted a cross-modality review encompassing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), electroencephalography (EEG), functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), and other techniques. They concluded that although modality-specific inconsistencies exist, several brain regions including the anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, thalamus, and the default mode network (DMN) consistently exhibit differences across methods. These findings highlight both converging patterns and the neurobiological diversity observed across individuals on the ASD spectrum [ 8 ]. Mahajan and Mostofsky proposed that ASD spectrum traits may reflect multiple neurobiological profiles, each associated with different patterns of brain morphology and connectivity, potentially contributing to the observed diversity in individual experiences and responses to intervention [ 9 ]. These findings support the view that ASD encompasses a range of neurodevelopmental dimensions rather than representing a single underlying biological profile. To better understand the diversity within ASD, recent studies have adopted data-driven approaches to identify GM-based neurobiological subtypes. For instance, Chen et al. applied voxel-based morphometry and clustering techniques to group male participants on the ASD spectrum into three neuroanatomically distinct subtypes, each associated with differing behavioral characteristics and patterns of functional connectivity [ 10 ]. Similarly, Xu et al. utilized single-subject GM networks and graph-theoretical metrics to identify three subtypes with different topological organization and cognitive profiles [ 11 ]. Liu et al. employed a semi-supervised clustering framework to delineate two GM-based subtypes based on volumetric features derived from GM, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid [ 12 ]. In another study, Shan et al. used normative modeling and non-negative matrix factorization to identify three GM-based subtypes, each characterized by unique structural patterns and behavioral traits related to social communication [ 13 ]. Extending this work, Guo et al. introduced individual differential structural covariance networks (IDSCNs) to explore within-spectrum variability, identifying two subtypes with divergent frontal and subcortical covariance patterns associated with differences in repetitive behaviors [ 14 ]. Building on these findings, Zhang et al. further examined the reproducibility and robustness of IDSCN-based subtyping, supporting its potential utility in capturing meaningful neurobiological distinctions [ 15 ]. However, despite these advances, several limitations persist. First, most existing studies employ unsupervised clustering methods that require arbitrary pre-specification of the number of clusters, which may obscure latent heterogeneity in the data given the unknown true number of ASD subtypes. Second, while recent subtyping efforts have explored structural features such as voxel-level gray matter volume (GMV) and structural covariance, these modalities are often used in isolation, potentially limiting their ability to capture multidimensional neurobiological differences. For instance, although the individual differential structural covariance network has been used to identify ASD subtypes, its application as a primary clustering feature may overemphasize fine-grained covariance patterns while neglecting global volumetric variation. Nonetheless, IDSCN remains a valuable complementary tool for characterizing subtype-specific network features after subtype identification, offering validation of structural distinctiveness at the network level. Moreover, few studies have leveraged molecular or genetic data to contextualize neuroanatomical subtypes. To overcome these limitations, we propose an integrative subtyping framework that combines supervised machine learning and multimodal neuroimaging features. Specifically, we applied a supervised Affinity Propagation (AP) algorithm to GMV difference maps between ASD individuals and typical developing, enabling subtype discovery without the need to preset the number of clusters. This approach yielded two robust subtypes: Subtype 1, characterized by widespread GMV reductions in brain regions involved in social cognition, sensorimotor processing, limbic regulation, and salience detection, and Subtype 2, which exhibited GMV increases in the same networks. Beyond anatomical profiles, we further examined the IDSCN patterns within each subtype, revealing distinct structural covariance signatures aligned with symptom severity. Finally, to explore the molecular basis of these subtypes, we conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), which revealed different biological pathway enrichment patterns in Subtype 1 and Subtype 2. ### Unveiling determinants and system optimization: a CFIR-ToC integrated approach to rehabilitation service utilization for children with autism spectrum disorder

Additionally, it intends to construct multi-stakeholder action pathways and networks in combination with the Theory of Change, thereby forming an operational rehabilitation support system. The ultimate goal is to enhance the health equity and overall health status of autistic children and their families. Methods A two-phase mixed approach was used: (1) CFIR-based semi-structured interviews and qualitative analysis with structured coding (alphabets for facilitators, numerals for barriers), following COREQ guidelines; (2) ToC-guided translation of CFIR-identified facilitators into stakeholder responsibilities (matched with barriers) to construct a support system with multi-stakeholder pathways/networks. Results 37 caregivers (30 service-engaged, 7 non-engaged) were interviewed, revealing 9/15 facilitators/barriers for engaged families and 2/9 for non-engaged ones. Universal barriers include high costs, geographical resource gaps, and insufficient caregiver skills. An evidence-based pathway clarifies stakeholder responsibilities to target barriers; a multi-level network defines collaboration among Core, Intermediate, and Collaborative tiers. Conclusions This study extensively analyzed the barriers and facilitators to rehabilitation service utilization among both service-engaged and non-engaged families of autistic children, addressing the research gaps of incomplete identification of influencing factors and limited representativeness of study populations. It constructed an evidence-based, multi-component action pathway involving multiple stakeholders and a multi-level, multi-stakeholders collaborative network, thereby forming an operational rehabilitation support system. This system provides a reference for global efforts to improve the accessibility, acceptability, geographic accessibility, and affordability of autism services, contributing to enhanced family well-being, improved social adaptability, and better health outcomes. Such a full-chain evidence-based pathway, spanning from problem analysis to solution formulation, offers an analytical framework for advancing health equity and overall health status among vulnerable populations. Background Ensuring early childhood development and equal resource access for persons with disabilities are enshrined in United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) Targets 4.2 and 4.5. Children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), embodying the dual identities of “disabled” and “child,” make advancing their overall health and health equity a shared entry point and priority for achieving these strategic goals. ASD represents a pressing global health challenge. It was estimated that around 1 in 127 individuals worldwide lived with autism in 2021 [ 1 ] imposing substantial socioeconomic burdens on families and societies. Lifetime supportive care costs amount to $1.4 million in both the United States and the United Kingdom for autistic individuals without intellectual disability; for those with co-occurring intellectual disability, the lifetime cost rises to $2.4 million in the US and £1.5 million (equivalent to $2.2 million) in the UK [ 2 ].Globally, autistic disorder accounts for over 58 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) per 100,000 population, while other autism spectrum disorders contribute 53 DALYs per 100,000 population [ 3 ]. Therefore, identifying the barriers and facilitators of rehabilitation service utilization and developing actionable pathways based on these determinants to promote effective service utilization is key to reducing familial and societal burdens. Although previous studies have identified multiple factors associated with rehabilitation service utilization among children with ASD, they have largely focused on sociodemographic factors, such as children’s gender [ 4 ], ethnicity [ 5 ], parental socioeconomic status [ 6 ], and educational pathways [ 7 ]. Meanwhile, inherent constraints of rehabilitation service delivery systems, including limited service capacity, workforce shortages, prolonged waiting times, and variable professional qualifications and communication quality, also affect families’ ability and willingness to access services [ 8 – 11 ]. However, procedural and contextual factors—including families’ recognition of rehabilitation needs, care-seeking decision-making, intra-family coordination, and communication with rehabilitation institutions—have received comparatively limited research attention. More importantly, research has predominantly focused on families whose children have already accessed rehabilitation or autism-related services. For example, previous studies have explored parents’ experiences with family-centered services in Ontario [ 12 ] and the service-access experiences of parents whose children had received autism care services during the preceding year [ 13 ]. Families whose children have never received rehabilitation services remain underrepresented in the literature, and their barriers to care-seeking, decision-making processes, unmet needs, and actual service expectations are insufficiently understood. This gap highlights the need for a stronger health-equity perspective, as families excluded from or unable to enter the service system may experience greater structural and social disadvantages. In addition, although prior research has recognized the roles of parents [ 14 – 16 ], community and non-governmental organizations [ 17 – 22 ], governments [ 23 – 25 ], healthcare institutions [ 26 , 27 ], educational systems [ 28 ], and cross-sectoral collaboration [ 29 , 30 ], substantial gaps remain regarding implementation pathways. ### Cardiovascular effects of methylphenidate, risperidone, and their combination in children and adolescents with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder: a prospective 3-month observational study
**כתב עת:** 📄 BMC Pediatr | **סוג מחקר:** מחקר תצפיתי | **מחברים:** Oveisi S et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Aug 6

Background We aimed to evaluate changes in hemodynamic parameters and electrocardiographic (ECG) indices related to ventricular arrhythmia risk after three months of treatment with methylphenidate (MPH), risperidone (RIS), or their combination in children and adolescents with ADHD. Methods A total of 150 children and adolescents with DSM-5-diagnosed ADHD were prospectively enrolled at Qods Hospital. Participants received methylphenidate ( n = 50), risperidone ( n = 50), or combination therapy ( n = 50). Cardiovascular outcomes were assessed using age- and weight-adjusted two-way repeated-measures ANCOVA to evaluate changes over time and differences among treatment groups. Results Significant group × time interactions were observed for systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, and QTc interval after adjustment for baseline age and weight. After three months, systolic and diastolic blood pressure increased in the methylphenidate and combination therapy groups, while heart rate showed an overall increase, with the greatest rise observed in the methylphenidate group. QTc interval increased over time across all treatment groups, with the largest prolongation observed in the combination therapy group (377.9 ± 25.4 to 424.5 ± 24.9 ms). However, mean QTc values remained below commonly accepted thresholds for clinically significant QT prolongation, and no significant longitudinal changes were observed in QRS duration or PR intervals. Conclusion Treatment with methylphenidate, risperidone, and combination therapy was associated with modest changes in heart rate, blood pressure, and QTc interval over three months. These findings highlight the importance of baseline and follow-up cardiovascular monitoring in children and adolescents receiving ADHD medications.

Introduction Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is one of the most common neurodevelopmental disorders in childhood. A worldwide systematic review and meta-regression analysis estimated the pooled prevalence of ADHD among children and adolescents at approximately 5.3%, highlighting its substantial public health burden [ 1 ]. ADHD is characterized by developmentally inappropriate and impairing levels of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interfere with daily functioning and social, academic, and occupational performance [ 2 ]. Diagnostic classifications for ADHD have evolved over time, with differences between major international systems. In the 11th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), the World Health Organization adopted the term “attention deficit hyperactivity disorder” (ADHD), replacing the previous terminology of “hyperkinetic disorder” used in earlier ICD classifications [ 3 ]. Although ADHD diagnostic criteria in ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR are largely comparable, some differences exist regarding symptom thresholds, clinical presentations, and diagnostic requirements [ 4 ]. These diagnostic frameworks describe overlapping clinical constructs, while ADHD remains the preferred terminology in contemporary clinical practice [ 4 ]. The use of pharmacological treatments for ADHD has increased substantially worldwide over recent decades, with notable rises in medication prescribing among both children and adults [ 5 ]. Although randomized clinical trials and observational studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of ADHD medications in reducing core symptoms and improving functional outcomes, concerns regarding their long-term safety, particularly potential cardiovascular effects, remain an important clinical consideration [ 6 – 8 ]. Pharmacological treatment of ADHD includes both stimulant and non-stimulant medications. According to current clinical guidelines, stimulant medications, primarily methylphenidate- and amphetamine-based preparations, are considered first-line pharmacological options for many children and adolescents with ADHD. Non-stimulant medications, including atomoxetine, guanfacine, and clonidine, represent alternative treatment options, particularly for patients with inadequate response to stimulants or those experiencing adverse effects [ 8 , 9 ]. Methylphenidate (MPH), a central nervous system stimulant, is one of the most commonly used medications for ADHD, whereas risperidone (RIS), an atypical antipsychotic, is frequently prescribed for children and adolescents with ADHD-associated behavioral disorders. These medications may be administered as monotherapy or combination therapy when clinically indicated. Although effective, they may be associated with neurological, metabolic, and cardiovascular adverse effects [ 7 – 9 ]. Cardiovascular effects are of particular clinical importance in pediatric populations because stimulants and antipsychotics may influence heart rate, blood pressure, and cardiac electrophysiological parameters. Previous studies have reported alterations in ECG parameters, including QT and corrected QT (QTc) interval prolongation, which may increase the risk of ventricular arrhythmias in susceptible individuals [ 10 – 14 ]. Therefore, careful assessment of ventricular repolarization parameters is essential, particularly in children receiving combined therapy. The present study was designed to investigate changes in ECG parameters and hemodynamic variables among patients with ADHD receiving MPH, RIS, or combination therapy. As this was an observational study conducted in routine clinical practice, standardized rating scales such as the Conners Rating Scale were not routinely used, and formal assessments of ADHD severity were not performed.

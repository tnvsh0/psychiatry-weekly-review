### Childhood maltreatment and defenses: A meta-analytic review
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Clinical Psychology Review (Clin Psychol Rev) *(IF: 12.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Tracchegiani J et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 2

Defenses are theorized to develop within early caregiving relationships, yet no meta-analysis has quantified their association with childhood maltreatment. This PRISMA 2020-guided meta-analysis, which was not preregistered, addressed this gap for Overall Defensive Functioning (ODF) and immature, neurotic, and mature defenses. We searched PsycINFO, PubMed, Web of Science, and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global through August 20, 2026. Eligible studies quantified maltreatment before age 18 and ODF or defense categories, excluding studies using only dichotomous maltreatment indicators or broad trauma inventories underrepresenting maltreatment. We rated study quality with an adapted NIH Quality Assessment Tool. Random-effects models pooled Fisher's z, back-transformed to r; meta-regression and subgroup analyses tested moderators. We included 35 cross-sectional studies (34 independent samples; N = 13,604). Greater maltreatment was associated with lower ODF (k = 7; r = -.17, 95% CI [-.20, -.13]) and greater reliance on immature (k = 30; r = .24, 95% CI [.18, .29]) and neurotic defenses (k = 21; r = .12, 95% CI [.06, .19]), whereas the mature-defense association was negligible (k = 24; r = -.04, 95% CI [-.09, .01]). Heterogeneity was substantial for defense categories but not ODF; publication bias was not evident. Exploratory moderator analyses suggested variation by maltreatment instrument and sex composition (immature), sample type (neurotic), and defense instrument (mature). Cross-sectional, retrospective evidence precludes causal inference. Findings are consistent with psychodynamic and developmental models in which childhood maltreatment shifts defensive organization toward less adaptive modes without precluding mature defenses; prospective longitudinal studies should test this proposition.

### Do adolescent, parent, and dyadic behaviors during the family meal predict response to family-based treatment for anorexia nervosa?
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Behaviour Research and Therapy (Behav Res Ther) *(IF: 5.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Singh S et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 30

OBJECTIVE: In family-based treatment (FBT) for anorexia nervosa, patient and caregiver behaviors during the family meal (FM) predict treatment outcomes. Intensive parental coaching (IPC) includes a second FM, yet whether behaviors during IPC predict end-of-treatment (EOT) outcomes is unknown.

METHODS: Secondary data from a randomized controlled FBT trial (N = 83) were analyzed. Adolescents without early response (+2.4 kg by session four) were randomized to FBT-as-usual or IPC. Videotaped FM sessions were behavior coded. Logistic regressions tested, in the whole sample, whether FM1 behaviors predicted early response. Linear regressions tested whether FM behaviors predicted treatment outcomes at EOT among responders (n = 27) and nonresponders randomized to IPC (n = 17) separately. Lag sequential analyses explored, in the whole sample, whether dyadic transitions during FM1 predict early response.

RESULTS: During FM1, greater adolescent restriction (OR = 1.06) and negative affect (OR = 1.10) predicted nonresponse, whereas parental modeling eating predicted early response (OR = 0.97). Among responders, adolescent food approach (f 2  = 0.19) and meal arrangement (f 2  = 0.20) predicted psychological outcomes. Among IPC nonresponders, changes in parent meal preparation showed the largest associations with psychological outcomes (f 2  = 0.95), while changes in adolescent meal arrangement showed the largest associations with both psychological (f 2  = 0.33) and weight outcomes (f 2  = 0.20). Adolescent not eating followed by parent eating was the only dyadic transition predicting early response (OR = 0.20); most other behaviors and transitions either exhibited small effects or were not associated with outcomes.

DISCUSSION: FM behaviors may provide clinically relevant information about FBT and IPC response and potential intervention targets. However, given our modest sample size, conclusions remain tentative.

### Enhancing Empathic Functioning in Married Individuals Experiencing Marital Conflict Through Mentalization-Based Therapy in Iran
**כתב עת:** 📄 J Marital Fam Ther | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Mousapour Z et al. | **תאריך:** 2027 Jan

Empathy impairment may contribute to the persistence of marital conflict. Although mentalization is theoretically relevant to understanding mental states and interpersonal functioning, limited research has examined whether Mentalization-Based Therapy (MBT) is associated with changes in empathy among married individuals experiencing marital conflict. This study examined the effect of MBT on self-reported empathy using a pretest-posttest control-group design. Thirty married individuals experiencing marital conflict were randomly allocated to an experimental group or a control group. The experimental group received 12 weekly 45-min MBT sessions. Empathy was assessed using the 28-item Davis Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) and analyzed using analysis of covariance. After controlling for pretest empathy, the experimental group showed significantly higher posttest empathy than the control group, F(1, 27) = 12.643, p = 0.001, partial η 2  = 0.319. The findings provide preliminary evidence that participation in MBT may be associated with improved self-reported empathy in this population.

### Virtual reality-supported mindfulness practice in relieving stress in postgraduate students: a pilot randomised controlled trial
**כתב עת:** 📄 BMC Psychol | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Qi Y et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Aug 5

Background Virtual reality (VR)-based mindfulness intervention may be a potentially effective approach for stress relief in postgraduate students. This pilot study examined the preliminary effects of a VR-based mindfulness intervention on stress reduction among postgraduate students, in comparison with a conventional mindfulness intervention and a control condition. In addition, the study evaluated the feasibility, acceptability, and user experience associated with delivering mindfulness through VR technology. Methods A three-arm, assessor-blinded pilot randomised controlled trial was conducted. Forty-five full-time postgraduate students were randomly assigned ( n = 15 per group) to a mindfulness group (15-minute audio-guided mindful practice twice a week for two weeks), a VR mindfulness group (VR-based mindfulness intervention twice weekly for two weeks), or a control group. The feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary effects of VR-based mindfulness intervention on relieving stress were examined. Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted with participants in the VR mindfulness group to explore their experiences following the intervention. Results This pilot study supported the feasibility of VR-based mindfulness intervention with satisfactory recruitment (54.88%), attrition (4.44%), and intervention completion (95.56%) rates. Participants in both the VR-based mindfulness group ( β = -0.422, 95%CI: -0.804 to -0.039, p = 0.031, g = -0.42) and the mindfulness group ( β = -0.563, 95%CI: -1.052 to -0.074, p = 0.024, g = -0.44) exhibited significant reductions in perceived stress scores compared to the control group at post-intervention. Additionally, significant reductions on total mood disturbance and negative mood were observed in both VR-based mindfulness group ( β = -16.553, 95%CI: -28.800 to -4.306, p = 0.008, g = -0.52; β = -22.159, 95%CI: -40.827 to -3.491, p = 0.020, g = -0.52) and mindfulness groups ( β = -16.004, 95%CI: -30.500 to -1.509, p = 0.030, g = -0.59; β = -23.029, 95%CI: -45.307 to -0.752, p = 0.043, g = -0.53). However, no statistically significant differences between groups were found in the total scores of mindfulness ( p > 0.05). Conclusion This pilot study demonstrated the feasibility and potentially preliminary effectiveness of VR-based mindfulness intervention for postgraduate students. Further multi-centre randomised controlled trials with a larger sample size and longer follow-up are recommended to ascertain the effects of VR-based mindfulness intervention for stress relief among postgraduate students. Trial registration This study has been registered in ClinicalTrials.gov ( NCT05948280 ) on 17th July 2023.

Introduction In the pursuit of academic and personal goals, university students are continuously evaluated by their institutions, which exposes them to various stressors [ 1 ]. Postgraduate students, in comparison, experience even higher levels of stress, with prevalence significantly higher than undergraduates [ 2 ]. Common sources of stress for postgraduate students include schoolwork, financial pressures, research assistant or teaching assistant responsibilities, career planning, and family issues [ 3 , 4 ]. Furthermore, as postgraduates tend to pay more attention to academic performance rather than integrating into campus life, they may have limited access to psychological support through campus activities and organisations, which negatively affects their mental health and stress levels [ 2 ]. Excessive stress levels not only compromise students’ overall well-being, such as increasing their risk of illness, poor sleep quality, eating disorders, increased healthcare utilisation, and depression, but also adversely affect academic performance, including higher dropout rates, reducing academic achievement and social functioning, and even suicidal ideation [ 5 ]. Mindfulness can benefit one’s emotions in a ‘non-judgement’ way, which is achieved by adjusting one’s breathing, training oneself to be ‘present at the moment’, and adopting attention-focused mindfulness practices to reduce ruminations (i.e., thinking about negative things repeatedly) and negative emotion [ 6 ]. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown that mindfulness-based programmes, including mindfulness-based stress reduction, are associated with reductions in psychological distress, perceived stress, and other mental health-related outcomes in non-clinical adult populations [ 7 ]. Evidence from undergraduate and graduate student populations also suggests that stress-reduction interventions, including mindfulness-based approaches, may be beneficial for reducing stress [ 8 ]. Given the substantial academic demands and limited time availability among postgraduate students, brief mindfulness formats may offer a particularly feasible and practical approach for stress management in this population [ 9 ]. As an emotion-regulation skill, mindfulness can be further enhanced through virtual reality (VR) technology. VR uses a headset with a display that projects an interactive, audiovisual 360-degree virtual environment [ 10 ]. VR enables users to generate vivid environments that can also be manipulated on demand, providing a sense of ‘being there’ during the process [ 11 ]. Immersive VR provides aesthetic environments, allowing users to be virtually present in a personalised setting [ 12 ], while audio guidance replaces the role of the trainer traditionally performed in mindfulness training, ensuring consistency in the quality of training. The interaction between environment and training enhanced participants’ feelings of involvement and the efficacy of meditation [ 13 ]. Consequently, VR-based mindfulness intervention may offer advantages over traditional approaches by enhancing present-moment awareness and minimising external distractions. In recent years, a combination of mindfulness and/or meditation training with VR has been promoted. Studies have examined the feasibility of incorporating VR mindfulness training, whereas participants found that VR-based mindfulness intervention improved calmness and relaxation and reduced stress [ 14 ]. Furthermore, several studies have concluded that VR demonstrated high acceptability for the practice of mindfulness and significantly promoted treatment adherence [ 14 – 18 ]. Notably, in the case of university students, the implementation of additional VR scenarios into the practice of mindfulness meditation is particularly well accepted because of their high affinity for new technologies [ 19 ]. Nevertheless, previous systematic reviews have pointed to limitations of VR-based mindfulness interventions, including poor video quality and the discomfort caused by the weight of VR headsets [ 12 ]. At present, little is known about how VR may facilitate or hinder mindfulness practice, or how specific design features of headsets (e.g. comfort, weight, and degree of immersion) may impact the perceived benefits of VR-supported mindfulness. Therefore, addressing these gaps is essential for informing intervention design and ensuring the acceptability of future mental health interventions in postgraduate populations. The current pilot study aims to test the feasibility and acceptability of a VR-based mindfulness intervention in postgraduate students. We also aim to test the preliminary effects of VR-based mindfulness practice in improving stress in postgraduate students as compared to conventional mindfulness and control. Additionally, the experience of using VR for mindfulness was also explored in this population. ### Effects of digital stress-management interventions in breast cancer patients 18 months after intervention access
**כתב עת:** 📄 Breast Cancer Res Treat | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Svendsen K et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 4

Purpose Digital stress-management tools show promise, but their long-term impact remains unclear. In the Coping After Breast Cancer (CABC) trial, the app-based stress-management program StressProffen , with either cognitive behavioral therapy intervention (CBI) or a mindfulness-based intervention (MBI), showed no advantage over usual care after six months of access. However, benefits may emerge gradually, as users need time not only to become familiar with the content and techniques, but also to internalize, apply and automatize their use in response to real-life stressors. The a priori defined aims of the current study were to examine; 1) whether extended (18 months) access to the digital stress-management interventions CBI and MBI were associated with changes in perceived stress (stress) (primary outcome), measures of health-related quality of life, mindfulness, fatigue, coping, anxiety, depression, and sleep (secondary outcomes), 2) association between app use and changes in stress level and investigate baseline characteristics as moderators of change in stress in the intervention groups, and 3) identify predictors of change in stress among patients with breast cancer. Methods Of 430 patients randomized to MBI, CBI, or usual care, 76% ( n = 327) completed the 18-month survey. Linear mixed-effects models were used to assess between-group differences in primary and secondary outcomes. Moderator analyses were performed to examine whether specific baseline characteristics modified the intervention effects on stress levels. Predictor analysis of change in stress was performed for the total sample. Results At 18 months, all three groups showed stable trends over time, with no significant difference between either intervention group and controls. A larger stress reduction was observed among younger participants (≤50 years) in both CBI and MBI compared to controls. Higher baseline stress levels predicted significantly larger stress reductions in the total sample, but the moderator analysis showed that the effects were only significant in the CBI group. Conclusion Neither CBI nor MBI showed statistically significant improvements at 18 months compared to controls. The results however suggest that long-term access to stress management interventions may help young women with breast cancer and those with high initial stress to reduce their stress levels. Clinicaltrials ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04480203 . Supplementary Information The online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10549-026-08117-x .

Background A breast cancer diagnosis can be stressful, bringing worry and uncertainty about the disease, treatment, prognosis and risk of recurrence. These factors can affect the ability to cope with stress [ 1 ] as well as physical pain, and may influence psychological well-being and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [ 2 – 4 ]. Individual differences in perceived stress and stress responses among breast cancer patients may depend on the appraisal of challenges (stressors) and expectations regarding outcomes (response outcome expectancies). When patients believe they can cope with stressors, the stress response may be reduced. Conversely, feelings of helplessness or hopelessness may sustain the stress response [ 1 , 5 ]. Psychosocial stress-management interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and mindfulness-based interventions [ 6 , 7 ] have been associated with reduced cancer-related challenges such as stress, anxiety, depression, fatigue, insomnia, and pain, as well as improved HRQoL [ 8 – 17 ]. However, these interventions are typically delivered in person either individually or in groups over multiple sessions, and are not always readily accessible. Consequently, research on digital psychosocial stress-management interventions, which may improve access, is increasing [ 18 – 23 ]. Compared with care-as-usual controls, the original digital stress-management intervention StressProffen TM , with mainly CBT content and aspects of mindfulness, found larger effects among cancer survivors at 6 months compared to 3 months, and these enhanced effects appeared to persist after 12 months of intervention access [ 24 ]. The original StressProffen intervention was later adapted into two distinct versions: a cognitive behavioral intervention (CBI) and a mindfulness-based intervention (MBI) [ 25 ]. Both versions incorporated varying amounts of newly developed content, while retaining the original StressProffen program layout [ 25 ]. These two interventions were tested in women with breast cancer in the Coping After Breast Cancer (CABC) trial [ 25 – 27 ]. After 6-months of access to these interventions, no statistically significant effect on perceived stress level, HRQoL or other cancer-related side effects were found among breast cancer patients receiving either intervention compared to usual care controls [ 26 ]. Although engagement with all interventions often declines over time, benefits from stress-management programs may persist or, in some cases, become apparent after the intervention period if participants continue to apply acquired coping strategies [ 28 – 31 ]. In the original StressProffen trial, findings of reduced stress, symptoms of depression, self-regulatory fatigue and improved HRQoL also persisted after 12 months access [ 24 ]. Persistent effects on perceived stress level or HRQoL have also been shown for a few digital stress-management interventions in cancer patients [ 32 – 34 ]. In general, long-term follow-up beyond 6 months of use is important to evaluate whether intervention effects are sustained or emerge over time [ 10 ]. The current study hence hypothesized a priori that effects could be present at 18 months, even if they were minimal after 6 months. Additionally, several factors, including age and family status, may moderate the impact of psychosocial interventions [ 35 – 37 ] and should be specifically studied. The a priori objectives of the study were: Objective 1: To determine between-group (MBI, CBI and controls) effects on change in perceived stress level (primary outcome), measures of HRQoL, mindfulness, fatigue, coping, anxiety, depression and sleep (secondary outcomes) from baseline to 18 months after intervention access. Objective 2: To determine whether application (app) use (from baseline to 18 months) affected changes in perceived stress level, and whether baseline characteristics (age and living with children under 18 years [ 37 ], baseline stress levels [ 38 ], expectations on effect of stress-management intervention, response outcome expectancies (hopelessness, helplessness and coping)[1], breast cancer stage and educational level at baseline moderate percieved stress reduction at 18 months in the two intervention groups. Objective 3: To determine whether baseline characteristics, relationship status, body mass index (BMI), alcohol use, smoking and exercise assessed at baseline predict changes in percieved stress from baseline to 6 and 18 months in the total study sample. ### Effects of a jail-adapted dialectical behavioral therapy skills group on in-custody outcomes and reentry preparedness: A randomized controlled trial
**כתב עת:** 📄 Psychol Serv | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Verona E et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 2

People who are involved in the criminal-legal system often experience difficulty regulating emotions, refraining from impulsive behaviors, and communicating effectively. Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), particularly the skills training group component, may be well-suited to address the risks and needs of correctional populations, but adaptations of DBT in these settings have outpaced rigorous evidence for their effectiveness. This gap is especially notable in short-term jail detention settings, underscoring the need for large-scale randomized evaluations of DBT skills groups on both psychological outcomes and prerelease readiness among jail inmates. The current preregistered randomized controlled trial evaluated the effects of a jail-adapted DBT skills group on in-custody changes in coping, psychiatric symptoms, and jail misconduct and on reentry-related preparedness. Participants were 640 individuals (83% male, 69.2% White, 13.8% Hispanic) incarcerated in a county jail randomized to receive a 12-session DBT skills group or control treatment. They completed baseline and prerelease assessments of relevant outcomes. Small effects of DBT were observed for improved adaptive coping, decreased antisocial behavior in custody, and positive perceptions of reentry capabilities and support. Findings from the present study highlight the insights gleaned from examining effects of DBT group treatment in regard to both behavioral outcomes and expectations for reentry.

### Mobile Units for Integrated Addiction and HIV Prevention Services for People Who Inject Drugs: The HPTN 094 Randomized Clinical Trial
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Psychiatry *(IF: 25.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Shoptaw S et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 4

Key Points Question Does providing 26 weeks of integrated HIV prevention and addiction services for people who inject drugs using peer navigation delivered in a mobile unit produce superior outcomes compared to peer navigation to existing standard-of-care community-based services alone? Findings In this randomized clinical trial, HIV prevention and addiction outcomes were similar between study arms, though the intervention showed a greater reduction in all-cause mortality compared with standard of care. Meaning The findings suggest that integrated mobile service delivery models may reduce all-cause mortality for people who inject drugs.

This randomized clinical trial evaluates the use of preexposure prophylaxis for HIV and medications for opioid use disorder among individuals who inject drugs.

Importance Integrated models to deliver HIV preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD) for people who inject drugs are needed. Objective To determine whether integrated PrEP and MOUD services delivered on a mobile unit with manualized peer navigation improved PrEP and MOUD use compared with peer navigation to existing community-based services. Design, Setting, and Participants This 2-group, open-label, randomized clinical trial was conducted between May 2021 and September 2024 in Houston, Texas; Los Angeles, California; New York, New York; Philadelphia, Pennsylvania; and Washington, DC, among people aged 18 years and older currently injecting drugs, without HIV, with OUD, and not taking MOUD but willing to consider MOUD. Interventions The intervention arm (n = 203) consisted of 26 weeks of manualized peer navigation plus integrated PrEP, MOUD, and limited clinical and supportive services delivered on a mobile unit. The active control arm (n = 206) consisted of 26 weeks of manualized peer navigation to available community-based PrEP, MOUD, harm reduction, and supportive services. Main Outcomes and Measures The co–primary outcomes were PrEP use and MOUD use at 26 weeks. Secondary outcomes included PrEP and MOUD use at 52 weeks, HIV incidence, and all-cause mortality. Results Of 842 people who inject drugs who were screened, 409 were randomized: 134 (32.8%) female; 146 (11.2%) aged 30 years and younger vs older than 30 years. At the 26-week visit, the intervention and active control arms were similar for confirmed PrEP use: 11/203 (5.4%) vs 6/206 (2.9%) and MOUD use: 30/203 (14.8%) vs 22/206 (10.7%), respectively. At the 52-week visit, intervention and active control arms were similar for confirmed PrEP and MOUD: 4/203 (2.0%) vs 4/206 (1.9%) and MOUD use: 27/203 (13.3%) vs 29/206 (14.1%). One HIV acquisition occurred in the intervention and 2 in the active control arms; HIV incidence rate of 0.9 per 100 person-years (95% CI, 0.2-2.6). Four deaths were confirmed in the intervention arm and 13 in the active control arm (hazard ratio, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.1-0.9; P = .03). Conclusions and Relevance In this randomized clinical trial, mobile delivery of integrated HIV prevention, addiction, and navigation services did not improve primary outcomes beyond peer navigation alone. The significantly lower all-cause death rate at 52 weeks in the intervention arm bears replication for delivering integrated PrEP and MOUD services to people who inject drugs. Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04804072

Introduction Opioid use disorder (OUD) is a major driver of HIV acquisition in the US and a leading contributor to mortality among people who use opioids, whether alone or in combination with stimulants. Beyond HIV acquisition and overdose, people who inject drugs experience elevated risks of comorbid conditions, including hepatitis C, bacterial infections related to nonsterile injection practices, injection-related wounds, and other sexually transmitted infections. People who inject drugs face substantial barriers to accessing key services and support necessary for consistent engagement in HIV prevention and addiction treatment. These barriers include poverty, unstable housing, food insecurity, and limited access to health care, all of which impede regular use of HIV preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and medication for OUD (MOUD). Siloed medical and addiction treatment systems further restrict access by fragmenting care for primary health services, HIV PrEP, and MOUD. Mobile units offer a way to address multiple barriers faced by people who inject drugs in accessing integrated services, including HIV prevention, MOUD, primary care, and social services. Mobile unit-based care differs from care based in conventional brick-and-mortar settings for people who inject drugs along key features of location, integration, immediacy, and service proximity. Yet, rigorous evaluation of mobile unit-based interventions for people who inject drugs remains limited. This study tests whether advantages of integrated HIV and MOUD services using a mobile unit combined with peer navigation would produce statistically significant improvements in primary outcomes of PrEP and MOUD compared to peer navigation to existing standard of care, community-based services alone for people who inject drugs in 5 US cities. Methods Trial Design and Oversight HPTN 094 was a 2-arm, individually randomized, controlled, open-label study that evaluated whether an integrated intervention delivered in a mobile unit improved use of HIV PrEP and MOUD at 26 and 52 weeks ( Figure 1 ). The trial protocol and statistical analysis plan are in Supplement 1 . A single institutional review board, Advarra, provided oversight of the research. Biannual data monitoring oversight was provided by an independent study monitoring committee at the HPTN. All participants provided written informed consent. The study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials ( CONSORT ) reporting guideline. Participants Eligibility criteria included current injection drug use, being aged 18 years and older, the ability to provide informed consent, not currently using but willing to use MOUD, self-reported sharing injection equipment, or having condomless sex in the last 3 months with people with HIV or persons of unknown HIV serostatus, and being able and willing to provide adequate contact information of a person or persons who could help locate participants for follow-up visits. Participants were excluded from the study if they self-reported MOUD in the past 30 days as part of a treatment program or if they were enrolled in another interventional study. Although people with HIV were recruited and randomized, enrollment numbers were insufficient to support rigorous analysis in that group. Study Sites HPTN 094 was conducted in Houston, Texas; Los Angeles, California; New York, New York; Philadelphia, Pennsylvania; and Washington, DC. The cities represented diverse large urban contexts with substantial HIV and overdose risks among people who inject drugs. Study sites were selected based on prior experience conducting research on HIV prevention and addiction for people who inject drugs, on geographic diversity, and on HIV prevalence in people who inject drugs. Mobile Units Los Angeles, Houston, and New York chose identical 33-ft medical units that contained an examination table, space for processing laboratory samples, and a toilet; Philadelphia and Washington, DC, chose 26-ft, similarly equipped medical units. Data collection during screening, enrollment, week 26, and week 52 occurred in the mobile unit for all participants. ### Cost-Effectiveness of Early Dose Reduction or Discontinuation Versus Antipsychotic Maintenance After First-Episode Psychosis: Results From the HAMLETT Study
**כתב עת:** 📄 Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica (Acta Psychiatr Scand) | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Thielen FW et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 4

BACKGROUND: After remission from a first episode psychosis (FEP), international clinical guidelines recommend continuation of antipsychotic medication for at least 1 year, although many patients wish to reduce or discontinue medication due to side effects or long-term health concerns. Evidence on the cost-effectiveness of early dose reduction or discontinuation compared to continuation remains limited.

METHODS: A trial-based cost-utility analysis was conducted alongside the ongoing HAMLETT study, in which 347 remitted FEP participants were randomized to either early guided dose reduction or discontinuation or maintenance treatment for 1 year. Both healthcare and societal perspectives over a 24-month time horizon were considered. Health outcomes were expressed as quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) derived from the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire using Dutch valuation tariffs. Costs were estimated using patient reported healthcare utilization and administrative pharmacy data. Incremental costs and effects were analysed using seemingly unrelated regression with uncertainty assessed through nonparametric bootstrapping and multiple sensitivity analyses.

RESULTS: Early dose reduction or discontinuation was associated with lower quality-adjusted life years and higher or comparable costs compared with maintenance treatment over the 2-year analysis period. In the base-case societal perspective analysis, incremental costs were €23,563 and incremental effects were -0.077 QALYs, resulting in an incremental net monetary benefit of -€27,430 at a willingness-to-pay threshold of €50,000 per QALY at 24 months. Results were consistent across several scenarios with only approximately 9% probability that early dose reduction or discontinuation was cost-effective at the assumed willingness-to-pay threshold. However, a sex-at-birth subgroup analysis suggested a small and uncertain positive incremental net monetary benefit among female participants.

CONCLUSION: Over a 24-month period, early dose reduction or discontinuation of antipsychotic medication after remission from a FEP is unlikely to be cost-effective compared with maintenance treatment from both healthcare and societal perspectives. Whether longer term functional improvements associated with early dose reduction translate into cost-effective benefits remains uncertain and requires follow-up.

TRIAL REGISTRATION: EudraCT number: 2017-002406-12.

### Quality of media reporting of suicidal behaviour in India
**כתב עת:** 📄 Natl Med J India | **סוג מחקר:** תיאור מקרה | **מחברים:** Debnath S, Suri A | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov-Dec

BACKGROUND: India has a suicide rate of 18-21 fatalities per 100,000 people (compared to a global rate of 11 per 100,000), constituting around 28% of all suicides worldwide. Conscientious media coverage of suicide is one of the pillars of the World Health Organization's (WHO) life framework for suicide prevention. Media reporting of suicide events has thus far gone without sufficient scrutiny in India.

METHODS: A scholarly content analysis study was carried out on news articles reporting on suicide attempts and deaths in 6 daily newspapers in India between 1st January 2023, and 31st January 2023. The quality of suicide reporting against the WHO guidelines and the Press Council of India (PCI) 2022 recommendation was assessed. Descriptive statistics were used.

RESULTS: A total of 117 articles were analysed. The average number of suicide articles per newspaper per week was 3.9 (range 0.4-13.2). The names, occupation, and method of suicide were mentioned in 93 (79.5%), 58 (49.6%), and 97 (82.9%) of articles, respectively. 58.1% offered a single cause for the suicide event, whereas negative life events were mentioned for 63.2% of the news. All news articles included at least one harmful information according to the WHO media reporting guidelines. 84 (71.8%) news articles violated the recommendation provided in the guideline by not including any helpful information. The reporting of suicide violated the PCI 2022 guidelines in every domain.

CONCLUSION: Media coverage of suicidal behaviour is often suboptimal and fails to meet the standards set by the WHO suicide reporting guidelines and PCI edition 2022 guidelines.

### Suicide reporting in a changing media landscape: perspectives from Nepali media professionals
**כתב עת:** 📄 BMC Public Health | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Krishnamoorthy S et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Aug 5

Background Responsible media reporting of suicide is an evidence-based population-level suicide prevention intervention. To devise programs to engage with the media, it is imperative to understand the experiences and perspectives of media professionals. Suicides are highly newsworthy in Nepal and deeply shaped by socio-cultural factors such as gender roles, caste, and ethnicity. Yet little is known about media professionals’ practices and experiences in reporting on suicide. This study examines Nepali media professionals’ perspectives and experiences of suicide reporting, and the contextual forces shaping their work. Methods In 2025, semi-structured qualitative interviews with 22 diverse media professionals with experience reporting on suicide-related news were undertaken across different regions in Nepal. Semi-structured interviews explored why suicide incidents are reported, how suicide news is sourced and produced, and perceived challenges and responsibilities in reporting. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed and translated, and analysed using a thematic approach that combined deductive and inductive coding. Results Commercial pressures, audience demands, and socio-cultural factors were seen to drive decisions about which suicides become news (e.g. female suicides), how they are framed, and whose deaths are overlooked. While not allocated to one beat, suicides were often treated as crime news, with brief and graphic reports. A demand-supply logic was used to explain the high level of coverage of suicide news, with a high degree of audience interest in these stories. Participants described how organisational pressures, changing media environments, and sociocultural factors such as gender norms, stigma, and cultural narratives shaped suicide reporting. Conclusions Our findings shed light on how media professionals in Nepal understand and enact suicide reporting in a rapidly changing environment. By foregrounding media professionals’ own accounts of news values, routines and constraints, this study provides a contextual foundation for future work on media, suicide and public communication in Nepal. Background Globally, suicide accounts for around 720,000 deaths per year, with three-quarters of these deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [ 1 ]. Suicide rates in Nepal are among the highest globally, with recent estimates indicating approximately 20–24 deaths per 100,000 population per year, and males experiencing higher suicide mortality than females [ 2 ]. These figures should be interpreted with caution, in the absence of a national suicide registry [ 3 ]. Suicide data in Nepal are also filtered through reporting systems shaped by social, cultural, legal, and medical factors, making them less reliable [ 4 ]. Suicide research in Nepal is understood through culturally shared explanations shaped by gender roles, family relationships, social expectations and structural inequalities. Emerging qualitative evidence has attempted to examine what people think and believe about suicide which is often a part of their gendered cultural scripts [ 5 ]. These narratives may also influence which suicide incidents are considered newsworthy. For example, our previous study in South Asia found that suicides among females were substantially more likely to be reported in the news than suicides among males due to cultural framings around judgement and blame [ 6 ]. Female suicide is frequently explained as a response to persistent oppression, domestic violence, marital conflict, dowry-related abuse, restricted autonomy, and threats to family honour [ 5 , 7 , 8 ]. In contrast, male suicide is commonly explained through narratives of financial hardship, unemployment, migration, alcohol use, and the inability to fulfil socially prescribed roles as providers, alongside expectations regarding suppressing emotional distress [ 5 , 7 ]. Across both males and females, suicide is often interpreted through interpersonal and moral frameworks involving shame ( ijjat ), family conflict, social exclusion, and societal expectations [ 5 , 7 ]. Suicide patterns among males and females are shaped by the intersection of multiple socio-demographic factors, including age, marital status, employment, education, socioeconomic status, and health conditions, resulting in differences in suicide methods, profiles, and underlying contributing factors [ 9 ]. Therefore, suicide is often framed as the culmination of several socio-cultural constraints rather than individual psychopathology. These culturally embedded narratives shape not only public understandings of suicide but also the broader environment in which suicide is communicated, interpreted, and represented. A public health approach to suicide prevention that addresses broader social determinants has recently gained increasing global momentum, including in low- and middle-income countries [ 10 , 11 ]. Responsible media reporting has been recognised as one of the few effective, population level strategies for preventing suicides [ 12 – 14 ]. A substantial body of evidence demonstrates that media reporting can shape suicide-related behaviours. While sensationalised and graphic reporting has been associated with increased suicide risk through the “Werther effect”, responsible reporting and portrayals of hope, recovery, and alternatives to suicide have been linked to reduced suicide rates through the “Papageno effect" [ 15 ]. Importantly, efforts to curtail irresponsible media reporting have been linked to reductions in suicide rates [ 16 , 17 ] and have also been found to shape audience experiences of suicide reporting [ 18 ]. Accordingly, the WHO has issued guidelines to promote responsible media reporting [ 14 ]. While this represents an important step toward addressing the issue, much of the existing evidence on media reporting comes from high-income countries (HICs). Evidence on suicide and media in LMICs is expanding but remains concentrated in a few countries and dominated by newspaper analyses [ 19 ]. Research examining the lived experiences and perspectives of media professionals demonstrates that suicide reporting is shaped by an interplay between professional judgement, ethical responsibilities and broader socio-cultural contexts rather than media guidelines alone [ 20 – 22 ]. Media professionals frequently report balancing between competing imperatives such as informing the public, meeting audience expectations and producing news within a commercial and time pressed environment[ 20 , 21 ]. Consequently, responsible reporting guidelines represent only one influence among many, with their implementation often constrained by newsroom practices, local media environments, and professional norms [ 21 , 22 ]. This may partly explain the inconsistent implementation of WHO media guidelines across countries, particularly in LMICs which are often viewed as recipients of strategies and guidelines developed in HICs [ 23 ]. Media professionals have also highlighted their limited involvement in guideline development, scepticism regarding the evidence linking reporting styles with imitative suicide, and concerns that reporting restrictions may constitute censorship or reinforce taboos around suicide [ 21 , 22 ]. Additional challenges identified in India include fatalistic attitudes towards suicide, limited confidence in crisis support services, and the influential role of senior editorial leadership in shaping reporting practices [ 20 ]. At the same time, media professionals’ central roles in agenda-setting, framing, and priming public responses underscore their potential as important partners in suicide prevention efforts [ 20 ].

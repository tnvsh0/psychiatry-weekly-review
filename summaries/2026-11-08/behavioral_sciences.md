### The Gambler's Fallacy Fallacy Fallacy (Commentary on Xiang et al., 2025)
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Psychological Science (Psychol Sci) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Banki D, Simonsohn U | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 4

Xiang et al. concluded that the gambler's fallacy does not originate in biased beliefs. Reanalyzing their data, we arrived at the opposite conclusion. We show, more generally, that their main analysis-examining whether the median belief is biased-is not diagnostic of the presence of the gambler's fallacy, whereas our test of the share of probability estimates for the streak ending above or below 50% is diagnostic. We also report results from a new study in which at least 80% of gambler's-fallacy predictions originated in biased beliefs.

### Speech categorization consistency is robustly associated with receptive oral language performance
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (J Exp Psychol Gen) *(IF: 5.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Kim H et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 2

Speech perception research on individual differences and clinical populations has traditionally emphasized the gradient/categorical dimension of categorization. Recent work, however, highlights categorization consistency—trial-by-trial response variability—as a more informative index of individual differences. Whether this index is relevant for language functions remains unknown. Across three experiments, we tested whether speech categorization consistency, rather than gradiency, was associated with receptive oral language ability. In Experiment 1 , English-speaking adults ( n =57) completed a speech Visual Analog Scaling (VAS) task, rating a speech continuum on a continuous scale, alongside two language assessments. Greater consistency in speech categorization was strongly associated with better receptive oral language performance, whereas gradiency showed no such relationship. Experiment 2 ( n =76) added a visual VAS task in which participants performed the same task with a visual continuum, to determine whether the observed effects reflected domain-general categorization mechanisms. Speech consistency again showed a strong association with language ability, and commonality analyses indicated that this effect was largely unique to speech, not to visual consistency. Experiment 3 ( n =95) equated the number of speech and visual continua and added a non-verbal IQ measure. Despite shared variance across domains, speech categorization consistency retained a unique association with language performance beyond visual categorization consistency and non-verbal IQ. These findings suggest that categorization consistency reflects both domain-general and speech-specific components, with speech consistency showing the most robust relationship to receptive oral language performance. This reframes individual differences in speech categorization by highlighting categorization consistency as an important dimension linking perceptual processing and broader language function.

Introduction A crucial component of speech perception is the ability to map a continuous and variable acoustic signal onto phonetic categories (e.g., /b/ and /p/). These categories, in turn, form the foundation for higher-order language processes like word recognition and sentence comprehension. This process is far from trivial because speech is highly variable, with variability arising from numerous factors, such as differences among talkers, speech rates, and phonetic contexts ( Holt & Lotto, 2010 ; McMurray & Jongman, 2011 ). As such, the mechanisms listeners employ to solve these perceptual challenges are foundational for broader communicative competence. Given its importance, it is perhaps unsurprising that individual differences in speech perception have been linked to a range of language-related clinical outcomes. A substantial body of work has documented associations between speech perception and various real-world consequences, including dyslexia ( Robertson et al., 2009 ; Serniclaes et al., 2004 ; Werker & Tees, 1987 ), brain damage ( Blumstein et al., 1977 ; Dial et al., 2019 ; Wolmetz et al., 2011 ), understanding speech-in-noise ( Myers et al., 2024 ; Rizzi & Bidelman, 2024 ), and success in second/foreign language acquisition ( Honda et al., 2024a , 2024b ; Kapnoula & Samuel, 2024 ). This has also been extensively investigated in the context of Developmental Language Disorders (DLD) ( Coady et al., 2005 ; McMurray et al., 2014 ; Robertson et al., 2009 ; Ziegler et al., 2005 ), a disorder affecting 10–12% of children ( Bishop & Snowling, 2004 ; Catts et al., 2005 ) characterized by poor oral language in the absence of other obvious causes. Here, a longstanding hypothesis argues that poor language ability (or DLD) stems from a deficit in lower-level speech perception: impaired speech perception is an early obstacle for language development that can delay other aspects of language ability. Importantly, DLD is widely seen as the lower end of a continuous distribution of language ability ( Leonard, 1991 ; Tomblin, 2014 ; Tomblin & Zhang, 1999 ). Thus, this hypothesized relationship between speech perception, language development, and DLD is relevant to broader variation in functional language ability, even within the typical population. Despite decades of work, the relationship between speech perception and language abilities remains unsettled. Evidence has been inconsistent, and findings are often difficult to reconcile (e.g., Bishop & McArthur, 2004 ; McMurray et al., 2014 ; Robertson et al., 2009 ). One contributing factor may lie in how speech perception is typically assessed. Most studies used traditional forced-choice tasks, in which listeners identify sounds along a continuum between two phonemes (e.g., /b/-/p/) by selecting one of two discrete options. These responses are used to construct a psychometric function that estimates the boundary location and the steepness—or slope—of the categorization function. In the context of individual differences, particularly for populations with language-related disorders, it is often assumed that categorization should be discrete ( Joanisse et al., 2000 ; Ziegler et al., 2005 ). Thus, shallower slopes are typically interpreted as reflecting noisier or less precise category representations. However, this interpretation rests on the assumption that speech categories are inherently discrete. Recent theoretical work in speech perception challenges this view, suggesting that speech categories are inherently gradient, which allows for more flexible speech perception ( Schouten et al., 2003 ; Toscano et al., 2010 ) ( McMurray, 2022 , for a review). From this perspective, it is unclear whether a shallower slope indicated by the forced-choice task represents a disrupted or a more gradient system. Moreover, forced-choice tasks constrain responses to binary categories, potentially obscuring the underlying dimensionality of any differences. To address these limitations, researchers have increasingly turned to the Visual Analog Scaling (VAS) task ( Kapnoula et al., 2017 ; Kong & Edwards, 2016 ) (based on Massaro & Cohen, 1983 ), which offers a continuous alternative to the forced-choice task. In the VAS task, listeners also hear tokens from a speech continuum (e.g., beach-peach ), but they rate them on a continuous scale. This continuous response format allows for a more direct mapping between continuous levels of category activation and the response space by avoiding the imposition of binary responses. Over the past decade, the VAS task has proven effective in delineating individual differences in speech categorization. While listeners generally perceive speech sounds in a gradient manner, they vary in how gradient or categorical their responses are (e.g., Kapnoula et al., 2017 ). These differences are stable over time ( Kong & Edwards, 2016 ) and have been linked to various downstream speech processes ( Kapnoula et al., 2021 ), functional and structural neural correlates ( Fuhrmeister & Myers, 2021 ; Kapnoula & McMurray, 2021 ), and second language learning ( Fuhrmeister et al., 2023 ; Honda et al., 2024a , 2024b ; Kapnoula et al., 2021 ). Despite this growing body of research, two critical limitations remain. First, these studies have largely focused on the relationship between variation in speech categorization and cognitively adjacent processes (e.g., cue integration, second language speech sound learning, auditory neuroscience). They have not asked about more distal relationships, such as the relationship between speech sound categorization in the VAS task and language as a whole. Second, to date, most studies using the VAS task have focused on the slope of the categorization function as the primary metric of interest. The slope (gradiency) indexes how abruptly listeners transition between categories, with steeper slopes reflecting more categorical perception and shallower slopes indicating more gradient use of intermediate ratings.

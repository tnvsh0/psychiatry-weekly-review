### Parent Comfort With Artificial Intelligence-Led Behavioral Therapy for Their Child
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Pediatrics (JAMA Pediatr) *(IF: 27.6)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Martoma RA et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 2

This survey study assesses the comfort of parents with pediatric behavioral therapy led by artificial intelligence (AI) by evaluating results from a cross-sectional survey of US adults with at least 1 child aged 5 to 17 years.

Shortages in pediatric behavioral health services have increased interest in leveraging artificial intelligence (AI) for support, and platforms that offer to deliver these services are increasingly accessible to children and adolescents. In a 2025 national survey, 19.2% of US youth aged 12 to 21 years reported using AI chatbots for mental health advice, often without disclosure to parents or clinicians. Pediatric behavioral therapy requires clinical evaluation, family involvement, safety planning, and clear escalation procedures. The American Psychological Association has cautioned that adolescent AI use raises concerns about developmental vulnerability, inaccurate health information, and privacy. Parents’ views may inform how AI-led behavioral therapy is governed for children, yet parent comfort with this approach is unknown. Methods We conducted a cross-sectional survey of US adults in November 2025 through the OutbreaksNearMe participatory surveillance platform using a nonprobability end-page river sample on SurveyMonkey, with survey weights approximating US Census demographic targets for age, gender, race and ethnicity, education, and income. The analytic sample included adults with at least 1 child aged 5 to 17 years. The Boston Children’s Hospital institutional review board approved the study, which followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology ( STROBE ) reporting guideline. Participants provided electronic informed consent. Participants self-selected race and ethnicity from survey categories for weighting and adjustment. The primary outcome was an ordered 4-level response to “How comfortable would you be with a therapy session led by an AI for your children?” Options were “not comfortable at all,” “not too comfortable,” “somewhat comfortable,” and “very comfortable.” Parallel items asked about therapy and primary care for themselves. Associations with higher comfort were estimated using survey-weighted cumulative-logit ordinal logistic regression among parents with complete data. Models adjusted for parent age, gender, race and ethnicity, educational attainment, household income, chronic illness, health care satisfaction, household child age groups, and the AI-related measures reported in the Figure and Table . The proportional-odds assumption was assessed qualitatively using threshold-specific estimates and graphical summaries. Weighted proportions and 95% CIs used logit-transformed Taylor-linearized standard errors. Analyses used R version 4.6.0 (R Foundation). Results Among 3102 respondents, 584 had at least 1 child aged 5 to 17 years, and 572 responded to the primary outcome regarding comfort level with AI-led behavioral therapy for their child. After weighting, the sample was 53.3% female, 45.7% male, and 1.0% not listed or nonconforming; 6.2% were Asian or Pacific Islander, 12.0% Black, 23.6% Hispanic, 54.2% White, and 3.9% other. Weighted comfort levels were 47.2% not comfortable at all (95% CI, 42.1%-52.3%), 28.8% not too comfortable (95% CI, 24.5%-33.6%), 16.2% somewhat comfortable (95% CI, 12.7%-20.5%), and 7.8% very comfortable (95% CI, 5.3%-11.3%). In the multivariable ordinal regression model (n = 554), higher comfort was associated with parents’ own comfort with AI-led behavioral therapy for themselves (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 20.94; 95% CI, 9.78-44.86) and AI-led primary care for themselves (aOR, 3.78; 95% CI, 2.16-6.59). Higher comfort was also associated with expectations that AI would perform better than (aOR, 10.12; 95% CI, 4.38-23.38) or about the same as (aOR, 3.31; 95% CI, 1.95-5.61) medical professionals for pediatric behavioral therapy. Child receipt of behavioral therapy in the past year was also associated with higher comfort (aOR, 2.07; 95% CI, 1.22-3.50). General AI use by parents and children was not associated with comfort. When asked which factors would matter most in deciding about AI-led behavioral therapy for their child, parents (n = 546) most frequently selected accuracy of diagnosis (58.5%), level of empathy (49.4%), recommendation from a medical professional (46.8%), and confidentiality and privacy (43.7%); cost (32.8%), scheduling flexibility (29.5%), and appointment wait time (27.7%) were selected less often. Discussion In this cross-sectional survey of US parents, approximately three-quarters were not comfortable at all or not too comfortable with AI-led behavioral therapy for their child. Higher comfort was most strongly associated with parents’ own comfort with AI-led health care and expectations that AI would perform similarly to or better than medical professionals, with prior child behavioral therapy also associated. These findings add the parent perspective to prior work on youth AI chatbot use. Parent acceptance may depend more on AI-related health care trust than on convenience or familiarity alone; general AI use was unrelated to comfort. Diagnostic accuracy, empathy, professional recommendation, and privacy were selected as priorities more often than cost, scheduling flexibility, or wait time. Clinician recommendations may be a modifiable lever for acceptance yet remain uncharacterized for pediatric AI-led therapy. Limitations Limitations include the nonprobability sample, modest sample size limiting precision and assessment of subgroup heterogeneity, and unmeasured familiarity with health care AI. Comfort may not reflect actual use, appropriateness, or safety. Cross-sectional data preclude causal inference, and parent comfort for self and child may reflect the same underlying construct. Conclusions This study found that most parents reported low comfort with AI-led behavioral therapy for their children. As these tools scale, policy should emphasize rigorous clinical evaluation, privacy protections, clinician and family oversight, and escalation pathways to earn parental trust.

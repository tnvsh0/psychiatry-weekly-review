### Longitudinal relations between social withdrawal subtypes, internalizing problems, and well-being among Chinese youth: exploring within-person and between-person effects
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Development and Psychopathology (Dev Psychopathol) *(IF: 5.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Zhao R et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 9

To date, the extant literature on the development and implications of social withdrawal is comprised predominantly of studies focused on between-person associations. As such, we have limited understanding of the intra-individual processes that may underlie these relations. The goal of this study was to explore both between- and within-person associations between different subtypes of social withdrawal (shyness, unsociability, social avoidance), internalizing problems, and indices of well-being in a sample of children and early adolescents from mainland China. A total of 1,001 children (563 boys; 100% Han descent) in grades 4-8 ( M age  = 11.71 years,  SD  = 1.45, range = 7.83-14.17) participated in a four-wave longitudinal data collection procedure. Results from random intercept cross-lagged panel models (RI-CLPM) indicated that social withdrawal subtypes were all associated with indices of internalizing problems (depression, loneliness, social anxiety) and poorer well-being at the between-person level (standardized effect sizes ranged from .21 to .61). Within-person analyses revealed reciprocal relations between shyness and well-being, unsociability and depression, unsociability and loneliness, as well as social avoidance and loneliness. These findings offer preliminary evidence that a within-person perspective could be useful for developing tailored interventions to support children's psychological adjustment in Chinese society.

### Neighborhood poly-victimization and adolescent mental health: Community violence, sexual street harassment, and family cohesion across two waves in urban Mexico
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Development and Psychopathology (Dev Psychopathol) *(IF: 5.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Lopez AM et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 9

Violence exposure profoundly disrupts adolescent development, such as in Mexico, where narco-related crime has normalized chronic insecurity. Utilizing a two-wave longitudinal design, we examined poly-victimization (community violence exposure and sexual street harassment), family cohesion, and psychological health among 346 Mexican adolescents ( M age  = 13.02,  SD  = 0.98; 47.9% girls, 52.1% boys) in Uruapan, Mexico. Path models showed poly-victimization was associated with elevated depressive and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms cross-sectionally, but not longitudinally once baseline symptoms were controlled. Witnessing violence, but not personal victimization, remained cross-sectionally associated with both outcomes. Family cohesion did not buffer these associations; instead, cohesion exacerbated the cross-sectional link between sexual street harassment and PTSD symptoms. Girls reported higher depressive and PTSD symptoms than boys, though gender did not moderate any violence-symptom association. Findings suggest family cohesion, often assumed protective, may intensify rather than buffer the psychological toll of sexual street harassment among Mexican adolescents facing chronic violence.

### Influence of sex, puberty, and childhood psychopathology on developmental trajectories of the visual P300 across adolescence
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Child Development (Child Dev) *(IF: 5.4)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Trayvick J et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 13

Research indicates the visual P300 decreases across adolescence due to maturation of neural attention networks. However, there is limited research examining whether assigned sex at birth and puberty influence this neurodevelopment. A blunted visual P300 has also been linked with internalizing and externalizing psychopathology, but it is unclear whether childhood psychopathology predicts changes in the P300 across adolescence. The present study examined whether sex, pubertal development, and childhood internalizing and externalizing symptoms predicted trajectories of the visual P300 across adolescence. The sample included 531 New York youth (45.5% female; 89.4% White; 19.7% Hispanic) who completed a flanker task to elicit the visual P300 at ages 9, 12, 15, 18, and 21 (2010-2025). Multilevel growth modeling indicated that the visual P300 was characterized by a quadratic decrease from late childhood to early adulthood; males showed a slower rate of change than females. Females with greater age 12 pubertal development showed a faster rate of change. Males with higher age 9 externalizing symptoms and depression symptoms had blunted baseline P300s. Age 9 internalizing and externalizing symptoms did not relate to slope. Results highlight the need to better understand the developmental of neural indicators of attentional processing that have been linked to future psychopathology.

The P300 is a neural signal reflecting attention and cognitive processing, which decreases in amplitude during adolescence due to maturation of neural attention networks. The present study involved 531 youth ages 9 to 21, measuring their sex, pubertal development, early psychopathology symptoms, and P300 development across adolescence. The P300 decreased in a curved pattern across adolescence into early adulthood, and females showed a faster decrease than males. The P300 of more physically mature females at age 12 decreased faster. Males with more baseline externalizing problems and depression had a smaller baseline P300, though symptoms did not predict how the P300 changed over time. These findings suggest sex, puberty, and early psychopathology all impact attention-related brain development during adolescence.

### Parent-Child Physiological Synchrony: Links With Parent Emotion Regulation and Children's Independent Emotion Regulation
**כתב עת:** 📄 Developmental Science (Dev Sci) *(IF: 4.4)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Perry NB et al. | **תאריך:** 2027 Jan

ABSTRACT Developmental theories propose that physiological synchrony is a key process through which co‐regulatory experiences support children's emerging capacity for independent emotion regulation. Caregivers’ emotion regulation abilities are also theorized to influence their capacity to engage in synchronous interactions, thereby shaping co‐regulatory experiences. Despite these propositions, few studies have empirically examined these developmental processes. The current study examined associations between parent–child respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) synchrony during dyadic problem solving and children's physiological (i.e, RSA reactivity) and behavioral regulation during a subsequent independent challenge. The study also examined whether parents’ emotion regulation difficulties predicted RSA synchrony and whether synchrony statistically accounted for associations between parents’ emotion regulation difficulties and children's regulatory outcomes. Participants included 57 preschoolers ( M _age = 4.26 years; 49% male) and their parents (50 mothers, 7 fathers). Families were predominantly White (68%), with 62% reporting a master's degree or higher. Greater parent‐driven RSA synchrony during dyadic interactions was associated with reduced reliance on distraction and help‐seeking strategies during the independent problem‐solving task but was not associated with children's physiological regulation. Parents reporting greater difficulties maintaining goal‐directed behavior and less emotional awareness exhibited lower levels of parent‐driven synchrony, and synchrony emerged as an indirect pathway linking these parent characteristics to children's behavioral regulation. Findings provide preliminary support for physiological synchrony as a developmental mechanism linking parent emotion regulation to children's independent emotion regulation during early childhood.

Summary Findings support parent–child physiological synchrony as a potential pathway linking parents’ emotion regulation capacities to children's independent emotion regulation. Greater parent‐driven physiological synchrony during dyadic problem solving was not associated with children's physiological regulation during independent problem solving. Greater parent‐driven physiological synchrony during dyadic problem solving predicted less reliance on external behavioral regulation during independent problem solving. Greater parental difficulty maintaining goal‐directed behavior during distress and lower emotional awareness predicted reduced parent‐driven physiological synchrony.

1 Introduction Emotion regulation is widely recognized as a foundational developmental task of early childhood, with individual differences in children's regulatory abilities associated with functioning across academic (Djambazova‐Popordanoska 2016 ; Wong et al. 2023 ), social (De Neve et al. 2023 ; Qashmer 2023 ), and psychological domains (Klinge et al. 2025 ; Perry et al. 2018 ). Developmental theories propose that emotion regulation emerges within the context of caregiver–child interactions (Morris et al. 2017 ; Sameroff 2010 ; Sroufe 1997 ). Early in life, caregivers must regulate their own emotions while helping children manage physiological arousal and behavioral distress (Eisenberg et al. 1998 ; Morris et al. 2007 ; Snyder et al. 2025 ). Over time, these co‐regulatory experiences are thought to contribute to children's increasing capacity for independent emotion regulation (Perry and Calkins 2018 ). Despite the prominence of these theoretical perspectives, relatively little research has directly examined the distinct processes through which caregiver–child interactions support children's emerging self‐regulation. To address this gap, the current study examined whether parent–child physiological synchrony during dyadic interaction was associated with children's independent biobehavioral emotion regulation and whether synchrony was predicted by distinct aspects of parents’ own regulatory capacities. We also tested whether physiological synchrony represented a potential developmental pathway linking parents’ emotion regulation to children's independent emotion regulation. 2 Parent–Child Physiological Synchrony and Children's Emotion Regulation Caregivers provide behavioral support by modeling appropriate emotional responses and teaching regulatory strategies (Bell 2020 ; Eisenberg et al. 1998 ), but they may also shape the development of neural and physiological systems that support adaptive responses to emotional challenge (Hofer 1987 ; Shonkoff et al. 2012 ). Physiological synchrony, defined as coordination between caregivers’ and children's physiological responses during emotionally salient interactions (DePasquale 2020 ), is thought to reflect mutual attunement to emotional states, potentially allowing caregivers to respond more effectively to children's changing regulatory needs. As such, physiological synchrony has been proposed as one possible mechanism through which co‐regulatory experiences contribute to children's emerging self‐regulatory capacities (Davis et al. 2018 ; Feldman et al. 2010 ). A substantial body of research on physiological synchrony has focused on the parasympathetic nervous system (PNS), given its central role in the regulation of physiological arousal, behavior, and emotion (Porges 2003 , 2025 ). During states of rest, parasympathetic influence via the vagus nerve promotes regulated cardiac activity, whereas environmental challenge can elicit vagal withdrawal, allowing physiological resources to be mobilized in support of active coping (Porges et al. 1996 ). Developmental scientists have proposed that caregivers may facilitate maturation of the vagal system, including greater myelination of vagal fibers, by consistently helping children flexibly upregulate and downregulate arousal (Porges and Furman 2011 ). If so, greater caregiver–child physiological synchrony may be associated not only with more adaptive PNS responses to emotional challenge, but also with the behavioral regulation strategies these biological systems support (Porges 2025 ; Tang et al. 2025 ). PNS functioning is commonly indexed using respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), a component of heart rate variability that occurs at the frequency of respiration (Beauchaine and Thayer 2015 ; Ritz 2024 ). RSA provides a noninvasive index of parasympathetic regulation, with decreases in RSA during environmental challenge generally interpreted as an adaptive physiological response that supports engagement with changing contextual demands (Sacrey et al. 2021 ). Extending this work to the dyadic level, researchers have examined the extent to which caregivers’ and children's RSA responses are synchronized during interaction. To model RSA synchrony, researchers frequently use multilevel coupled autoregressive models (Hu et al. 2021 ; Lunkenheimer et al. 2015 ; Lunkenheimer et al. 2018 ), which allow for a distinction between caregiver‐driven synchrony, in which caregivers’ RSA predicts children's subsequent RSA, and child‐driven synchrony, in which children's RSA predicts caregivers’ subsequent RSA. These time‐lagged associations provide insight into the strength and direction of physiological coupling during caregiver–child interactions. Including person‐average RSA as a covariate further helps distinguish within‐dyad physiological coupling from stable individual differences in RSA. ### Tracing the impact of maternal communication deviance on joint engagement: The mediating role of maternal supportive behaviors
**כתב עת:** 📄 Infant Mental Health Journal (Infant Ment Health J) *(IF: 2.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Sousa P et al. | **תאריך:** 2027 Jan

Abstract Communication Deviance is a disorganized communicative style that can disrupt shared meaning‐making in parent–child interactions and is considered a risk factor for later mental health difficulties. This longitudinal study, conducted in the UK, examined whether maternal Communication Deviance assessed during pregnancy predicted joint engagement and related processes. Participants were 229 mothers (95.9% White British; M = 27.6 years, SD = 6.1). Maternal Communication Deviance was assessed antenatally, and mother–child dyads were observed during play at 30‐months. Communication Deviance predicted lower total joint engagement, including supported ( β = –.18, p = .007), coordinated ( β = –.17, p = .016), and symbol‐infused joint engagement ( β = –.24, p < .001), and was associated with reduced supportive behaviors ( β = –.40, p < .001) and dyadic attunement ( β = –.31, p < .001). Mediation analyses indicated that supportive behaviors accounted for the associations between Communication Deviance and joint engagement outcomes. These findings suggest that Communication Deviance may constrain children's opportunities for shared attention and meaning making. The study supports developmental models that highlight early communicative processes as foundational to socio‐cognitive and relational development and identifies supportive behaviors as a potential target for interventions promoting early mental health and parent–child interaction. Key Findings Higher maternal Communication Deviance during pregnancy predicted less Total, Supported, Coordinated, and Symbol‐infused joint engagement at 30 months. Reduced maternal supportive behaviors fully explained the link between Communication Deviance and lower joint engagement and dyadic attunement. Maternal Communication Deviance may act as an early environmental risk factor, highlighting the importance of supporting maternal responsiveness to promote healthy development. Statement of Relevance This study examines how maternal Communication Deviance measured during pregnancy predicts joint engagement and dyadic attunement at 30 months, processes central to early socio‐cognitive and relational development. By identifying maternal supportive behaviors as a key mechanism linking Communication Deviance to interactional quality, the research highlights a modifiable target for early intervention. Findings advance understanding of early environmental risk factors and inform strategies to support parent–child interactions, directly addressing foundational processes relevant to child and early childhood mental health.

1 COMMUNICATION DEVIANCE AS A RISK FACTOR FOR PSYCHOPATHOLOGY Communication Deviance refers to a communicative style characterized by vagueness, ambiguity, and disruptions in shared meaning between interlocutors (Singer, 1965a , b ; Singer et al., 1978 ; Tienari & Wahlberg, 2008 ; Wynne, 1963a ; Wynne et al., 1963 , 1977b , 1984 ). It encompasses a range of linguistic and interactional disturbances that can hinder mutual understanding during social exchange (e.g., unclear referents, contradictory statements, or poorly timed contributions). Communication Deviance has been most extensively studied in the context of schizophrenia‐spectrum disorders, where elevated levels in parents have been associated with increased risk of thought disorder and broader psychopathology in offspring (de Sousa et al., 2014 ; Roisko et al., 2014 ). Longitudinal findings indicate that Communication Deviance precedes the onset of such difficulties, supporting its role as an environmental risk factor rather than a response to child characteristics (Goldstein, 1987 ). However, Communication Deviance is not specific to schizophrenia; similar communicative patterns have been observed in families experiencing other forms of psychopathology, relational distress, and needs, suggesting it is better conceptualized as a dimensional and transdiagnostic construct (Miklowitz & Stackman, 1992 ). Evidence from adoption studies further suggests that Communication Deviance interacts with genetic vulnerability, such that its impact on offspring outcomes depends on broader risk context (Wahlberg et al., 1997 , 2000 ). Overall, this body of work supports the view that parental CD constitutes an environmental risk factor in the development of psychopathology. However, the mechanisms through which CD operates in early caregiving contexts remain poorly understood. 2 PLAUSIBLE DEVELOPMENTAL MECHANISM One proposed pathway is through the disruption of early interactional processes in childhood. In particular, Communication Deviance may interfere with joint engagement which refers to episodes of mutual attention, communication, and emotional connection between caregiver and child around a common interest or task (Adamson et al., 2004 , 2009 , 2012 ). These interactions are critical for the development of social understanding, emotional regulation, and cognitive skills (Adamson et al., 2019 ) and the quality of nonverbal and verbal interactions (e.g., dyadic connectedness and fluency) seemed to be important for the child's cognitive and social development (Hirsh‐Pasek et al., 2015 ). If caregiver communication is vague, inconsistent, or poorly timed, opportunities for sustained shared attention and meaning making may be reduced. In this way, Communication Deviance may constrain the quality of early interactional experiences that support development. Although this account is theoretically plausible, it has not yet been directly tested. 2.1 Joint engagement Joint engagement refers to sustained, reciprocal interactions between a child and a social partner in which both participants are actively involved in a shared activity and maintain a common focus over time (Adamson, 2018 ; Adamson et al., 2014 ; Trevarthen & Aitken, 2001 ). These engagement states are characterized not only by shared attention, but also by the quality of the interaction, including affective attunement, reciprocity, and the use of gestures, actions, and language. This construct is grounded in sociocultural developmental theory (Vygotsky & Cole, 1978 ) and early work on infants’ emerging capacity to share attention with caregivers (Scaife & Bruner, 1975 ). Within these broader engagement states, joint attention is best conceptualized as a set of social‐communicative skills that enable the coordination of attention between partners, including behaviors such as responding to joint attention, initiating joint attention, gaze following, and pointing (Adamson et al., 2019 ; Mundy, 2018 ). Rather than being synonymous with joint engagement, these skills support the initiation, maintenance, and elaboration of joint engagement episodes. Thus, joint attention reflects the child's moment‐to‐moment attentional coordination abilities, whereas joint engagement captures the broader, dynamic context in which these skills are embedded and expressed (Adamson et al., 2019 ). Disruptions to both joint attention skills and joint engagement states are well documented in autism and are thought to contribute to broader differences in social communication and cognition (Adamson et al., 2009 ; Nyström et al., 2019 ). These early social interactions are supported by developing perceptual‐motor capacities (Butterworth & Jarrett, 1991 ) and neurocognitive mechanisms that orient the child toward social information (Baron‐Cohen, 1997 ), but they are fundamentally shaped within relational and affective contexts (Hobson, 2005 ; Hobson & Hobson, 2011 ; Racine & Carpendale, 2007 ). During the first year of life, infants begin to engage in behaviors that recruit others’ attention, such as gaze following and pointing, with more consistent use emerging toward the end of the first year (Salter & Carpenter, 2025 ). Across the second year, episodes of joint engagement become increasingly sustained, differentiated, and socially coordinated (Bakeman & Adamson, 1984 ; Charman et al., 2000 ; Mundy & Gomes, 1998 ).

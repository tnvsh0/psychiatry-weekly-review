### Imagery-based fear conditioning and extinction learning in individuals with psychotic liability: an EEG study
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Behaviour Research and Therapy (Behav Res Ther) *(IF: 5.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Ahrens-Demirdal NE et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 4

BACKGROUND: Pavlovian fear conditioning using aversive imagery rather than physical stimuli as the unconditioned stimulus (US) provides a powerful paradigm to study fear learning in psychosis, in which threat expectations often develop in the absence of proximal external threats. This preregistered study aimed to identify behavioral, psychophysiological, and neural aberrancies in imagery-based fear acquisition and extinction learning associated with psychotic liability.

METHODS: Participants at-risk for psychosis (n = 70 with elevated psychosis proneness, n = 28 with verified clinical high-risk status) and non-clinical controls (n = 55) underwent an imagery-based fear conditioning paradigm. Participants learnt to produce aversive, neutral (US), or no imagery in response to cues (shapes) that were systematically paired with a different neutral face (conditioned stimulus; CS). Behavioral ratings, EEG (late positive potential; LPP), and psychophysiological responses (skin conductance responses; SCRs) were assessed.

RESULTS: The at-risk group showed more pronounced acquisition learning in fear and arousal ratings and LPPs than controls. This pattern was driven by elevated affect ratings to the CS+ that had been paired with aversive imagery. In contrast, explicit expectancy ratings indicated less CS discrimination in at-risk individuals. The at-risk group also showed reduced extinction learning in expectancy but faster extinction learning in fear and arousal ratings than controls.

CONCLUSIONS: Psychotic liability is associated with an increased susceptibility to acquire fear through negative mental imagery, even in the absence of actual threat. These findings provide novel insights into the pathogenesis of psychosis, suggesting that imagined threat beliefs could contribute to delusion formation.

### Effects of mindfulness-based interventions on sleep quality in university students: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
**כתב עת:** 📄 Front Public Health | **סוג מחקר:** מטה-אנליזה | **מחברים:** Wang H et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

Background Sleep problems are common among university students due to academic stress, lifestyle factors, and psychological distress and may adversely affect physical and mental health, academic performance, and social functioning. Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) have increasingly been used as a non-pharmacological approach to improve sleep health; however, previous findings remain inconsistent, and evidence specifically addressing sleep quality in university students remains limited. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the effects of MBIs on sleep quality in university students and explore factors that may account for variations in intervention effects. Methods We searched seven databases—PubMed, Web of Science, EBSCO, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, Embase, and ScienceDirect—from inception to June 26, 2026. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating MBIs for sleep quality in university students were included. Two reviewers independently conducted study selection, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment using the RoB 2 tool. Meta-analysis was conducted in Stata 17.0 using a random-effects model. Subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, publication bias assessment, and GRADE assessment were also performed. Results Ten RCTs involving 638 university students were included, with 335 participants in the intervention groups and 303 in the control groups. MBIs significantly improved sleep quality [SMD = −0.97, 95% CI (−1.43, −0.51), p < 0.001], although substantial heterogeneity was observed ( I 2 = 85.1%). Subgroup analyses revealed statistically significant between-group differences with respect to sleep-outcome measurement instrument ( p = 0.001), intervention type ( p = 0.009), and session duration ( p = 0.030), indicating that these variables may partially account for the observed heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the pooled results. Egger’s test revealed no evidence of significant publication bias ( p = 0.270). The certainty of the evidence was rated as low according to the GRADE framework. Conclusion MBIs may improve sleep quality in university students. However, given the substantial heterogeneity and low certainty of evidence, further high-quality RCTs are needed to confirm these findings. Systematic review registration CRD420261458068, https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420261458068 .

1 Introduction Sleep is a fundamental physiological need essential for maintaining human health and normal functioning and plays a critical role in cognitive performance, emotional regulation, mental health, and cardiovascular and metabolic function ( 1 ). The university years represent a critical transitional period from adolescence to adulthood. During this period, students are exposed to multiple stressors, including academic competition, examination pressure, and career planning, while also commonly engaging in behaviors such as irregular sleep schedules and caffeine consumption, all of which may adversely affect sleep quality. A meta-analysis of 48 studies involving 95,938 undergraduate students reported a pooled prevalence of insomnia symptoms of 46.9% ( 2 ). Similarly, a systematic review and meta-analysis of university students in Africa reported pooled prevalences of poor sleep quality of 62.34% among general university students, 64.81% among health science students, and 60.33% among medical students ( 3 ), further highlighting the high prevalence of sleep problems among university students. Importantly, poor sleep quality may have a range of adverse consequences. It may impair learning and academic performance among university students ( 4 , 5 ). In addition, sleep problems are closely associated with anxiety, depression, elevated stress levels, and reduced quality of life ( 6 , 7 ). Taken together, these findings indicate that sleep problems are common among university students and may adversely affect both their academic performance and their physical and mental health. Current approaches to improving sleep quality among university students can generally be classified as pharmacological or non-pharmacological interventions. Although pharmacological treatments may alleviate sleep problems to some extent, uncertainty remains regarding their long-term benefits and safety. A study of university students found that continued melatonin use was associated with subsequent increases in sleep disturbance and depressive symptoms ( 8 ). Therefore, pharmacological treatment may not be an appropriate routine long-term strategy for managing sleep problems in university students. In contrast, mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs), as a non-pharmacological approach, have increasingly been applied to the management of sleep and psychological health in university populations. These interventions are predominantly meditation-centered and train individuals to observe present-moment bodily sensations, emotions, and cognitive experiences with an open, accepting, and non-judgmental stance, thereby diminishing automatic reactivity to negative thoughts and stressful stimuli ( 9–11 ). Greeson et al. reported that the Koru mindfulness program, developed for university students and emerging adults, significantly reduced stress and alleviated sleep problems ( 12 ). Another study found that a low-intensity mindfulness intervention improved subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, and habitual sleep efficiency, with some benefits persisting into the later stages of the intervention ( 13 ). Collectively, these findings suggest that MBIs may represent a useful adjunctive approach to promoting sleep health in university settings. Although several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have suggested that MBIs may improve sleep quality in university students, findings from previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses remain inconsistent. A meta-analysis including five studies found only a small improvement in sleep quality following MBIs, and the pooled effect did not reach statistical significance ( 14 ). Another meta-analysis specifically examining mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) among university students included 29 RCTs overall; however, only three studies contributed data on sleep quality, and the pooled effect was likewise not statistically significant ( 15 ). In contrast, another meta-analysis including only two studies found that MBIs may significantly improve sleep quality in university students ( 16 ). Notably, previous meta-analyses have generally examined MBIs as one subgroup among several psychological interventions or have treated sleep quality as one of multiple secondary outcomes of MBIs. Consequently, systematic evaluations specifically focusing on the effects of MBIs on sleep quality in university students remain relatively limited. Moreover, previous analyses of sleep quality have included relatively few studies and small overall sample sizes, which may have resulted in unstable pooled effect estimates and limited the precision and reliability of their conclusions. Given these limitations in the existing literature, the present study aimed to systematically evaluate the effects of MBIs on sleep quality in university students and to conduct exploratory subgroup analyses to examine whether intervention characteristics, delivery modalities, and outcome measurement instruments may influence the observed intervention effects. ### Written exposure therapy as treatment for comorbid PTSD and SUD: a systematic review and meta-analysis
**כתב עת:** 📄 Curr Psychiatry Rep | **סוג מחקר:** מטה-אנליזה | **מחברים:** Fatima T et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 7

Purpose of review This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness, feasibility, and acceptability of Written Exposure Therapy (WET) when it is used as a component in integrated treatment of comorbid PTSD and Substance Use Disorder (SUD). A systematic review and meta-analysis was used to synthesise foundational knowledge regarding WET. Recent findings Twelve studies were included and analysed in the review. Random-effects meta-analysis of six studies showed large overall pre/post decrease in PTSD severity (Hedges’ g = 1.18, 95% CI [0.68, 1.72], p < 0.001). Rate of retention (69.03%) was higher than typically seen in integrated prolonged exposure treatments for PTSD-SUD. The majority of participants and providers found WET feasible and acceptable. A minority of participants found concurrent treatment for both disorders burdensome. Summary Findings suggest that can be an effective component in integrated treatment of PTSD-SUD, owing to its efficacy, brevity, and low-burden of integration. Limitations of the current findings include small samples, lack of comparator groups, and limited information regarding treatment components and change in SUD. Supplementary Information The online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11920-026-01724-x .

Introduction Post Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) and Substance Use Disorder (SUD) are significant mental health concerns that are frequently comorbid. Prevalence of this comorbidity ranges from 25–49% in the general population, and is typically higher in clinical populations [ 1 – 3 ]. Patients with comorbid PTSD-SUD experience a more severe, complex, costly, and chronic course of illness compared to patients with either disorder alone. Unfortunately, both disorders maintain each other, impede recovery for either condition, and are more difficult to treat when comorbid [ 1 , 4 – 9 ]. PTSD and traumatic exposure exert significant influence over the impacts and outcomes of PTSD-SUD. Substance use is more common in individuals who report trauma exposure or meet criteria for PTSD, with a higher number of traumatic events becoming increasingly predictive of SUD severity [ 10 , 11 ]. PTSD severity predicts faster relapse following SUD treatment, with patients endorsing elevated PTSD symptoms prior to and immediately following relapse [ 7 , 12 , 13 ]. Finally, a reduction in PTSD severity accounts for a greater and more sustained reduction in SUD, while a reduction in SUD severity does not account for a similar or greater reduction in PTSD [ 7 , 8 , 12 , 14 ]. Treatment of comorbid PTSD-SUD Growing evidence suggests that exposure-based integrated interventions are safe, tolerable, and more effective than non-exposure-based interventions in the treatment of PTSD-SUD, without exacerbating or compromising outcomes for SUD [ 12 , 14 – 20 ]. However, existing treatments have inherent constraints that limit their accessibility and retention. Integrated treatments are typically longer than treatment for either disorder [ 21 ], requiring between 12–15 sessions that each last 60–90 min [ 20 , 22 , 23 ]. Most clinicians manage high clinical workloads, and are typically trained for either PTSD or SUD but not both. The burden of resources and time required by specialized integrated treatments is quite high [ 4 , 24 ]. Finally, studies show that less than 40% of participants receive an adequate dose of exposure before dropping out of integrated treatment, and more than 40% leave prior to completion [ 25 – 28 ]. In this landscape, Written Exposure Therapy (WET) is emerging as a modality of interest. WET is a brief therapy utilising exposure through writing, across 5–7 60-min sessions, with no between-session homework [ 29 ]. It has been established as an effective treatment for PTSD across a variety of demographic and cultural contexts [ 30 ] and is shown to be non-inferior to existing exposure-based therapies [ 31 , 32 ]. Recent literature has started to explore its use for the treatment of PTSD-SUD, as it offers several potential advantages. Its brevity can allow for easier integration into existing SUD treatment programs. Its highly structured delivery and low training burden may make it more feasible for clinicians. And in its less cognitively-demanding nature, it may also be more accessible to a broader range of clients. Finally, by both frontloading the exposure in the first session and via writing, WET may also reduce treatment dropout. To date, only exploratory and pilot studies with small samples have explored the potential of WET as a treatment for PTSD-SUD. However, no systematic collation of WET’s effectiveness for PTSD-SUD treatment exists in the current literature. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of the international literature to: Examine changes in PTSD symptoms following Written Exposure Therapy (WET) among individuals with comorbid PTSD and substance use disorders (SUD). Evaluate preliminary evidence regarding the impact of WET integrated within SUD treatment settings on substance use–related outcomes.. Assess the feasibility and acceptability of WET as a component of integrated treatment for PTSD-SUD. Method The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines were followed for conducting this review (PRISMA 2020) [ 33 ]. The review was registered with the Prospero International Prospective Register for Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; ID CRD42024602565) [ 34 ]. Studies that implement or plan to implement WET as treatment for adults presenting with co-morbid PTSD/trauma and SUD were included. Detailed inclusion criteria and search terms used are detailed in Appendix A. The initial search was conducted in November 2024 and included publications from January 2000 to October 2024. Studies short-listed for inclusion were screened by two reviewers and any disputes were resolved by discussion among all three reviewers. Data extraction and analysis A data extraction template was created in Covidence. Data were extracted by two reviewers regarding the publication, sample size and demographic characteristics, retention, any comparators or control treatments, psychometric measures, data analysis strategy, reported outcomes, conclusions, and limitations of the study. A risk of bias assessment (RoB) was conducted for studies that reported quantitative outcomes for change in PTSD or SUD, as these were considered most relevant to the goals of this review. The Cochrane RoB in Non-Randomised Studies – of Interventions Version 2 (ROBINS-I-V2) [ 35 ] was used to assess pilot studies, and the RoB 2 [ 36 ] was used to assess RCTs. The RoB was completed independently by two reviewers, and discrepancies were resolved with discussion among all three reviewers. A meta-analysis was considered where results were reported by 3 or more studies for psychometric tools that measure change in PTSD or SUD. Only the PCL-5 met these criteria, results for which were reported by six studies. Standardized mean change effect sizes (Hedges’ g ) were calculated using pre- and post-treatment PTSD symptom scores among participants receiving WET. Change-score standard deviations were derived from reported baseline and follow-up standard deviations using an assumed pre–post correlation of r = 0.50 when change-score standard deviations were not reported. Small-sample bias was corrected using Hedges’ adjustment. Random-effects meta-analysis models were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q , I 2 and τ 2 statistics. ### A tailored online mindfulness intervention for junior doctors: a mixed-methods study
**כתב עת:** 📄 BMC Med Educ | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Moore S et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Aug 12

Background Junior doctor training is stressful and associated with significant challenges, including poor mental health and burnout. Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) are increasingly being offered within junior doctor education programs to support junior doctor wellbeing, with online platforms for delivery attracting interest. Methods This mixed-method randomised waitlist control trial was conducted with junior doctors across two hospitals in Western Australia. The intervention was an 8-week online mindfulness training program, with usual training as control. Outcome measures were changes from baseline to program completion (eight weeks) and then one-month follow-up for self-reported surveys of trait mindfulness, perceived stress, self-compassion, occupational stress, and work engagement. Intervention and control participants completed surveys at all three time points. Semi-structured interviews and focus groups were conducted with participants at program completion. Results Seventy-two (35 intervention, 37 control) junior doctors were randomised. Seventeen did not complete the baseline measures. Data for the remaining 55 participants (28 Intervention, 27 Control) were retained for analysis. Most intervention participants formally practiced mindfulness two to three times per week for five to seven minutes however quality of formal and informal practice was low. For the intervention group, perceived stress increased at program completion (-1.4, 95% CI − 2.6, -0.3, p = 0.0151) while work engagement was decreased (-2.9, CI − 5.6, -0.2, p = 0.0355) at final follow-up. There were no significant changes in any of the outcomes for the control group at any follow-up point. Thirteen intervention participants undertook focus groups or individual interviews about their experience. Despite lack of positive quantitative findings, benefits reported regarding the program during interview included: improved self-awareness, emotion regulation, self-compassion and work productivity, as well as professional and personal relationships. Barriers to participation in the program included limited time, work stress, and challenges with elements of the intervention. Conclusion This novel online mindfulness training program was not associated with improvements in measured outcomes including trait-mindfulness, perceived stress, self-compassion, occupational stress or work engagement for participating junior doctors. This contrasts with our qualitative findings suggesting that junior doctors experience some wellbeing benefits from participating, and with the results of our similar trial in medical students. While the dose of mindfulness practice was the same for both trials, junior doctors’ adherence to practice was lower than medical students’ adherence. Organisational factors such as stressful workplaces and time limitations likely minimised the impact of the mindfulness intervention in this group. Burnout during early stages of junior doctor training has negative effects on trainees’ professional behaviour and impacts patient safety through poor communication and high medical error rates [ 7 – 9 ]. Further, personal impacts of burnout such as depression [ 10 , 11 ] and suicidal ideation [ 12 ] are worrisome, as many Australian doctors, particularly junior doctors, do not seek help due to concerns regarding privacy, confidentiality, feelings of humiliation, and fear of being reported to medical registration bodies [ 13 ]. Underlying reasons for junior doctor stress and burnout have been well documented in the literature and include poor work-life balance, anxieties about suboptimal practices and poor patient outcomes, high workloads and on-call commitments, low job satisfaction, poor career development and poor work environment [ 14 ]. In response to these significant problems for junior doctors worldwide, statements have been issued by national medical associations recommending evidence-based change at national, hospital, program and non-work levels to optimise junior doctor wellbeing. These organisations include the Collaborative for Healing and Renewal in Medicine (CHARM) - a group of experts in medical education and burnout interventions based in the United States [ 15 ], the British Medical Association in the United Kingdom [ 16 ] and the Australian Medical Association in Australia [ 17 ]. The list of wellbeing interventions recommended by most of these bodies, including CHARM, incorporate system-level changes as well as programs aimed at the individual, including the development and research of evidence-based mindfulness and resilience training for medical students and trainee doctors for best practice [ 15 ]. MBIs have also been found to increase the trait of self-compassion amongst non-clinical populations [ 20 , 21 ]. Higher levels of self-compassion are associated with lower levels of stress and more effective coping strategies [ 22 ], better emotion regulation in times of adversity [ 23 ] and protection from burnout [ 7 ]. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that mindfulness practice may improve engagement with work [ 24 , 25 ], and higher levels of work engagement are generally regarded as an antidote to burnout [ 26 ]. In line with the CHARM and other recommendations, MBIs have been introduced to medical school curricula around the world and their effectiveness for reducing psychological distress and promoting wellbeing in medical students has been documented [ 27 , 28 ]. MBIs have also been taken up by hospital medical education units to provide junior doctors with tools to manage their stress, optimise their wellbeing and reduce the risk of burnout. Quantitative studies evaluating these interventions have demonstrated improvements in wellbeing outcomes for junior doctors, including in objective measures such as trait-mindfulness, perceived stress, mood, self-compassion and burnout [ 29 – 34 ]. Qualitative and mixed-methods studies have also assessed MBIs delivered to junior doctors, reporting their utility for stress-management at work, improved self-care and enhanced relationships [ 30 , 35 – 38 ]. ### The impact of cognitive behavioral therapy and third-wave therapies on the quality of life of patients with obsessive-compulsive disorder: a systematic review and meta-analysis
**כתב עת:** 📄 BMC Psychol | **סוג מחקר:** מטה-אנליזה | **מחברים:** Wang Z et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Aug 14

Objective The aim of this study is to evaluate the impact of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and third-wave therapies on the quality of life of patients with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). Methods A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library databases, up until June 20, 2026, to identify randomised controlled trials (RCTs) that used quality of life as an outcome measure. Quality assessment was performed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool. Meta-analysis was conducted using Stata 18.0 software, and subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses and tests for publication bias were performed. Results A total of 13 randomised controlled trials ( N = 1,324) were included. The meta-analysis revealed that CBT and third-wave therapies improve quality of life in patients with OCD (SMD = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.29, P = 0.004), with no overall heterogeneity ( I² = 0.0%). The results of sensitivity analyses were stable, and the risk of publication bias was low. Subgroup analyses indicated that CBT and third-wave therapies were effective in improving quality of life in non-European countries, early studies (≤ 2020), short-term interventions (≤ 12 weeks), adult populations, standard CBT, third-wave therapies, active control groups, and subjective experience/satisfaction scales; however, statistical significance was not achieved in some subgroups. Since the differences between subgroups aren’t significant, the subgroup analysis is just exploratory, so the results should be interpreted with caution. Conclusion CBT and third-wave therapies can improve the quality of life of patients with OCD to a certain extent, but the overall effect is modest. Future research should be larger in scale, multi-center, and prospective. It’s also necessary to further clarify the mechanisms behind different intervention modules, optimize intervention plans, and improve long-term follow-up, so that we can provide stronger evidence to support clinical practice. Supplementary Information The online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05305-8 .

Introduction Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a mental disorder characterised by persistent intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive behaviours or mental acts (compulsions) [ 1 ]. Several multinational studies show that the lifetime prevalence of OCD in the general population is 4.1% [ 2 ], and the average age of onset for obsessive-compulsive symptoms in adult patients seeking treatment is 16.9 years [ 3 ], and it is listed as one of the top ten causes of disability worldwide [ 4 ]. Several studies [ 5 , 6 ] have confirmed that OCD significantly impairs patients’ quality of life, affecting a range of areas including social, occupational and family functioning, subjective well-being, and the ability to participate in leisure activities [ 5 ]. Furthermore, in the absence of effective intervention, OCD can develop into a chronic condition, significantly impairing patients’ quality of life [ 7 ]. Early detection and appropriate intervention for OCD can therefore alleviate individual suffering and reduce the public health burden associated with the condition [ 8 ]. Treatment for OCD primarily comprises two main categories: psychotherapy and medication [ 9 ]. Psychotherapy is usually recommended first because it’s safer and has better long-term effects [ 8 ]. Among various psychological interventions, cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), including exposure and response prevention (ERP), as a first-line treatment, has been widely proven to effectively relieve obsessive-compulsive symptoms and holds a central place in clinical guidelines [ 10 ]. CBT has evolved through the classical development of traditional behavioural and cognitive techniques, the widespread adoption of internet-based remote interventions, and the emergence of the third wave centred on acceptance and mindfulness, resulting in a diverse and more accessible system of interventions [ 11 – 15 ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that third-wave therapies helps to alleviate symptoms associated with OCD [ 16 ]; however, existing research has found that symptom relief does not necessarily equate to an improvement in quality of life [ 17 ]. Quality of life is a multidimensional, subjective outcome measure distinct from disease symptoms, encompassing various aspects such as physical well-being, emotional state, social interaction and daily functioning [ 18 , 19 ]. Furthermore, most previous reviews have focused their analysis solely on core symptoms, paying little attention to the important dimension of patients’ quality of life [ 20 , 21 ], and thus there are certain limitations to the research. Given the current state of research and the limitations of previous reviews, this study systematically reviewed relevant randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and employed meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of CBT in improving the quality of life of patients with OCD, compare differences in efficacy across different intervention settings, and focus on exploring the asynchronous nature of the improvement in obsessive-compulsive symptoms and the enhancement of quality of life. The aim is to supplement the evidence base for cognitive behavioural therapy interventions in OCD, refine the clinical efficacy evaluation system, and provide a scientific basis for optimising CBT intervention protocols, implementing targeted quality-of-life interventions, and improving patient prognosis. Methods The entire process of this systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the internationally recognised PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines. This research project has been registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261287363). Inclusion and exclusion criteria Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients diagnosed with OCD in accordance with internationally recognised diagnostic criteria (e.g. DSM-5, ICD-10/11); (2) Studies investigating the impact of psychological interventions—such as CBT, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, and acceptance and commitment therapy—on the quality of life of patients with OCD; (3) Study design: randomised controlled trial (RCT); (4) Outcome measures must relate to quality of life; (5) Studies must be in English. Exclusion criteria: (1) Studies where the population doesn’t primarily have OCD, or mixed samples where OCD patient outcome data can’t be extracted separately; (2) Studies including participants with serious mental disorders (like schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, substance use disorders) or neurological diseases; (3) Studies with missing relevant information or data; (4) Studies where the full text can’t be obtained or isn’t publicly available; (5) Duplicate publications. Search terms were formulated using a combination of MeSH subject headings and free text.

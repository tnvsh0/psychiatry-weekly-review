### Comparative efficacy and acceptability of antidepressants in the long-term treatment of major depressive disorder: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
**כתב עת:** ⭐ The Lancet Psychiatry (Lancet Psychiatry) *(IF: 64.3)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** van Haaren PCF et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 10

BACKGROUND: Major depressive disorder carries high risks of relapse and chronicity, necessitating treatments that achieve and maintain response (sustained response). This study aimed to compare antidepressants on their ability to achieve sustained response, and on acceptability, by synthesising data from randomised controlled trials.

METHODS: For this systematic review and network meta-analysis, we searched the Cochrane Register, AMED, CINAHL, Embase, LILACS, MEDLINE, MEDLINE In-Progress, PsycINFO, the UK National Research Register, and regulatory agency websites from inception to March 27, 2026. We included double-blind randomised controlled trials that compared any of 21 antidepressants head-to-head or with placebo for long-term treatment (>12 weeks) of adult unipolar major depressive disorder. We included enrichment, non-enrichment, continuation, and continuation-extension designs, and extracted study-level data. We could not derive the sex or gender distribution of participants other than female and did not extract data on race or ethnicity. Primary outcomes were efficacy (sustained response rate) and acceptability (all-cause discontinuation). We synthesised data in random-effects pairwise and network meta-analyses to estimate pooled odds ratios (ORs), using fluoxetine as a reference in the ranked forest plots. The primary analysis included head-to-head continuation and continuation-extension design studies. Enrichment design studies were incorporated in the analysis using a chain-of-evidence approach. Risk-of-bias assessment was done using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. No people with lived experience were involved in the design, conduct, or interpretation of this study.

FINDINGS: From 54 231 records, 115 studies (30 428 participants; mean age 45·3 years [SD 8·5], 20 066 [66%] female) were included. In head-to-head continuation and continuation-extension design studies (35 studies; n=9888), sustained response differed across antidepressants. Escitalopram (OR 1·62, 95% CI 1·21-2·18), duloxetine (OR 1·57, 1·08-2·26), and agomelatine (OR 1·40, 1·06-1·85) showed higher sustained response rates than fluoxetine. Bupropion and reboxetine showed lower sustained response rates against four other antidepressants in the league table of pairwise comparisons. Escitalopram (OR 0·60, 0·43-0·83) and duloxetine (OR 0·64, 0·44-0·95) demonstrated lower all-cause discontinuation compared with fluoxetine, while reboxetine and milnacipran showed higher all-cause discontinuation against six and four other antidepressants, respectively, in the league table of pairwise comparisons. Sensitivity analyses incorporating enrichment-design studies corroborated these results. However, the certainty of evidence of the network meta-analysis results and the risk of bias in the included studies (19 [54%] of 35 had high risk of bias) might limit the reliability of the current findings.

INTERPRETATION: Antidepressants can differ in their efficacy for achieving sustained response and in their acceptability. These findings can inform the shared decision making for patients, prescribers, and carers in real-world practice. However, more head-to-head comparative studies are needed to provide reliable information about long-term effects of all licensed antidepressants.

### Rethinking University Mental Health for the AI Era: From Crisis Response to Core Mission
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Psychiatry *(IF: 25.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Kessler RC, Ono SJ | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 11

IMPORTANCE: Universities have become frontline mental health care settings for adolescents and young adults, a group with rapidly rising rates of common psychiatric disorders. Conventional campus counseling services cannot meet this demand. Students are increasingly turning to unregulated generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools. There is an urgent need for an evidence-informed architecture that integrates digital tools and AI into university mental health systems safely, equitably, and at scale.

OBJECTIVE: To synthesize epidemiologic and clinical trial data on student mental health and digital interventions and integrate these findings with experience in institutional governance to propose an integrated, data-driven, stepped-care model for student mental health, implemented and governed at the university level, that begins with universal prevention, tracks need, and uses precision treatment selection to monitor and match students in need with the least-intensive effective level of care.

EVIDENCE REVIEW: We draw on findings from the World Mental Health International College Student (WMH-ICS) initiative, national surveys of student mental health and service use, randomized clinical trials of student internet-delivered digital interventions, implementation studies of digital navigators and task sharing, and emerging evaluations of AI-supported mental health systems.

FINDINGS: WMH-ICS studies find that 37% to 58% of first-year students worldwide meet 12-month criteria for a DSM-5 disorder, with low treatment rates and substantial impairment in academic performance. This unmet need exceeds the capacity of traditional short-term, face-to-face counseling services, even with expanded staffing and telehealth contracts. Hybrid stepped-care models combining universal prevention with light-touch human support (eg, digital navigators) and low-intensity digital interventions offer a more scalable approach that can expand reach, optimize use of clinicians' limited time, and improve outcomes. Building on these strands, we outline a 2-step system anchored in prematriculation life skills training and a guided first-year cohort model, embedded within a broader digital ecosystem that includes measurement, precision treatment algorithms, and explicit AI governance.

CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE: Reframing student mental health as core to the mission of universities justifies investment in digitally enabled care. With appropriate guardrails, AI can be integrated as a regulated component of this ecosystem, extending the reach of effective interventions while maintaining safety, equity, and institutional accountability.

### Clinical Risk Factors for Suicide in Persons With Epilepsy: A Nationwide Population-Based Case-Control Study
**כתב עת:** 📄 Neurology *(IF: 4.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Melin S et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Dec 8

BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Individuals with epilepsy face an elevated risk of suicide, but risk factors for suicide remain incompletely understood. We conducted a nationwide case-control study in Sweden to investigate clinical characteristics associated with suicide risk in persons with epilepsy.

METHODS: Persons with epilepsy were identified through Swedish nationwide registers, with epilepsy diagnoses validated through medical record review. Cases were individuals who died by suicide between 2006 and 2011, validated through autopsy reports, and controls were derived from the source population using similar validation procedures. Characteristics included epilepsy type, etiology, tonic-clonic seizure (TCS) frequency, antiseizure medication (ASM) treatment, living conditions, and psychiatric comorbidity. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were estimated using conditional logistic regression.

RESULTS: The study included 111 suicide cases and 1,147 controls. Suicide cases had a mean age at epilepsy onset of 20 years, and 35% were female. Alcohol abuse (OR 11.81, 95% CI 6.25-22.30) and other substance abuse (OR 10.71, 95% CI 4.52-25.38) showed stronger associations than depression (OR 2.94, 95% CI 1.56-5.53) and anxiety disorders (OR 2.93, 95% CI 1.57-5.44). Living alone was associated with increased risk (OR 2.59, 95% CI 1.58-4.26). Recent TCS showed a dose-response relationship, with the highest risk among individuals experiencing 4-10 TCS in the preceding year (OR 5.01, 95% CI 2.2-11.3). Structural and unknown epilepsy etiologies were associated with elevated risk (OR 3.07, 95% CI 1.0-9.5 and OR 8.80, 95% CI 2.9-27.1, respectively). ASM treatment was associated with lower suicide risk (monotherapy OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.27-0.95; polytherapy OR 0.21, 95% CI 0.1-0.5), and nonadherence was associated with higher risk (OR 3.02, 95% CI 1.8-5.1).

DISCUSSION: This study identified a distinct risk profile for suicide in persons with epilepsy, highlighting alcohol and substance abuse, living alone, seizure burden, nonadherence, and structural or unknown etiologies as key risk factors. The stronger associations for substance abuse may reflect characteristics specific to epilepsy populations and the focus on completed suicide. Screening for substance abuse and improved seizure control may contribute to suicide prevention. Major limitations include the modest number of cases and incomplete information on prior suicidal behavior.

### Reward reactivity in non-suicidal self-injury in adolescents: protocol for a longitudinal study
**כתב עת:** 📄 BMC Psychiatry | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Ma H et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Aug 18

Background The high incidence and serious consequences of non-suicidal self-injurious (NSSI) behaviors in adolescents warrants the attention of medical professionals, sociologists, and educators. Evidences of enhanced reward reactivity have been observed in adolescents with NSSI as well as those with suicidal risks. It is hypothesized that elevated reward reactivity toward general rewards may increase the risk of suicide. To test the hypothesis, a cohort study has been designed to systematically evaluate the reward reactivity in combination with other risk factors commonly reported in NSSI adolescents, with follow-up for at least 24 months. Methods This protocol is for a longitudinal cohort study that aims to enroll adolescents with NSSI and healthy controls at the Affiliated Kangning Hospital of Ningbo University. The primary outcome will be time to first documented suicide attempt during the 24-month follow-up, while psychological well-being and related clinical measures will be assessed as secondary outcomes. Clinical information, pathopsychological assessments and brief cognitive assessment will be collected at the baseline. Reward reactivity will primarily be tested in behavioral tasks under functional magnetic resonance imaging, accompanied with the assessment of the traits of reinforcement. Discussion This study proposes a highly integrative, multimodal investigation of the neurobiological and systemic inflammatory pathways underlying NSSI in Chinese adolescents with MDD, with a central focus on reward circuit dysfunction. Trial registration This study is registered in https://www.chictr.org.cn under protocol registration number ID: ChiCTR2500107121. It was registered on August 4, 2025. Recruitment started in September, 2023. The version of this protocol is v1.0.

Background Non-suicidal self-injury behaviors, previously known as deliberate self-mutilation, deliberate self-harm (DSH), or self-injurious behaviors [ 1 ], is globally increasing in recent decades. Half a century ago, literature about self-mutilation was usually related with certain genetic and developmental disorders [ 2 ]. During 1972 and 1973, incidence observed base on the Accident and Emergency Departments of hospitals was 0.17% in male and 0.66% in female aged 15–20 years in the City of Bristol, UK, 91% of which was taken the forms of drug overdose [ 3 ]. A review in 1983 [ 4 ] claimed that self-harm behaviors occurring in late adolescence were seen ‘among violent and antisocial youth in institutional settings’. However, when Klonsky and colleges [ 5 ] drew attention to the nonclinical population in 2003, they reported 4% percent of the participants with a history of DSH in a military recruiting sample of young people in the USA. This was one of the earliest surveys that raised NSSI as a prevalent problem. Meanwhile, a survey of a community sample in Quebec reported the prevalence of DSH as high as 13.9% in the year of 2002 [ 6 ]. Demographic surveys since then reported similar prevalence worldwide, around 17% to 21% in community samples in Finland [ 7 ], Belgium [ 8 ], and Israel [ 9 ]. In China, one of the earliest demographic studies about deliberate self-harm was carried out in 2006 in Hong Kong, which reported 13.4% and 19.7% prevalence rates in the male and female students of 9–12 years old, respectively [ 10 ]. Another study in mainland China [ 11 ] reported the rates of 22.7% and 22.6% in the male and female middle school students in 2011. Meanwhile, a most recent survey reported lifetime prevalence of NSSI among adolescents is as high as 28.7% [ 12 ]. The wide spread phenomenon of NSSI behaviors in adolescents warrants the attention of medical professionals, sociologists, and educators. NSSI as a medical term was established in the fifth version of the Statistical and Diagnostic Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) in the section of Emerging measures and models. NSSI is defined as the deliberate, self-inflicted destruction of body tissue without suicidal intent and for purposes not socially sanctioned, includes behaviors such as cutting, burning, biting and scratching skin [ 13 ]. It is usually co-morbid with depressive disorders. However, a recent survey in a community sample in China [ 14 ] found 32.1% of the adolescents who had self-injurious history were not depressive. Moreover, this cohort seemed to show more resilient features than their non-NSSI counterparts. On the other hand, NSSI associates closely with suicidal risks. Suicidal ideation and attempt were found in 42.2% and 13.8% adolescents with frequent NSSI behaviors, 8.0 and 12.2 times higher compared with those without these behaviors [ 15 ]. Few longitudinal researches about the developmental outcomes have been conducted. One of these studies in Sweden found two thirds of the adolescents stopped the self-injurious behaviors when reaching young adulthood. However, these young people still had elevated level of stress, anxiety and emotional dysregulation, though milder than those with prolonged NSSI behaviors [ 16 ]. Therefore, NSSI seems to be a developmental problem in emotionally unstable adolescents with significant elevated risks of suicide. NSSI adolescents and suicidal patients may differ from “purely” depressed ones in reward activity. Low reactivity to reward feedback was observed in depressed adolescents [ 17 , 18 ]. However, NSSI adolescents had increased putamen reactivity to monetary rewards [ 19 ], while suicidal young people had also relatively increased electroencephalogram(EEG) reactivity to rewards [ 20 ]. Similar to substance addiction, brain reactivity to visual cue was increased in NSSI subjects [ 21 ]. Painful stimuli evoked greater response in self-injurious subjects in several valence- related brain areas, and correlation between blood oxygen level dependent(BOLD) signal and relief was greater in NSSI patients in areas associated with reward/pain and addiction including thalamus, dorsal striatum and anterior precuneus [ 22 ]. The phenomenon of diverging reward reactivity in distressful adolescents suggests its potential application in behavioral prediction and intervention development. It is not clear whether the up-regulated reward reactivity is genetically determined or could be affected by the environment. Large twin surveys suggested that the correlation between NSSI and suicidal ideation could mostly be explained by overlapping genetic influences [ 23 , 24 ], while trauma experience may confer direct risk among females [ 24 ]. Several DNA methylation sites had been reported in NSSI adolescents with childhood adversity, such as the Glucocorticoid receptor gene [ 25 ] and catecholamine- O- methyl- transferase(COMT) [ 26 ]. Hence we propose the hypothesis that enhanced reactivity toward general rewards and rewarding pain may increase the risk of suicide in self-injurious patients. Both genetic and environmental factors could contribute to the altered reward reactivity. To test the hypothesis and hopefully to develop predictive models for suicidal risk, we will build a cohort with clinical NSSI adolescents. Reward reactivity is assessed with task-state fMRI. The reinforcement traits, the severity of addictive feature, the presence of game disorder and the anhedonic feelings are also evaluated with self-rating scales, in order to give a full profile of the reward system. Pathological manifestation as well as environment assessment is carried out for detailed risk assessment. This is the first longitudinal fMRI study to examine reward reactivity as a prospective predictor of suicide attempts in Chinese adolescents with NSSI. By integrating multimodal assessments within a specialized cohort and biobank, this study addresses a critical gap in the literature and provides a foundation for future risk stratification and intervention research. ### Trends and seasonal patterns in suicide and traffic accident mortality among youth in Hungary, 2000-2024: a nationwide population-based study
**כתב עת:** 📄 BMC Pediatr | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Urfi ZG et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Jul 29

Background External causes of death represent the leading causes of mortality among adolescents and young adults. This study aimed to investigate long-term, seasonal patterns, and regional disparities in mortality from external causes among individuals aged 15–24 years in Hungary between 2000 and 2024, including comparisons between the pre-pandemic (2000–2019) and pandemic (2020–2024) periods. Methods Nationwide data on monthly and annual mortality from external causes (ICD-10: V01–Y89) were obtained from the Dissemination Database of the Hungarian Central Statistical Office for 2000–2024. Analyses focused on the two leading causes of external mortality in this age group: suicide and self-harm (X60–X84, Y87.0) and traffic accidents (V01–V99). Mortality rates were calculated using corresponding population data stratified by sex, age group, and region. Annual and seasonal trends were analysed using negative binomial regression models with population offsets. Results are presented as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Results Between 2000 and 2024, 7,209 deaths from external causes were recorded among individuals aged 15–24 years (79% males). Significant declining trends were observed in overall external-cause mortality (IRR = 0.97; 95% CI: 0.96–0.97; p < 0.001), suicide (IRR = 0.98; 95% CI: 0.97–0.98; p < 0.001), and traffic accident mortality (IRR = 0.95; 95% CI: 0.94–0.95; p < 0.001). Seasonal variation was significant for overall external-cause mortality, with a peak in July ( p < 0.001), and for suicide and traffic accident mortality, peaking in May ( p < 0.001) and August ( p < 0.001), respectively. Regional disparities were pronounced, with the highest mortality rates consistently observed in the Southern Great Plain region. Mortality patterns during the COVID-19 period did not substantially deviate from pre-pandemic trends. Conclusions Although mortality from external causes among Hungarian youth declined significantly over the past 25 years, substantial gender and regional inequalities persist. The marked summer peak in traffic accident mortality and the elevated risk in specific regions highlight the need for targeted, seasonally sensitive prevention strategies. Background Hungary has long ranked among the countries with the highest mortality rates within the European Union [ 1 ]. Although cardiovascular diseases and cancers account for the majority of deaths in the total population, the pattern of mortality among adolescents and young adults differs substantially [ 2 ]. According to mortality data from the Hungarian Central Statistical Office (HCSO), external causes of death account for approximately 60% of deaths among individuals aged 15–24 years and traffic accidents and suicide together constitute nearly three-quarters of external-cause deaths in this age group [ 2 ]. External causes of death constitute a heterogeneous group of events, including unintentional injuries, intentional self-harm (suicide), assault, and injuries of undetermined intent, as classified by the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) [ 3 ]. Among young people, these deaths are particularly tragic because they occur at the beginning of the life course and are largely preventable. Beyond the immediate loss of life, they generate profound social, psychological, and economic consequences for families and communities. Understanding their temporal and spatial distribution is therefore of major public health importance. Suicide remains a significant public health concern worldwide and has consistently ranked among the leading causes of death in young people [ 4 , 5 ]. Hungary historically exhibited one of the highest suicide rates in Europe, although a long-term decline has been observed over recent decades [ 6 , 7 ]. Traffic accidents similarly represent a major source of premature mortality among youth [ 8 ], particularly among young males, who are more likely to engage in high-risk behaviours [ 9 ]. In the Hungarian population aged 15–24 years, suicide and traffic accidents constitute the two leading causes of external-cause mortality and together account for the majority of deaths from external causes [ 2 ]. Examining these causes in parallel may therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of preventable mortality patterns among Hungarian youth. Previous Hungarian studies have primarily focused on overall suicide trends or adult populations. Mortality patterns among children under 15 years have recently been analysed in detail, highlighting the predominance of traffic-related deaths in that younger age group [ 10 ]. To our knowledge, no study has yet extended a similarly comprehensive analysis to the 15–24-year age group, despite the distinct developmental, behavioural, and social characteristics of this life stage. Consequently, comprehensive investigations of long-term trends, seasonal variation, and regional disparities specifically in the 15–24-year age group remain limited. Moreover, little is known about how these patterns evolved during the COVID-19 pandemic period in Hungary. Given that the majority of deaths among individuals aged 15–24 years are attributable to external causes, a detailed examination of their annual trends, seasonal fluctuations, and territorial distribution is essential. The present nationwide population-based study, therefore, aimed to investigate mortality from all external causes, with particular emphasis on suicide and traffic accidents, among Hungarian youth between 2000 and 2024. In addition, we compared patterns observed during the pre-pandemic (2000–2019) and pandemic (2020–2024) periods to assess potential deviations associated with COVID-19-related societal changes. Causes of death were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) [ 3 ]. External causes were defined using codes V01–Y89. Within this category, the two most frequent causes in this age group – traffic accidents (V01–V99) and suicides and self-harm (X60–X84, Y87.0) – were analysed in detail. Monthly and annual numbers of deaths were retrieved stratified by sex, five-year age group (15–19 and 20–24 years), and European Union’s Nomenclature of Territorial Units for Statistics, level 2 (NUTS2) region. Corresponding annual population data were obtained from the same database. As official monthly population data for the 15–24-year age group were not available, monthly population denominators were estimated by interpolating between annual mid-year population figures and adjusting for monthly counts of births and deaths. This approach allowed approximation of the population at risk for seasonal analyses.

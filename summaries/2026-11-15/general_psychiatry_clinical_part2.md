### Dialogical therapy plus treatment as usual versus treatment as usual in adults with distressing and persistent auditory hallucinations in the UK (Talking with Voices-II): a randomised controlled trial
**כתב עת:** ⭐ The Lancet Psychiatry (Lancet Psychiatry) *(IF: 64.3)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Longden E et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 10

BACKGROUND: Existing treatments for auditory hallucinations (hereafter voice hearing) among people with serious mental health problems (SMHPs; diagnoses include schizophrenia spectrum disorders, bipolar disorders, and personality disorders) show only modest clinical benefits, produce inconsistent long-term outcomes, and seldom prioritise personal recovery as a primary treatment goal. We aimed to investigate the effectiveness of Talking with Voices (TwV), a lived-experience informed intervention in which therapists engage in direct dialogue with voices, in improving both personal recovery and clinical outcomes for individuals experiencing persistent and distressing voice hearing.

METHODS: Talking with Voices-II was a rater-blinded, parallel-arm, multisite, randomised controlled trial to test the efficacy and underlying mechanisms of TwV therapy compared with treatment as usual (TAU) in community-based National Health Service mental health services across four UK sites. Participants with SMHPs who experienced distressing voices were randomly assigned (1:1), using random-sized blocks, stratified by site and presence of schizophrenia diagnosis, to receive either TwV plus TAU or TAU alone. Therapy was provided in up to 26 sessions over 8 months, and assessments were conducted at baseline, 8 months, and 14 months by assessors masked to treatment allocation. The primary outcome was the Questionnaire about the Process of Recovery score at 8 months, a self-reported measure of personal recovery in psychosis. Secondary outcomes were voice-related measures, self-beliefs, dissociation, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms. The primary analysis followed the intention-to-treat principle, and moderation and mediation were prespecified in the statistical analysis plan. The mediation analysis was carried out using the mediate command, utilising bootstrapping to obtain percentile CIs. The trial was prospectively registered with ISRCTN, ISRCTN15897915, and all research and clinical procedures were co-led by individuals with lived experience of psychosis.

FINDINGS: Between Sept 1, 2023, and June 30, 2025, we screened 1316 participants, of whom 309 consented, completed baseline assessments, and were randomly assigned to either TwV plus TAU (n=156) or TAU alone (n=153). The mean age of participants was 41·1 years (SD 13·1). 159 (52%) participants were men, 145 (47%) were women, and four (1%) were another gender, and one preferred not to say; 222 (72%) participants were White. At 8 months, personal recovery was significantly better in the TwV group than in the TAU group (adjusted difference 6·97 [95% CI 5·00-8·95]; p<0·0001; standardised effect size [SES] 0·63). Significant differences favouring TwV were found for 12 (80%) of 15 of secondary outcomes, unadjusted for multiple testing, including all voice-related measures, negative self-beliefs, and PTSD outcomes, with SES ranging from 0·18 to 0·86. There were no effects on dissociation-related measures or positive self-beliefs (SES ranging from <0·01 to 0·16). An effect was still evident on personal recovery at 14 months, although reduced (adjusted difference 3·55 [0·96-6·14]; p=0·0073; SES=0·32). 55 serious adverse events occurred in 36 participants in the TwV group, and 60 serious adverse events occurred in 45 participants in the TAU group, none of which were deemed definitely related to the therapy or research procedures.

INTERPRETATION: TwV significantly improves personal recovery, showing efficacy for individuals with SMHPs who experience distressing voices. Further research comparing TwV with an active control and evaluating its implementation in routine care settings is warranted.

FUNDING: National Institute for Health and Care Research.

### Prolonged exposure, eye movement desensitization and reprocessing therapy, and cognitive restructuring versus waiting-list for individuals with PTSD and psychosis (RE.PROCESS): A multicenter, four-arm, assessor-masked, superiority randomized controlled trial
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Psychol Med *(IF: 6.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Burger SR et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 9

BACKGROUND: Evidence indicates the potential efficacy of trauma-focused therapies for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in psychosis. This is the first randomized controlled trial to evaluate the superiority of Prolonged Exposure (PE), Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing therapy (EMDR), and Cognitive Restructuring (CR) plus treatment as usual, compared to a waiting-list (WL) plus treatment as usual control group.

METHODS: We conducted a multicenter, assessor-masked, randomized controlled trial in patients with a lifetime schizophrenia spectrum psychotic disorder and PTSD. Participants ( N  = 162) were randomized (1:1:1:1) to PE, EMDR, CR, or WL and followed up until 6 months post-randomization. Therapy conditions had a maximum of 16 twice-weekly sessions. Primary outcome was researcher-rated PTSD symptom severity (Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5; CAPS-5).

RESULTS: PE, EMDR, and CR significantly reduced PTSD severity compared to WL over time from baseline to 6-month follow-up. At 6-months, adjusted differences and between-group effect sizes on the CAPS-5 were -9.76 (95% CI -16.13,-3.38;  p = 0.0029) with  d = -0.90 (95% CI -1.36,-0.44) for PE, -11.74 (95% CI -18.04,-5.44;  p = 0.0003) with  d = -1.04 (95% CI -1.50,-0.58) for EMDR, and -12.72 (95% CI -18.89,-6.55;  p = 0.0001) with  d = -1.00 (95% CI -1.46,-0.54) for CR. Significant therapy effects were also found for secondary PTSD (-related) outcomes, but not for psychosis.

CONCLUSIONS: In comparison to WL, PE, EMDR, and CR were effective in reducing PTSD symptoms in individuals with PTSD and psychosis, with large effects. Replication in larger samples is needed, although these results support expanding access to trauma therapies in this underserved population.

### Conceptualization and Management of Suicide Risk in Individuals with a History of Traumatic Brain Injury
**כתב עת:** 📄 Phys Med Rehabil Clin N Am | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Boffa JW et al. | **תאריך:** 2027 Feb

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is associated with greater functional impairment, psychiatric symptoms, and suicide risk. Routine suicide prevention efforts are essential for TBI care. Screening supports risk assessment and guides steps such as safety planning and lethal means counseling. Risk management should be adjusted for TBI sequelae by using direct language, visual aids, relating questions to TBI symptoms, and obtaining collateral information. Research on suicide interventions specific to TBI remains limited, with few targeted trials and primarily small, non-generalizable samples, underscoring the need for rigorous evaluation of interventions for this population.

### Staff Experiences Delivering Hospital Outreach Post-Suicidal Engagement (HOPE) in a Regional Context: A Qualitative Study
**כתב עת:** 📄 Int J Ment Health Nurs | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Modderman C et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Dec

ABSTRACT Suicide rates remain disproportionately high in regional Australia, where geographical, workforce and service‐system constraints can limit access to post‐suicidal care. This study explored staff experiences of delivering the Hospital Outreach Post‐Suicidal Engagement (HOPE) program across three regional hospital sites in Victoria, Australia. Semi‐structured individual interviews and one focus group were conducted with 16 HOPE staff, including nurses, peer workers, psychosocial support staff and allied health professionals. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, with reporting guided by the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ) checklist. Four themes were identified: regional influences on HOPE service delivery; adaptations to individual consumer needs; workforce capacity and teamwork; and HOPE's integration with the broader healthcare system. Staff valued HOPE's multidisciplinary and person‐centred approach but identified barriers associated with geographical distance, infrastructure, workforce capacity and fragmented service pathways. The findings demonstrate how regional context shapes the delivery of post‐suicidal care and highlight the importance of adequately resourced, flexible and integrated service models. These findings have implications for multidisciplinary HOPE practice, workforce planning and continuity of post‐suicidal care across regional communities.

1 Introduction Suicide remains a major public health concern globally and in Australia, where 3214 suicide deaths were recorded in 2023, representing a rate of 11.8 per 100 000 people (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2026 ). Suicide rates are higher in many regional, rural and remote communities than in metropolitan areas (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2026 ). In this study, the term ‘regional’ is used to describe non‐metropolitan locations (Department of Health 2026 ). People living in regional communities may experience reduced access to mental health services, longer travel distances, workforce shortages, stigma, social isolation and increased availability of lethal means (Casant and Helbich 2022 ; Houston 2024 ; Kavanagh et al. 2023 ). These challenges can affect both the immediate crisis responses and continuity of care following a suicidal crisis. Addressing suicide therefore requires approaches that respond to the psychological, social and structural factors influencing a person's wellbeing (Modderman, Veresova, McAleer, et al. 2026b ). A previous suicide attempt is one of the strongest predictors of future suicidal behaviour (Beghi et al. 2013 ), highlighting the importance of post‐suicidal care. Evidence suggests that effective post‐suicidal care services are integrated with broader health and community service systems, commence engagement as early as possible, and provide flexible, assertive and clinically supported follow‐up that is informed by the perspectives of people with lived experience. Although lived‐experience involvement is increasingly recognised as important in suicide prevention and post‐crisis support, research examining how it is actively incorporated into these services remains limited (Martin et al. 2023 ; Purdon et al. 2025 ). Despite the importance of post‐suicidal care, less is known about how these services are experienced by staff in regional settings. Regional service delivery may be shaped by geographical distance, local workforce capacity, infrastructure, referral pathways and the availability of complementary services. Therefore, further research is needed to understand the contextual and organisational factors that enable or constrain the delivery of post‐suicidal care in regional communities. 2 Background The Hospital Outreach Post‐Suicidal Engagement (HOPE) program was established in Victoria, Australia, with funding from the Victorian Government as part of the Suicide Prevention Framework 2016–2025 (Department of Health 2016 ). HOPE is a community‐based post‐suicidal care service that provides intensive, short‐term support for up to 12 weeks following a suicidal crisis. Multidisciplinary HOPE teams include mental health professionals and peer support workers with lived experience. They work collaboratively with consumers to deliver flexible, tailored, person‐centred support (Better Place Australia 2025 ). The HOPE model is designed to address the complex and interconnected needs that may follow a suicidal crisis. Support may include psychological and psychiatric care, housing assistance, financial counselling, substance‐use services, family‐violence support, employment assistance and cultural services (Better Place Australia 2025 ). HOPE has been widely implemented across Victoria, and a growing body of research has examined its outcomes (Kehoe et al. 2023 ; Williamson et al. 2021 ; Wright et al. 2021 ; Innes et al. 2026 ). Studies involving adults have reported reductions in suicidal ideation and psychological distress, as well as improvements in coping self‐efficacy, hopeful thinking and overall wellbeing (Kehoe et al. 2023 ; Williamson et al. 2021 ; Wright et al. 2021 ; Innes et al. 2026 ). Young people who have accessed HOPE have also reported reduced suicide risk and increased quality of life (Meyer et al. 2024 ), while parents have described the program positively (Webb et al. 2025 ). HOPE engagement has additionally been associated with reduced reliance on emergency departments and other hospital services (Wright et al. 2021 ). Existing research indicates that the characteristics and practices of HOPE staff are central to the delivery of the program (Kehoe et al. 2023 ; Williamson et al. 2021 ; Wright et al. 2021 ; Innes et al. 2026 ). However, previous research has focused primarily on consumer, carer and service outcomes, with limited attention to staff experiences of delivering HOPE. In addition, existing evaluations have largely been conducted in metropolitan settings (Kehoe et al. 2023 ; Williamson et al. 2021 ; Wright et al. 2021 ; Innes et al. 2026 ). These findings may not fully reflect the geographical, workforce, infrastructure and service‐system conditions that shape post‐suicidal care in regional communities. Consequently, little is known about how regional context and organisational conditions shape the delivery of HOPE. This study aimed to explore staff experiences of delivering the HOPE program across three regional hospital sites in Victoria, Australia. Specifically, it examined how geographical, organisational, workforce and service‐system factors shaped the delivery of responsive, person‐centred and continuous post‐suicidal care. The study also explored how collaboration between HOPE teams and other health and community services enabled or constrained care delivery in regional settings. The research question was: What are the experiences of staff delivering the HOPE program in regional settings? 3 Methods 3.1 Study Design A qualitative descriptive design was used to explore staff experiences of delivering the HOPE program across three regional hospital sites in Victoria, Australia. This design was appropriate because the study aimed to produce a clear, practise‐oriented account of staff experiences and of the organisational, geographical, workforce and service‐system conditions that shaped care delivery. Such an account can inform service planning by showing how these conditions affect the delivery of post‐suicidal care in practice. Qualitative description also supported analysis that remained close to participants' language, while recognising that the findings reflect interpretation within the social, organisational and temporal context in which the data were generated (Kim et al. 2017 ; Furidha 2023 ). Reporting was guided by the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ) checklist (Tong et al. 2007 ).

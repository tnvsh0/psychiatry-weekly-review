### Theoretical frameworks of extended reality interventions for eating and weight disorders and beyond: A systematic umbrella review
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Clinical Psychology Review (Clin Psychol Rev) *(IF: 12.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Korbanka TA et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 4

BACKGROUND: Extended reality (XR) is increasingly used in mental health treatment, including eating and weight disorders (EWD), due to its potential to target disorder-relevant mechanisms in immersive environments. This umbrella review synthesizes theoretical frameworks guiding XR-based psychotherapeutic applications. Our findings have also implications for other mental health disorders as they provide a framework on transdiagnostic mechanisms of XR-based interventions.

METHODS: Systematic searches of PubMed, PsycINFO, and Cochrane identified 44 review articles on XR-based psychotherapeutic interventions in EWD, including systematic and non-systematic reviews. Methodological quality was assessed using AMSTAR 2 and SANRA. The umbrella review was conducted and reported in accordance with PRISMA 20 guidelines.

RESULTS: The majority of reviews (n = 37) reported underlying theoretical frameworks with varying depth and quality. Our synthesis organizes these into a two-level taxonomy comprising first-order therapeutic mechanisms (behavioral, intersectional, and cognitive) and second-order XR-specific modulators (presence, embodiment, and the Proteus Effect).

CONCLUSIONS: XR appears to enhance established psychological mechanisms rather than generate novel therapeutic processes. A mechanism-oriented framework separating therapeutic mechanisms from XR-specific modulators may guide the development of more targeted and clinically precise XR interventions. Further experimental research is needed to validate mechanisms of action and optimize XR applications in EWD and beyond.

### Cognitive Behavioural Therapy to Optimize Post-Operative Fracture Recovery (COPE): a randomized controlled trial
**כתב עת:** 📄 CMAJ | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Busse JW et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 9

Background: Persistent pain after extremity fracture requiring surgical repair is common and associated with long-term morbidity. We sought to determine whether app-based cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) reduces persistent postsurgical pain after fracture repair. Methods: We performed a randomized controlled trial of adults from 11 American and Canadian hospitals with an extremity fracture treated with internal fixation. We allocated participants 1:1 to an app-based CBT program or usual care, and followed them at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months post-fracture. Our primary outcome was moderate-to-severe persistent post-surgical pain. We constructed a mixed-effects log-binomial model to explore the effect of CBT versus usual care on moderate-to-severe persistent post-surgical pain over 12 months postfracture. Results: We randomized 1009 participants to app-based CBT ( n = 506) or usual care ( n = 503). The mean age of participants was 44.5 years, and 129 (12.8%) presented with an open fracture. Adherence to CBT was modest, with 237/506 patients (46.8%) completing all 7 modules. App-based CBT, compared with usual care, did not reduce the risk of persistent postsurgical pain over 12 months postfracture (30.8% v. 30.3%, adjusted relative risk 1.02, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.89 to 1.16). The 365-day probability of returning to leisure activities without restrictions was 29.0% in the intervention group and 22.4% in the control group (hazard ratio 1.33, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.77). The other secondary outcomes showed no significant differences between treatments. Interpretation: Among adults with an operatively managed extremity fracture, app-based CBT did not reduce the risk of persistent postsurgical pain over 12 months. Future research is warranted to develop more effective CBT approaches for adults with extremity fractures and to evaluate alternative supportive interventions that may be more acceptable to patients with fracture. Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov , NCT04274530

Background Nursing students encounter acute anxiety during examinations, simulations, clinical skills training, and other health-related student contexts. We evaluated music-based interventions, with acute or short-term self-reported anxiety as the principal outcome and other psychological, physiological, health, and educational outcomes as secondary domains. Methods Following peer review, we repeated searches in PubMed/MEDLINE, CINAHL Complete, Embase, APA PsycINFO, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), the Web of Science Core Collection, and ClinicalTrials.gov from their inception to 19 September 2026, without outcome, study design, publication type, or language limits. Two reviewers independently screened, extracted, and assessed the risk of bias. The principal meta-analysis used a uniform post-intervention Hedges’ g from each eligible, individually randomized parallel-group trial. Random-effects models were estimated using Restricted Maximum Likelihood or Residual Maximum Likelihood (REML); Wald confidence intervals were the primary estimate, and Hartung–Knapp and prediction intervals were used to assess small-sample uncertainty. Results Of the 737 records identified, 337 duplicates were removed, and 400 records were screened. A total of 64 reports were sought; 39 unique complete documents were assessed; 26 reports representing 24 studies were included; and 13 full-text reports were excluded. Twenty studies were randomized, and four were non-randomized. Nine parallel-group trials (650 participants) contributed to the principal anxiety synthesis ( g = −0.31, 95% confidence interval (CI): −0.58 to −0.04; p = 0.024; I 2 = 64%; τ 2 = 0.107). The Hartung–Knapp interval (from −0.63 to 0.01; p = 0.055) and the 95% prediction interval (from −1.15 to 0.53) crossed zero. All nine principal trials were at high overall risk of bias, and certainty was very low. Conclusion Music-based interventions may reduce acute or short-term self-reported anxiety on average. However, the results are sensitive to the method used to quantify uncertainty, and the effects are likely to vary across settings. Evidence is insufficient to infer sustained psychological benefits, improved learning, or support for routine implementation. Systematic review registration The review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251274752). Public record URL: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251274752 .

Nursing students encounter examinations, simulations, invasive-skills training, and early clinical placements while managing workloads, responsibilities, observations, and fears of making errors ( 2–5 ). Anxiety and related distress are common across nursing programs ( 6–9 ). Support for students’ mental health is therefore relevant to both their participation in training and the future nursing workforce. In this review, music-based intervention is an umbrella term, not a claim of equivalence. We distinguish passive music listening or music medicine, active music making, and therapist-delivered music therapy. The last category requires a structured therapeutic intervention delivered by a music therapist; researcher- or educator-delivered listening is not labeled as formal music therapy ( 10 , 11 ). We also identify trials in which music was added to a shared co-intervention, such as aromatherapy, because these comparisons estimate only the incremental contribution of music. Music may redirect attention away from threat cues, support emotion regulation, increase perceived control when choice is offered, and alter autonomic arousal ( 12–17 ). The same attentional mechanism may also impose cognitive load or obscure task-relevant auditory information. Listening before a stressor may therefore differ from listening during simulation, skills performance, assessment, or clinical activity, where distraction could impair instruction, communication, alarms, or performance. These mechanisms were proposed more often than they were measured across the included studies. Earlier reviews addressed test anxiety and performance in nursing students ( 18 , 19 ). A 2026 meta-analysis across health sciences programs included 24 nursing-student reports ( 20 ). Other psychological, physiological, student health, and educational outcomes were secondary domains and were not interpreted as evidence that reduced anxiety improves competence or sustains mental health. ### Effects of Group-Based CBT/ERP and ACT on Emotion Dysregulation, Sleep, Anxiety Sensitivity and Psychological Flexibility in Adults With OCD
**כתב עת:** 📄 Clin Psychol Psychother | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Nielsen SKK et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov-Dec

ABSTRACT Introduction Cognitive behavioural therapy with exposure and response prevention (CBT/ERP) is the recommended first‐line psychological treatment for obsessive‐compulsive disorder (OCD). Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) has emerged as a promising alternative, yet treatment comparisons have primarily focused on symptom severity and even less is known about their effects on other domains. Study Aim We examined the effect of group‐based ACT and CBT/ERP on emotion regulation, anxiety sensitivity, insomnia and psychological flexibility in adults with OCD. Methods 176 adults with OCD were randomized to CBT/ERP or ACT and assessed with the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS), Sleep Condition Indicator (SCI), Anxiety Sensitivity Index‐Revised (ASI‐R) and Acceptance and Action Questionnaire for Obsessions and Compulsions (AAQ‐OC) at baseline, post‐treatment, 6‐month and 12‐month follow‐up and analysed with Mixed‐Model Repeated Measures (MMRM). Results Both treatments showed similar improvements in the four domains across timepoints. Conclusions In line with our hypotheses, our results indicated that ACT may be non‐inferior to CBT/ERP for all secondary outcomes. However, as the trial was powered for the primary outcome (symptom severity) rather than for these secondary measures, our findings should be interpreted as suggestive. Nonetheless, these findings add to the growing evidence that ACT may be considered a viable treatment for OCD.

Practitioner Points Group‐based ACT and CBT/ERP produced comparable improvements in emotion regulation difficulties, sleep quality, anxiety sensitivity and psychological flexibility, providing preliminary support for non‐inferiority under the chosen margin across these secondary outcomes. The findings suggest that both ACT and CBT/ERP may have benefits extending beyond reductions in core OCD symptoms, including improvements in broader psychological difficulties that commonly affect patients with OCD. Our results provide additional support for ACT being a viable treatment for OCD.

1 Introduction Obsessive‐compulsive disorder (OCD) is a chronic and potentially disabling mental disorder affecting approximately 1%–3% of the population worldwide (Santomauro et al. 2026 ). OCD is associated with substantial impairment across multiple areas of life and symptoms often persist without adequate treatment (Öst et al. 2015 ). Meta‐analytic evidence shows that cognitive behavioural therapy including exposure and response prevention (CBT/ERP) is an effective treatment for OCD, with large effects relative to waitlist and placebo conditions (Reid et al. 2021 ). Accordingly, CBT/ERP is recommended as a first‐line psychological treatment in clinical guidelines (NICE 2005 ). CBT/ERP helps patients systematically confront obsession‐related triggers while refraining from compulsions and other neutralizing behaviours, thereby facilitating new learning and reducing reliance on avoidance and ritualized responses (Foa 2010 ). Nevertheless, not all patients benefit sufficiently from treatment or complete it. A meta‐analysis reported pooled rates of response and clinically significant change of 65.0% and 50.0%, respectively, for ERP, although definitions varied across studies. Pooled dropout rates were 15.5% for CBT, 19.1% for ERP and 32.0% for ERP/CBT combined with antidepressant medication (Öst et al. 2015 ). These limitations highlight the need to evaluate alternative treatment approaches. Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) has emerged as a potentially effective treatment for OCD (Evey and Steinman 2023 ). Rather than primarily targeting the content or frequency of intrusive thoughts, ACT aims to encourage value‐based living, change how individuals relate to internal experiences and strengthen psychological flexibility (Twohig et al. 2006 ). Although studies indicate that ACT can reduce OCD symptom severity, relatively few trials have compared ACT directly with CBT/ERP (Evey and Steinman 2023 ). In their systematic review, Evey and Steinman ( 2023 ) included 17 studies on ACT, with 8 of them including a control condition and 1 comparing ACT combined with ERP to ERP. Recently, group‐based ACT was found to be non‐inferior to group‐based CBT/ERP in reducing OCD symptoms (Nielsen, Stuart, Winding, Øllgaard, et al. 2025 ). Treatment outcomes in studies of CBT/ERP and ACT for OCD have predominantly been evaluated using measures of OCD symptom severity, particularly the Yale–Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y‐BOCS) (Goodman et al. 1989 ). OCD is indeed also characterized by sleep disturbance, emotion regulation difficulties, anxiety sensitivity and psychological inflexibility. These domains have been associated with the severity or maintenance of OCD (Bluett et al. 2014 ; Olatunji and Wolitzky‐Taylor 2009 ; Paterson et al. 2013 ; See et al. 2022 ) and may remain clinically relevant even when obsessive‐compulsive symptoms improve. We therefore included sleep disturbance, emotion regulation difficulties, anxiety sensitivity and psychological inflexibility to assess clinically relevant difficulties and theoretically relevant dimensions of functioning that are not fully captured by measures of obsessive‐compulsive symptom severity. Together, they allow us to examine whether the benefits of treatment extend beyond reductions in obsessions and compulsions. Within this expectancy model, such interpretations amplify fear responses to bodily sensations and other anxiety‐related experiences and repeated amplification of fear is thought to maintain anxiety over time (Olatunji and Wolitzky‐Taylor 2009 ). Compulsions can temporarily reduce distress, thereby reinforcing avoidance behaviours and limiting opportunities to develop more adaptive responses to unwanted emotions. ### The Effects of Mindfulness-Based Programs on Pain-Related Cognitions in Individuals With Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
**כתב עת:** 📄 Pain Res Manag | **סוג מחקר:** מטה-אנליזה | **מחברים:** Barnet-Hepples T et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

Objectives Chronic musculoskeletal pain is a complex condition influenced by psychological factors, including pain‐related cognitions. This systematic review and meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials aimed to evaluate the effects of mindfulness‐based programs on pain‐related cognitions in individuals with chronic musculoskeletal pain. Secondary outcomes included depression, anxiety, psychological distress, pain intensity, disability, intervention adherence, and mindfulness‐related adverse events. Methods Eight databases were searched from inception to January 2026. Evidence was synthesized using random‐effects models and reported as standardized mean differences with 95% confidence intervals. Risk of bias was assessed using the revised Cochrane risk of bias tool. Evidence certainty was rated using GRADE. Results Fifty‐one studies ( n = 5997) were included and had a moderate‐to‐high risk of bias. Compared to no intervention, mindfulness‐based programs reduced pain catastrophizing (SMD: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.56–1.45) and increased pain acceptance (SMD: 1.12, 95% CI: 0.31–1.92) post‐intervention. They also reduced depression (SMD: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.50–1.38), anxiety (SMD: 1.08, 95% CI: 0.57–1.59), psychological distress (SMD: 0.75, 95% CI: 0.12–1.38), pain intensity (SMD: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.38–1.23), and disability (SMD: 0.48, 95% CI: 0.04–0.92) compared to no intervention. Minimal significant differences were observed between the effect of mindfulness‐based programs and cognitive behavioral therapy on these outcomes. Discussion Mindfulness‐based programs appear to be an effective self‐management strategy for individuals with chronic musculoskeletal pain. However, the certainty of evidence is mostly low with intervention adherence varying. Future research should clarify the direction and nature of the relationship between adherence and outcomes. Reporting of mindfulness‐related adverse events was insufficient to evaluate safety; future trials should incorporate comprehensive measurement.

1. Introduction Chronic musculoskeletal pain can profoundly impact psychological well‐being, increasing the risk of mental health disorders such as depression and anxiety [ 1 – 6 ]. In turn, psychological factors such as stress [ 2 , 7 , 8 ], depression [ 9 , 10 ], and anxiety [ 11 ] can contribute to the onset and persistence of chronic musculoskeletal pain. This affective distress is associated with increased pain intensity and worse physical function [ 12 , 13 ]. There is also substantial evidence that other psychological factors, particularly pain‐related cognitions, contribute to the trajectory of chronic musculoskeletal pain. Pain‐related cognitions are the thoughts, beliefs, and attitudes individuals hold regarding their pain experience. Maladaptive pain‐related cognitions such as pain catastrophizing and kinesiophobia can exacerbate pain and disability in people with chronic musculoskeletal pain [ 14 – 18 ]. Conversely, positive cognitions such as pain acceptance and pain self‐efficacy are associated with lower pain intensity and better physical function [ 19 – 21 ]. Further, several studies suggest that among people with comorbid chronic musculoskeletal pain and depression or anxiety, pain‐related cognitions can mediate the bidirectional relationship between these conditions [ 22 – 24 ]. Addressing psychological factors within chronic musculoskeletal pain management is crucial [ 25 – 29 ]. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is widely considered the gold‐standard psychological treatment for depression and anxiety [ 30 , 31 ] and is the main psychological intervention recommended in chronic musculoskeletal pain management guidelines [ 25 , 26 ]. However, barriers to widespread implementation persist. CBT is typically delivered by mental health professionals, such as psychologists and psychiatrists, yet growing workforce shortages and rising demand have limited service availability [ 32 – 34 ]. Moreover, the conventional one‐on‐one format of CBT is resource‐intensive and more costly than group‐based approaches [ 35 ]. Additional scalable, accessible, and cost‐effective options are needed. Mindfulness‐based programs have emerged as one potential alternative for addressing psychological well‐being in individuals with chronic musculoskeletal pain [ 36 , 37 ]. Practicing mindfulness involves paying attention, on purpose, as nonjudgmentally and open‐heartedly as possible, to the present moment—to what one is experiencing in their body, their mind, and the world around them [ 38 ]. Mindfulness‐based programs involve sustained, intensive training in mindfulness meditation, complemented by inquiry—a reflective practice that employs questioning to examine one’s experiences with curiosity and nonjudgment and distinguish direct experiences from habitual reactions and conceptions. They are thought to reduce the stress associated with living with a chronic condition by fostering decentering and equanimity—defined as even‐mindedness in the face of challenging experiences—and by enhancing emotional and behavioral self‐regulation [ 39 ]. Various mindfulness‐based programs are described in the literature; the two most widely adopted are mindfulness‐based stress reduction (MBSR) and mindfulness‐based cognitive therapy (MBCT) [ 40 ]. Hundreds of studies have investigated the health impacts of mindfulness across various populations [ 36 , 40 , 41 ]. Among people with chronic musculoskeletal pain, they have been shown in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to positively influence pain‐related cognitions [ 42 – 44 ], reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety [ 45 ], decrease perceptions of pain severity, and improve physical function [ 46 ]. However, findings across studies have been inconsistent. The rapid growth of research and inconsistent findings in this area present challenges for clinicians and people living with chronic musculoskeletal pain by contributing to uncertainty in decision‐making and creating barriers to evidence‐based practice. While several reviews have endeavored to synthesize the evidence for the impact of mindfulness among people with chronic pain, important limitations remain. First, no reviews have specifically focused on the effect of mindfulness‐based programs on pain‐related cognitions, despite their known relevance to clinical outcomes. Additionally, limited attention has been given to intervention adherence and mindfulness‐related adverse events (MRAEs). Second, many reviews examining important outcomes—including psychological well‐being, pain intensity, and disability—have been narrow in scope, focusing on a single pain condition such as chronic back pain or rheumatoid arthritis [ 47 – 52 ], or overly broad, including interventions with minimal mindfulness content such as acceptance and commitment therapy [ 53 – 55 ]. Third, as advised in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [ 56 ], the inclusion of nonrandomized studies in several reviews increases the risk of bias, potentially leading to inaccurate conclusions [ 50 , 54 , 57 , 58 ]. Finally, many meta‐analyses have pooled heterogeneous comparators, for instance, passive and active controls [ 51 , 59 – 61 ] or diverse active controls [ 58 , 59 , 62 ], limiting the precision and interpretability of the findings. A rigorous synthesis of evidence addressing these limitations is needed to clarify the effects of mindfulness‐based programs in people with chronic musculoskeletal pain and to inform clinical practice. ### Depression in autism spectrum disorder: a scoping review of pharmacologic, neuromodulation, and psychosocial interventions
**כתב עת:** 📄 Front Psychiatry | **סוג מחקר:** סקירה שיטתית | **מחברים:** Ghumman U et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

Introduction Major depressive disorder is common in autism spectrum disorder, but diagnosis and treatment are complicated by symptom overlap, atypical presentations, alexithymia, and limited ASD-specific evidence. Methods We conducted a scoping review of PubMed, PsycINFO, and reference lists for studies published from 1995 through 2025 examining pharmacologic, neuromodulation, and psychosocial interventions for depressive symptoms in ASD. Results Psychosocial interventions, including autism-adapted cognitive behavioral therapy, mindfulness-based therapy, dialectical behavior therapy-informed approaches, behavioral activation, PEERS, exercise, and animal-assisted therapy, were associated with improvements in depressive symptoms in open-label studies and small randomized trials. Neuromodulation findings were preliminary, while evidence for electroconvulsive therapy and deep brain stimulation was primarily case-based. Pharmacologic recommendations were based largely on expert consensus and indirect evidence rather than ASD-specific controlled trials. Discussion Depression treatment in ASD remains understudied. Larger controlled trials using ASD-validated depression measures, broader representation across support needs, and assessment of functional and long-term outcomes are needed.

1 Introduction Autism spectrum disorder (ASD), defined by social communication differences, restricted/repetitive behaviors, and sensory processing variations, affects almost 1 in 31 children, with prevalence rising globally ( 1 ). Psychiatric comorbidities are common in ASD, with Simonoff et al. reporting that 70% of children had at least one comorbid disorder and 41% had two or more. Joshi et al. demonstrated a substantial psychiatric comorbidity burden in psychiatrically referred youth with ASD. Depressive disorders are also prevalent, with pooled lifetime estimates of 14.4% in ASD overall and 37% in adults with ASD. ( 2 – 5 ). Major depressive disorder (MDD) manifests as persistent low mood, anhedonia, worthlessness, and cognitive/somatic symptoms, driving individual morbidity, relational strain, and more than $300B annual U.S. economic costs ( 6 , 7 ). In individuals with ASD, social adversities amplify risk: bullying, employment barriers, social isolation, and loneliness, all recognized contributors to depression ( 8 – 11 ). Yet evidence remains fragmented. Prior studies exhibit methodological heterogeneity (e.g., age, unvalidated scales, diagnostic overshadowing), likely underestimating burden. No standardized treatment pathway exists; clinicians extrapolate from neurotypical data despite sparse ASD-specific evidence across pharmacologic, psychosocial, and neuromodulation domains ( 5 , 12 ). Throughout this review, the term “individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD)” is used for consistency, while recognizing that both person-first and identity-first language are preferred by different members of the autism community. This PRISMA-ScR-guided scoping review synthesizes evidence from PubMed, PsycINFO, and reference list searches from 1995 to 2025 on pharmacologic, neuromodulation, and psychosocial treatments for depressive symptoms in individuals with ASD, with the goal of providing clinicians, patients, and families with a clearer overview of available options and the strength of the supporting evidence. 2 Materials and methods 2.1 Study design We conducted a scoping review of pharmacologic, neuromodulation, and psychosocial interventions for depressive symptoms in ASD guided by the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) framework ( 13 ). Given the limited number of ASD-specific depression intervention studies, the review also included indirect or clinically relevant evidence from ASD studies targeting related psychiatric symptoms (e.g., anxiety, emotional dysregulation, irritability) when these interventions are commonly used or recommended in the management of depression in ASD. 2.2 Data sources and search strategy We searched PubMed and PsycINFO databases for studies published between December 1, 1995, and December 1, 2025. Search terms combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords related to autism spectrum disorder (‘autism spectrum disorder,’ ‘autism,’ ‘Asperger syndrome’), depression (‘major depressive disorder,’ ‘depression,’ ‘depressive symptoms’), and intervention-related study designs (‘clinical trial,’ ‘randomized controlled trial,’ ‘intervention’). Reference lists of included primary studies and relevant systematic reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible primary studies. Records identified through manual reference-list screening were imported into Covidence along with records retrieved from the electronic database searches. Duplicate records were removed in Covidence prior to title and abstract screening. Only articles published in English were included. PubMed and PsycINFO were selected because they provide broad coverage of biomedical and mental health literature relevant to ASD and depression. To improve study identification, reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were also manually screened for additional eligible articles. Because the original database-specific search strings were not retained, a PubMed search strategy was reconstructed based on the documented methodology and scope of the review and is provided in the Supplementary Material. This reconstructed strategy is intended to provide readers with a more concrete representation of the search approach and should not be interpreted as the original search string. 2.3 Eligibility criteria Inclusion criteria comprised peer-reviewed articles reporting clinical outcome data in participants of any age with a confirmed ASD diagnosis (based on ADOS/ADI-R, DSM/ICD criteria, or clinical judgment). Eligible studies included interventions targeting depressive symptoms, major depressive disorder, internalizing symptoms, emotional dysregulation, suicidality, anxiety, or other psychiatric outcomes clinically relevant to depression management in ASD. Outcomes could include validated depression scales [e.g., Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS), Beck Depression Inventory-II [BDI-II], Patient Health Questionnaire–9 (PHQ-9), Children’s Depression Inventory–2 (CDI-2), Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS)], clinician-rated depressive symptom improvement, or related affective and internalizing outcomes. Interventional study designs included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental studies, open-label trials, pilot studies, case series, and case reports. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses were used to identify additional eligible primary studies through manual reference-list screening and to provide contextual background where appropriate but were not included as evidence units in this review. Interventions included pharmacologic treatments (e.g., antidepressants, antipsychotics, cannabinoids, and ketamine/esketamine), neuromodulation approaches (e.g., repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation [rTMS], intermittent theta burst stimulation [iTBS], electroconvulsive therapy [ECT], deep brain stimulation [DBS], transcranial direct current stimulation [tDCS]), and non-pharmacological approaches (e.g., CBT, DBT, Mindfulness-Based Interventions, behavioral activation, social skills interventions, exercise, animal-assisted therapy, nutraceuticals, and microbiota-focused interventions.). Given the limited number of ASD-specific intervention studies targeting major depressive disorder or depressive symptoms, we intentionally adopted broader eligibility criteria. Studies evaluating clinically related outcomes (e.g., anxiety, emotional dysregulation, irritability, suicidality, repetitive negative thinking, internalizing symptoms, and psychological well-being) were included when the intervention was considered clinically relevant to depression management in individuals with ASD.

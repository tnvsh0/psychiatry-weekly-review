### Brain health in children born extremely preterm: a multidimensional perspective
**כתב עת:** ⭐ The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health (Lancet Child Adolesc Health) *(IF: 45.7)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Padilla N et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 13

Gestational age and developmental stage of the brain at birth are major determinants of child health, and long-term consequences of preterm birth and altered brain maturation are inadequately understood simply through brain injury, survival rates, or diagnostic labels. In this Review, we conceptualise brain health in children born extremely preterm (before 28 weeks' gestation) as a developmental condition shaped by maturational timing, biological vulnerability, neonatal intensive care exposure, sensory and relational experiences, family circumstances, and social environments. We integrate evidence from research on early brain development and consciousness, long-term multidomain neurodevelopmental outcomes, neonatal intensive care practice, relational caregiving, early developmental interventions, and ethical, psychological, and social dimensions of care. Across these domains, extremely preterm birth emerges not as a single event but as a neuroplastic system that adapts to biological and environmental risks and protective factors that shape neurodevelopmental resilience and vulnerabilities and establish developmental trajectories. We argue that care for the infant born extremely preterm is optimised through a brain-health framework where care becomes a longitudinal, ethical process that integrates family support, minimises harmful exposures, promotes adaptive development, and fosters participation, wellbeing, and quality of life.

### Satisfaction, Regret, and Harm After Hormone Therapy in Youth From a Multidisciplinary Gender Clinic
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Pediatrics (JAMA Pediatr) *(IF: 27.6)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Kuper L et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 9

Key Points Question What degrees of satisfaction, regret, and harm are reported by transgender and gender-diverse participants who initiated testosterone or estrogen therapy (ie, hormone therapy [HT]) as youth, and are these experiences associated with demographic or clinical characteristics? Findings In this cohort study of 372 participants who received care at a single multidisciplinary clinic, satisfaction with HT was high and regret and harm were rare. Decisional regret was slightly higher among youths not currently receiving HT and those identifying on the nonbinary spectrum, but it remained low in absolute terms. Meaning Results provide support for existing standards of care rather than broad restrictions on youths’ access to HT.

Importance Concerns about regret and harm have led to restrictions on transgender and gender-diverse youths’ access to testosterone or estrogen therapy (ie, hormone therapy [HT]), while empirical data on decisional regret among those initiating HT as youth remain limited. Objectives To examine satisfaction, regret, and perceived harm among individuals who initiated HT as youth and to identify demographic and clinical characteristics associated with regret or harm and current HT status (receiving vs not). Design, Setting, and Participants This cohort study evaluates self-report surveys collected from October 2023 to October 2025 among former patients who initiated HT as youth at a single multidisciplinary gender clinic in the US. Data analysis was conducted from December 2025 to August 2026. Exposure Initiation of estrogen or testosterone. Main Outcomes and Measures Decision Regret Scale (DRS) total score (range, 0-100; higher scores indicate greater regret); item-level satisfaction, regret, and perceived harm; and current HT status. Associations with demographic and clinical characteristics were examined using nonparametric, Welch t , Fisher exact, or Pearson χ 2 tests as appropriate, with parametric sensitivity analyses for the DRS total score. Results Of the 376 participants who initiated HT as youth (112 [29.8%] initiating estrogen and 264 [70.2%] initiating testosterone; current age, 14-28 years; mean [SD] age, 20.9 [2.9] years; time since HT initiation: range, 1.2 months to 10.7 years; mean [SD], 4.9 [2.5] years), 4 were missing DRS scores, all of whom were currently receiving HT. Among the 372 respondents with DRS scores, overall decisional regret was very low (mean [SD] DRS score, 4.3 [14.7]; median [IQR], 0 [0-0]); 360 participants (96.8%) endorsed that receiving HT was the right decision, 10 (2.7%) reported regret, and 7 (1.9%) reported perceived harm. DRS scores were higher among participants not currently receiving HT vs currently receiving HT and among those identifying on the nonbinary vs binary spectrum; parametric sensitivity analyses estimated mean differences of 15.95 points (95% CI, 8.50-23.39) and 6.07 points (95% CI, 2.15-9.99), respectively. Participants not currently receiving HT (65 participants [17.3%]) were more likely than those currently receiving HT (311 [82.7%]) to have received testosterone (odds ratio, 3.58; 95% CI, 1.65-7.78; P = .001) and identify on the nonbinary spectrum (odds ratio, 8.88; 95% CI, 4.76-16.58; P < .001), were older (mean difference, 1.04 years; 95% CI, 0.45-1.64 years; P = .001), and had longer time since initiating HT (mean difference, 1.00 year; 95% CI, 0.48-1.53 years; P < .001). Neither regret or harm nor current HT status was associated with age at HT initiation or prepubertal gender diversity. Conclusions and Relevance The findings of this cohort study complement the literature documenting mental health benefits of HT, providing further support for existing standards of care for transgender and gender-diverse youth.

Introduction Current estimates suggest that approximately 3.3% of 13- to 17-year-olds in the US identify as transgender or gender diverse (TGD). However, only a small proportion receive hormone therapy (HT; ie, testosterone, estrogen), with insurance-based analyses indicating rates below 0.1% of the general youth population. More than a dozen short-term follow-up studies and several large cross-sectional and retrospective studies have consistently found improvements in mental health and quality of life among TGD youth receiving HT. Prior research from our clinic found significant reductions in body dissatisfaction, depression, and anxiety and improvement in psychosocial functioning in 2 cohorts of youth 1 year after initiating HT and/or puberty suppression. This previous research indirectly suggests that TGD youth may be satisfied with HT. However, concerns about potential regret and harm have intensified, leading to widespread efforts to prevent minors from accessing HT despite standards of care continuing to support its use. While more limited in scope, several initial studies have demonstrated very low levels of regret among youth, consistent with low regret levels documented among TGD adults. One of the first major longitudinal studies of youth receiving HT reported that no participants expressed regret regarding puberty suppression or HT an average of 6 years after starting treatment. Three other clinic-based studies of youth have documented regret rates between 0% and 2%. These findings are also consistent with a community-based sample of youth who socially transitioned in childhood and later accessed HT. While the rate of regret was slightly higher in this sample (5 of 119 respondents [4.2%]), nearly all respondents (115 of 119 [96.6%]) reported satisfaction with treatment. Preliminary research also suggests that rates of treatment discontinuation are low for those initiating HT as youth. van der Loos et al reported that only 16 of 720 participants (2.2%) who started puberty suppression as adolescents were no longer receiving HT at median (IQR) follow-up of 3.5 (1.5-6.6) years later for youth assigned female and 2.3 (1.2-4.8) years later for youth assigned male. In another clinic-based sample with median follow-up of 2.7 years, 57 of 1050 participants (5.4%) initiating HT as youth discontinued HT for at least 3 months, although 20 subsequently restarted and 210 were lost to follow-up. In a third sample using a US military insurance database, the 4-year continuation rate for those starting HT as minors was somewhat lower (74%), but it was still higher than for those who started HT as adults (64%). Most studies examining reasons for stopping treatment have been conducted with adults, and findings suggest that stopping treatment is influenced by a range of internal and external factors and thus is not synonymous with regret. Given recent increases in external barriers to accessing HT, rates of stopping treatment are likely increasing. The present study seeks to strengthen the research base regarding TGD youths’ experiences with HT, considering the substantial shifts in access to HT and concerns about the well-being of youth receiving this care. Building on our previous work, our first aim was to examine the degree of perceived satisfaction, regret, and harm among youth who initiated HT, including those who were currently receiving HT as well as those who were not. Our second aim was to examine whether demographic and clinical factors were associated with (1) youths’ reports of regret or harm and/or (2) current HT status (receiving vs not). ### Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder Accommodations, Behavior Therapy, and Stimulant Medication: A Randomized Clinical Trial
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Pediatrics (JAMA Pediatr) *(IF: 27.6)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Fabiano GA et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 13

IMPORTANCE: Although widely deployed, little is known about the efficacy of academic accommodations for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), alone or in combination of behavior therapy and stimulant medication.

OBJECTIVE: To evaluate the efficacy of academic accommodations alone and in combination with behavioral interventions and stimulant medication.

DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS: This was a crossover randomized clinical trial with participants introduced to all conditions. Children with ADHD were enrolled in kindergarten through sixth grade. Academic accommodations were changed daily in a randomized order. Stimulant medication and placebo alternated weekly. The general classroom and enhanced behavior classroom intervention alternated every 2 weeks. The study was conducted in summer treatment program classrooms. For the present study, behavior was evaluated in (1) a classroom period where participants completed independent assignments and (2) a classroom period where participants were taught a lesson, completed small group cooperative tasks related to the lesson, and then took a quiz. Study data were analyzed from March to July 2026.

INTERVENTIONS: The single and combined effects of academic accommodations, behavior therapy, and stimulant medication (methylphenidate, 0.3 mg/kg) were evaluated. Academic accommodations were extended time on tasks, frequent breaks during tasks, a low-distraction workspace, and the ability to stand at the desk, compared with a condition without an accommodation. A general classroom setting was compared with a classroom setting with enhanced behavior intervention.

MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES: Main outcomes were classroom rule violations during each of the two, 1-hour class periods, the number of seatwork items completed correctly (ie, academic productivity), and the quiz score.

RESULTS: Of 306 children screened, 280 were enrolled in the study. A total of 255 children (mean [SD] age, 8.8 [1.6] months; 201 male [79%]) started the intervention, and 235 (84%) completed the study. Compared with placebo, stimulant medication increased effects related to seatwork productivity by 22% (ie, the rate ratio [RR] for this condition indicated that receiving medication compared with placebo multiplied the expected count of problems completed per minute by 1.22; 95% CI, 1.19-1.25), and quiz scores were significantly improved by 3.9 points (95% CI, 3.10-4.70 points). Medication and behavioral therapy significantly reduced classroom rule-breaking behavior by 37% (RR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.60-0.67) and 61% (RR, 0.39; 95% CI, 0.37-0.42), respectively. There was no evidence that any of the academic accommodations significantly improved behavior or academic outcomes. No interactions were significant.

CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE: Results of this crossover randomized clinical trial question whether school-based academic accommodations for children with ADHD should continue to be emphasized as accommodations were ineffective when used alone or in combination with other ADHD treatments. Results support the continued use of behavioral and pharmacological treatment for ADHD.

TRIAL REGISTRATION: ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05469386.

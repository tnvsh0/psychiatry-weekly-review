### Guided self-help for depression adapted for autistic adults: a multi-centre randomised controlled phase 3 trial (ADEPT-2)
**כתב עת:** ⭐ Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics (Psychother Psychosom) *(IF: 15.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Russell A et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 13

Introduction Autistic people are disproportionately affected by depression and access recommended first line treatments such as Guided Self-Help (GSH)) which has not been developed to meet their needs. This study aims to test the effectiveness of autism adapted GSH for depression compared to treatment as usual. Methods A two parallel group multi-centre randomised controlled trial investigated the effectiveness of GSH adapted for autism. The RCT was registered on 09th August 2022 (ISRCTN: 17547011). Adults, ≥18 years, with a confirmed autism diagnosis and symptoms of depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 10) recruited from community settings were randomised to GSH or Treatment as Usual (TAU). Adapted GSH comprised psychoeducation and behavioural activation supported by nine meetings with a coach. Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II) scores at 16-weeks post-randomisation were analysed on an intention to treat basis. Additional measures at 16-weeks captured anxiety, well-being and service use with follow up at 32- and 52-weeks. Results Participants (n=266) were randomised to GSH (n=132) or TAU (n=134). Of participants randomised to GSH, 110 (81%) completed the minimum 6 session dose, five did not begin the intervention and 28 discontinued. Estimated between group mean difference in BDI-II scores at 16 weeks was -5.05 (95% CI: -7.85 to -2.24, P<0.001, d=0.47) in favour of GSH, with the difference sustained at 52-weeks. Conclusion The intervention named Autism Affirming Accompanied Self-Help for depression (AASH-DEP) needs to be rolled-out so clinicians and autistic people can be confident to access effective treatment for depression adapted for autism.

### Evaluation of low-intensity, family-based behavioral treatments for youth with anxiety and obsessive-compulsive disorders
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Behaviour Research and Therapy (Behav Res Ther) *(IF: 5.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Storch EA et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 9

OBJECTIVE: To examine the efficacy of two different low-intensity, family-based treatments (internet-based, therapist assisted CBT and parent training) relative to relaxation and mentorship training (RMT) for youth with anxiety and obsessive-compulsive disorders (OCD).

METHOD: 151 youth (age 7-13 years) with anxiety or OCD and their parent/caregiver were randomized to receive four telehealth sessions of either an internet-based, therapist-assisted CBT protocolknown as Learning to Understand and Navigate Anxiety (LUNA), parent training with bibliotherapy (SPACE-Light), or RMT over 12 weeks. Participants were assessed by blinded raters at baseline, post-treatment, and one-month follow-up.

RESULTS: Across measures, participants in all three treatment arms showed significant improvements from baseline to post-treatment and one-month follow-up on continuous outcomes. Clinician-rated outcomes indicated greater improvement forLUNA relative to parent training and RMT, particularly at follow-up. Differences in continuous outcomes between treatments were less consistent for child- and parent-reported outcomes, though still favored LUNA. Rates of treatment response and remission from all anxiety disorders were higher in LUNA relative to parent training and RMT in completer-only analyses, but not in intent-to-treat analyses. Dropout rates did not differ significantly between treatment arms.

CONCLUSION: A low-intensity internet-based, therapist-assisted cognitive behavioral therapy protocol demonstrated significant benefits for youth with anxiety and/or OCD, outperformed low-intensity parent training and relaxation approaches which were also effective.

CLINICAL TRIAL REGISTRATION NUMBER: NCT05967468, 7/12/2023.

### Online mindfulness-based cognitive therapy for people with multiple sclerosis with mild-to-moderate depression- protocol for a type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation study
**כתב עת:** 📄 PLoS One | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Simpson R et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

BACKGROUND: Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) is an effective treatment for recurrent depression in the general population and is recommended in clinical guidelines for those with mild-moderate depression. However, the effectiveness of MBCT in people with multiple sclerosis (PwMS) with mild to moderate depression is unknown. Further, how best to tailor and implement MBCT for PwMS is largely unstudied. This manuscript describes the study protocol for a hybrid-effectiveness study to evaluate online MBCT tailored for PwMS.

METHODS: This type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation study will employ an embedded mixed methods experimental design. Quantitative findings will be collected through a powered waitlist randomized controlled trial examining online MBCT in a minimum of 152 PwMS with case-ness of mild-moderate depression (Clinical Trials Gov #NCT07742579). The intervention will consist of an online 8-week MBCT course with weekly sessions conducted by qualified mindfulness instructors experienced int the care of PwMS. Questionnaires will be collected at baseline, post-intervention, and 3 months later. The primary outcome will be depression, measured using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Score - Depression (HADS-D). An intention-to-treat analysis will be conducted. Qualitative findings will be collected through semi-structured interviews with 30-40 MBCT participants, HCPs, MBCT instructors, and MS decision makers. Thematic analyses will be used to analyze interview data. The RE-AIM PRISM tool will be used to evaluate implementation and mixed data assessed in a joint display.

CONCLUSION: The results from this study will provide evidence regarding the effectiveness of online MBCT in PwMS with mild to moderate depression, recommendations for intervention tailoring and implementation. Findings will be disseminated through traditional and novel, creative knowledge mobilization methods.

### Effects of acceptance and commitment therapy on psychological and health-related outcomes in stroke patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
**כתב עת:** 📄 Front Neurol | **סוג מחקר:** מטה-אנליזה | **מחברים:** Li X et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

Background Stroke is a leading cause of disability worldwide and is frequently accompanied by psychological and functional impairments. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), a third-wave behavioral intervention focusing on psychological flexibility, has been increasingly applied in stroke rehabilitation. Although previous reviews have suggested beneficial effects of ACT in stroke patients, whether these benefits persist in the short term after the intervention ends, and the certainty of the evidence, remain unclear. Methods A systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261325108). Randomized controlled trials evaluating ACT in stroke patients were included. Pooled effects were calculated using random-effects models, with separate analyses for post-intervention and follow-up outcomes. Sources of heterogeneity were explored using subgroup analyses and meta-regression. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool, and certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE framework. Results Twenty-two RCTs involving 1,975 participants were included. ACT may be associated with reductions in depressive symptoms (post-intervention: SMD = −1.32, 95% CI: −1.80 to −0.84; follow-up: SMD = −0.76, 95% CI: −1.07 to −0.45) and anxiety symptoms (post-intervention: SMD = −1.36, 95% CI: −1.90 to −0.82; follow-up: SMD = −0.51, 95% CI: −1.42 to 0.40, not significant); depressive symptoms remained statistically significant at follow-up. Heterogeneity was considerable for both outcomes at post-intervention ( I 2 = 93.68% for depression and 93.60% for anxiety), so the pooled estimates represent average effects across studies. ACT may also be associated with improvements in psychological flexibility, quality of life, and sleep quality; however, these outcomes were based on a limited number of studies and showed considerable heterogeneity ( I 2 = 74.68 to 92.26%), so they should be interpreted cautiously. However, no statistically significant effects were observed for activities of daily living, neurological function, or hope. ACT did not appear to increase dropout rates in the available studies ( K = 16; RR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.58–1.69). Meta-regression analyses indicated that intervention duration may not be a significant moderator of treatment effects. The certainty of evidence ranged from very low to moderate, mainly due to risk of bias and inconsistency. Conclusion Acceptance and Commitment Therapy may be a useful adjunctive intervention for improving psychological outcomes in people with stroke. However, evidence for functional recovery remains insufficient. Future high-quality RCTs with standardized protocols, larger samples, and longer follow-up are needed to confirm the long-term effectiveness of ACT. Stroke is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide ( 1 ). Many survivors are left with persistent disability: in a large cohort of patients with ischemic stroke, approximately 40% had moderate-to-severe or persistent severe disability during the first year ( 2 ). Post-stroke depression affects roughly 10–35% of patients during the first 3 months ( 3 ), and anxiety affects about one in four patients when assessed with rating scales ( 4 ); anxiety after stroke has been described as the “other” poststroke mood disorder ( 5 ). Depression is associated with poorer functional outcomes over the first 5 ears after stroke ( 3 ), and at 6 months, with cognitive impairment ( 6 ). These symptoms are often not transient: long-term cohort data show that a considerable proportion of survivors still report elevated depressive and anxiety symptoms several years after stroke, and that these rates are relatively stable over time ( 7 ). Current management of post-stroke psychological distress relies mainly on pharmacological treatment and general psychosocial or rehabilitation support. Antidepressants are widely prescribed, but a Cochrane review found that the evidence for pharmacological and psychological interventions for depression after stroke is of very low certainty, and that antidepressants were associated with adverse events involving the central nervous system and the gastrointestinal tract ( 8 ). At present, traditional intervention methods such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) have been shown to reduce depressive and anxiety symptoms after stroke, with large effects at the end of treatment and moderate maintenance at 3 months, although the evidence comes from a small number of generally small studies ( 9 ). Psychological treatment is recommended in stroke care ( 10 ). Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) is the third wave of behavioral intervention, and it has gained increasing attention due to its focus on psychological flexibility ( 11 ). ACT emphasizes psychological flexibility, helping individuals accept distressing experiences and engage in value-based behaviors despite ongoing health challenges. This approach may be particularly relevant for stroke survivors who face persistent functional limitations and emotional distress during recovery ( 11 ). Rather than attempting to reduce or control distressing thoughts and feelings, ACT seeks to increase psychological flexibility — the capacity to remain in contact with the present moment and to persist in, or adjust, behavior in the service of personally meaningful values — through acceptance, cognitive defusion, and committed action ( 11 ). This process-oriented approach may be particularly relevant after stroke, where disability and uncertainty about recovery are often not modifiable: cognitive fusion has been found to predict depressive symptoms and to mediate the association between psychological flexibility and depression, suggesting that cognitive defusion may be a relevant treatment target ( 12 ). Previous evidence has primarily focused on immediate post-intervention outcomes, while the durability of ACT effects after intervention remains unclear ( 13 ). A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that ACT improved depression, anxiety, and quality of life in stroke patients ( 13 ). Evidence from other populations is mixed: a randomized trial in people with acquired brain injury found no superiority of ACT over an active control on the primary outcomes, although more participants showed long-term, individual-level improvement in depressive and anxiety symptoms ( 14 ); benefits have been reported in older adults with chronic low back pain ( 15 ) and in people with type 2 diabetes ( 16 ), and internet-delivered ACT has been associated with reduced anxiety ( 17 ). Whether the benefits of ACT are maintained after the intervention ends, and which outcomes and patient groups respond most consistently, therefore remain unclear. To address these uncertainties, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to provide an updated and comprehensive evaluation of ACT in stroke patients. Specifically, we evaluated the effects of ACT on psychological outcomes (depression, anxiety, and ACT-related psychological processes, including psychological flexibility and cognitive fusion), clinical outcomes (quality of life, sleep quality, activities of daily living, and neurological function), and all-cause attrition (used as an indirect indicator of participant retention rather than as a direct measure of acceptability). ### Efficacy of Digital Health Interventions for Insomnia: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials
**כתב עת:** 📄 J Med Internet Res | **סוג מחקר:** מטה-אנליזה | **מחברים:** Gao S et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 13

BACKGROUND: Insomnia is common, and treatments are limited by tolerability and access. Digital health interventions (DHIs) are increasingly used for sleep management, but evidence comparing multiple outcomes is scarce.

OBJECTIVE: This study aimed to systematically evaluate the effects of DHIs on 8 sleep outcomes, including insomnia severity, sleep efficiency, total sleep time, and sleep quality.

METHODS: We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, CNKI, CBM, VIP, Wanfang, ClinicalTrials.gov, and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's website from inception to July 2026. Two reviewers (SG and BZ) independently screened studies, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool. We included randomized controlled trials or controlled clinical trials comparing DHIs with any control in insomnia, without language restriction, and excluded experimental sleep deprivation, conference abstracts, protocols, and nonrandomized or noncontrolled designs. Pooled standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% CIs used restricted maximum likelihood with Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman correction; 95% prediction intervals (PIs) used the Nagashima method. We also performed subgroup analyses by intervention category, comparator type, diagnostic criteria, and country income, plus sensitivity analyses, Egger tests, and GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) assessment.

RESULTS: We included 44 randomized controlled trials (n=10,521) published between 2004 and 2026 across 14 countries. DHIs significantly improved insomnia severity (SMD -0.91, 95% CI -1.12 to -0.70), sleep efficiency (SMD 0.59, 95% CI 0.45-0.72), total sleep time (SMD 0.20, 95% CI 0.11-0.29), sleep quality (SMD -0.61, 95% CI -0.78 to -0.45), and secondary outcomes (SMDs -0.37 to -0.64). However, 95% PIs crossed the null for insomnia severity (-2.04 to 0.22), total sleep time (-0.05 to 0.46), and all secondary outcomes. Subgroup analyses showed larger insomnia severity effects in high- versus upper-middle-income countries, modality differences for sleep efficiency, and comparator-type differences for total sleep time (all  P <.01). Egger tests suggested small-study effects; sensitivity analyses did not change direction or significance. GRADE certainty ranged from very low to moderate.

CONCLUSIONS: Although DHIs showed statistically significant average effects on multiple sleep outcomes, wide PIs crossing or approaching the null, effects approaching or below Minimum Clinically Important Difference thresholds, and very low-to-moderate GRADE certainty indicate that the evidence remains exploratory. Subgroup analyses revealed effect modifications by country income, intervention modality, and comparator type, underscoring contextual factors. Unlike previous reviews focusing on single DHI formats or average statistical effects, this review provides stratified comparisons and integrates PIs and Minimum Clinically Important Difference conversion to distinguish statistical significance from clinical relevance. This paper provides an evidence synthesis framework showing that DHI benefits are context-dependent and insufficient for a standard clinical role. In practice, DHIs cannot be recommended as a standard substitute for face-to-face cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, although they may serve as a low-intensity option in stepped care. Large-scale, head-to-head implementation trials in diverse populations with extended follow-up are needed to define their optimal role.

### Outcomes from a Pilot Trial of a Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) Tobacco Cessation Intervention for Veterans at Heightened Risk for Suicide
**כתב עת:** 📄 Nicotine Tob Res | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Doran N et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 10

INTRODUCTION: Tobacco use has consistently been linked to suicide risk, and some data suggest that tobacco cessation may reduce risk. However, higher risk individuals are typically excluded from clinical trials. Dialectical Behavior Therapy is a psychotherapy approach originally designed to reduce suicide risk that may also be beneficial in treating addiction by enhancing emotion regulation and distress tolerance skills.

METHODS: The present study was a randomized pilot trial comparing a standard tobacco cessation intervention to a DBT-based integrated intervention for tobacco cessation and suicide prevention. Veterans (n = 68) who were daily users of cigarettes and/or e-cigarettes were randomized to DBT for cessation (DBT-C) or to treatment as usual (TAU). Participants in both conditions attended 12 weekly group psychotherapy sessions and were followed for a total of 24 weeks to evaluate changes in tobacco use, suicidality, emotion regulation and overall satisfaction with the intervention.

RESULTS: DBT-C and TAU participants did not differ in terms of abstinence at end-of-treatment or 24-week follow-up. There was a trend toward greater improvement in emotion regulation in DBT-C versus TAU. Participants generally were satisfied with the intervention.

CONCLUSIONS: This pilot trial provides preliminary evidence that individuals who use tobacco and are at elevated suicide risk can be effectively engaged in tobacco cessation interventions, and that such interventions may benefit emotional functioning as well as reducing tobacco use. DBT-C appears to be a potentially effective approach but additional, larger-scale research is needed to enable a more thorough assessment.

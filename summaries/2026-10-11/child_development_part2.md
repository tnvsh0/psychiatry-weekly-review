### The prospective associations between parent and adolescent emotional reactivity and adolescent psychopathology
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Development and Psychopathology (Dev Psychopathol) *(IF: 5.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Chiang SC et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 6

This study examined how adolescent and parent emotional reactivity to daily parent-adolescent conflict is associated with adolescent psychopathology over time. A community sample of 237 Taiwanese parent-adolescent dyads (adolescents:  M age  = 14.80,  SD age  = 2.08; 58% female; parents:  M age  = 42.36,  SD age  = 8.57; 68% female) completed 10 days of daily reports on parent-adolescent conflict and emotions, and adolescents reported depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and externalizing problems at baseline and six-month follow-up. The results showed that greater adolescent negative emotional (NE) reactivity predicted higher depressive ( β  = .17) and anxiety symptoms ( β  = .21), and adolescent positive emotional (PE) reactivity predicted higher depressive symptoms ( β  = .13), anxiety symptoms ( β  = .19), and externalizing problems ( β  = .17). Parent NE reactivity predicted higher adolescent depressive symptoms ( β  = .29) and externalizing problems ( β  = .15). Age moderated the associations between parent reactivity and anxiety symptoms, such that higher parent NE and PE reactivity were associated with greater anxiety only among younger adolescents. Findings highlight that daily emotional reactivity to parent-adolescent conflict is associated with adolescent psychopathology and underscore that parent reactivity represents a distinct risk process, especially consequential for anxiety in early adolescence.

### Transactional, social causation, or symptoms-driven? Disentangling the longitudinal associations between perceived family, peer, and teacher support and depressive symptoms among multicultural adolescents
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Development and Psychopathology (Dev Psychopathol) *(IF: 5.8)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Li J | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 6

This study examines whether perceived social support from family, peers, and teachers exhibits reciprocal relations with depressive symptoms (transactional model), acts as a protective factor (social causation model), or emerges as a consequence of depressive symptoms (symptoms-driven model) among South Korean multicultural adolescents. A four-wave longitudinal dataset from a nationally representative cohort ( N  = 1,500; 50.67% female;  M age  = 10.98 years,  SD  = 0.37) was analyzed using random intercept cross-lagged panel models. At the between-person level, all three perceived support sources were negatively associated with depressive symptoms across both the joint and bivariate models ( rs  = -0.69 to -0.63,  ps  < .001). At the within-person level, perceived family support predicted subsequent decreases in depressive symptoms in both modeling frameworks ( βs  = -0.07 to -0.05,  ps  < .05), and perceived peer support similarly emerged as a significant prospective predictor when modeled independently ( βs  = -0.07 to -0.06,  ps  < .05). Depressive symptoms did not predict changes in any perceived support source in either framework. The findings support a source-specific social causation model, with perceived family support emerging as the primary within-person protective factor, and highlight the family system as a pivotal intervention target for alleviating depressive symptoms in this vulnerable population.

### Food neophobia predicts early cognitive development: A longitudinal study
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Child Development (Child Dev) *(IF: 5.4)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Foinant D et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 8

Food neophobia, the tendency to reject novel food, has been linked to children's categorization and executive functions in cross-sectional research, but the developmental direction of these relations remains unclear. This longitudinal study examined reciprocal relations between these variables across one school year in French children. Participants were 113 children (50 girls; M age = 48.30 ± 7.20 months at Time 1) recruited from five public preschools in a large metropolitan area. Data were collected between 2023 and 2024 (Time 2). Race and ethnicity data were not collected due to national regulations. Caregivers reported children's food neophobia, and children completed a food categorization task and measures of working memory, inhibition, and cognitive flexibility. Most constructs showed temporal stability across waves. Higher food neophobia at Time 1 predicted lower categorization performance (β = -.263) and lower executive functions (β = -.195), controlling for prior levels. In contrast, categorization performance and executive functions did not predict subsequent changes in food neophobia. These findings suggest that food neophobia prospectively relates to later differences in children's executive functions and their ability to use food-category knowledge under task demands, consistent with the possibility that early avoidance tendencies shape engagement with cognitively informative experiences during development.

Children differ in their willingness to try new foods, a tendency known as food neophobia. Research has linked food neophobia to categorization, the ability to group items, and to executive functions, the skills that support remembering rules, controlling impulses, and adapting to changing situations. This study followed French preschool children over one school year to examine how food neophobia, categorization, and executive functions develop together over time. Children who were more neophobic at the first assessment showed lower categorization and executive-function performance 1 year later, even after accounting for their earlier performance. In contrast, categorization and executive functions did not predict later food neophobia. These findings suggest that food neophobia may be associated with differences in how children engage with everyday experiences that support learning and self-regulation in early childhood.

### Long-term legacy of parents' secure base script knowledge: Cascading implications for family relationships and children's positive development
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Developmental Psychology (Dev Psychol) *(IF: 5.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Herbert HM et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 5

A large body of research implicates adults’ attachment security, largely formed in their early experiences, as a key factor shaping their future romantic and parenting relationships. Family system theories suggest that the interparental relationship is key for other relationships within the family, including those in individual mother- and father-child dyads. Few studies, however, have elucidated how the legacy of parents’ attachment security unfolds through family relationships to child outcomes. We test a developmental longitudinal cascade, conceptualized as a sequential mediation model, from parents’ attachment security, assessed as their secure base script knowledge when children were infants, to their self-reported interparental relationship quality at toddler age, to their observed relationships with their children at age 3 years, and ultimately, to children’s developmental outcomes at age 4.5 years (self-regulation and moral self). We leverage multi-method data (interviews, reports, observations) from a study of 200 community families in U.S. Midwest (mothers, fathers, and children). We adopt recent perspectives that emphasize positive socialization processes and successful outcomes rather than detrimental pathways conferring risks. In analyses that controlled for the continuity of interparental and parent-child relationships, we supported several mediational paths. Results for mother-child dyads supported the entire hypothesized longitudinal sequence from parents’ attachment security to the parent-child relationship to children’s positive developmental outcomes. For father-child dyads, that key cascade was not supported. However, several shorter alternative indirect paths were found in both dyads. Public significance statement : Adopting a perspective focused on positive factors in development, we highlight how parents’ secure attachment, assessed when children are infants, promotes positive interparental relationships in the second year, then in turn, positive parent-child relationships at age three, and ultimately, children’s positive social-emotional outcomes at kindergarten age.

For the last several decades, following the ascent and increasing prominence of the science of relationships in psychology ( Collins & Laursen, 1999 ; Hinde & Stevenson-Hinde, 1979; Reis & Collins, 2004 ; Vangelisti et al., 2026 ), family relationships have been seen as key contexts in which to understand children’s development. Several distinct but dovetailing perspectives have produced rich literatures elucidating various aspects of origins and interconnected dynamics of family relationships. The blossoming science of relationships provides a rich framework for understanding the continuity of human functioning over the life span, from experiences of early care with one’s parents to forming romantic relationships as adults to becoming parents, providing early care to one’s offspring. Longitudinal studies especially can elucidate mechanisms responsible for the powerful legacy of early experiences. Attachment theory proposed that early relational experiences are key factors in forming individuals’ future enduring attachment orientations that continue to shape and guide their functioning in interpersonal relationships ( Bowlby, 1973 ; Cavallo & Perlman, 2026 ; Dugan et al., 2025 ; Fraley, 2002 ; Haydon et al., 2014 ; Main et al., 1985 ; Simpson & Karantzas, 2019 ; Simpson et al., 2026 ). Finally, parent-child relationships become the uniquely important contexts for children’s development ( Collins & Laursen, 1999 ; Kuczynski & de Mol, 2015 ; Maccoby, 1999 ; Maccoby & Martin, 1983 ). ### Experiences of mothers with borderline personality disorder following an intervention to support mother-infant relationships: "more tools in my toolbox"
**כתב עת:** 📄 Infant Mental Health Journal (Infant Ment Health J) *(IF: 2.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Pryor J et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov

Abstract Motherhood is inherently demanding, and some of these demands may be amplified for women with borderline personality disorder (BPD). The exacerbation of BPD symptoms from parenting‐related stressors can have adverse impacts on mother‐infant relationships and infant development. This qualitative study explores Australian mothers’ views of the impacts of mother‐infant dialectical therapy plus (MI‐DBT+). MI‐DBT+ is an innovative therapeutic intervention which integrates mother‐infant dialectical behavior Therapy (MI‐DBT), a known effective intervention for the mental health of mothers with BPD, with attachment and biobehavioral catch‐up (ABC) therapy. Specifically, eight mothers completed semi‐structured interviews about their experiences of intervention following completion of the MI‐DBT+ program. A thematic analysis of this interview data found that mothers appreciated learning more skills to enhance their understanding of their infants’ needs, behaviors and emotions. Mothers also reported improved reflective functioning and better control of their emotional responses. These skills assisted in managing emotional distress at both the individual and dyadic level. The study was limited due to available demographic data, with only maternal and child age available. Discussion also focuses on the fit between DBT and ABC, longer term developmental concerns and sustainability.

1 INTRODUCTION Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is a complex personality disorder characterized by an extensive pattern of instability within psychosocial functioning, interpersonal relationships, emotion regulation, empathy and self‐image (American Psychiatric Association, 2013 ; Gunderson et al., 2018 ). For a clinical diagnosis, an individual must meet five or more from nine BPD symptom criteria (American Psychiatric Association, 2013 ). Individuals with this diagnosis often experience heightened emotions, impulsivity and sensitivity to perceived abandonment, often leading to significant levels of distress (Mishra et al., 2023 ). While the prevalence of BPD is relatively low within the general population (approximately between 1% and 6%; Crowell et al., 2009 ; Grenyer et al., 2017 ), there is a heightened prevalence within perinatal (including both ante‐ and postnatal) populations of 14% (Prasad et al., 2022 ). Postnatally, mother‐baby units often have a high prevalence of mothers with BPD with Yelland et al ( 2015 ) finding that 23% of admitted women had this clinical diagnosis, increasing to a prevalence of 46% when the diagnosis was self‐reported. In general, BPD is prevalent among women who may encounter unique challenges related to accepting their identity as mothers (Zalewski et al., 2023 ) and expressing and regulating emotions when triggered by distress (Trull et al., 2018 ). The disorder often presents with comorbid conditions such as complex post‐traumatic stress disorder (cPTSD) (van Dijke et al., 2018 ), depression, and anxiety (Crowell et al., 2009 ). Notably the transition to motherhood is particularly challenging for women with BPD (Wendland et al., 2014 ). The increase in BPD prevalence postnatally is not surprising given that mothers with BPD are easily dysregulated by the typical stressors associated with the postnatal period. These include challenges such as sleep deprivation, infant crying, and strain on relationships, which make providing consistency in parenting an infant difficult (Geerling et al, 2019 ; Sved Williams & Apter, 2017 ). Disrupted affective communication or disoriented behaviors have been noted by a number of authors (Geerling et al., 2019 ; Hobson et al., 2009 ; Petfield et al., 2015 ). Moreover, a systematic review of parenting behaviors associated with BPD found lower levels of maternal sensitivity, with decreased instances of smiling, play, and imitation during mother‐infant interactions, when compared to mothers without BPD (Eyden et al., 2016 ). Mothers with BPD also report a decreased sense of self‐competence in maternal roles (Gillespie et al., 2022 ), difficulties in correctly identifying and responding to infant emotions and needs when their infant is displaying prolonged distress (Geerling et al., 2019 ) and are more likely to show hostility or reactivity toward their infants (Eyden et al., 2016 ). Substantial evidence demonstrates an association between maternal BPD and poorer outcomes in a variety of domains across childhood (Eyden et al., 2016 ; Petfield et al., 2015 ). In infancy, infants of mothers with BPD display low soothability, negative affect, and poorer responsivity when compared to infants of mothers with no diagnosis (Petfield et al, 2015 ). This impact extends into later childhood, where children of mothers with BPD are at increased risk of behavioral problems such as aggressive and oppositional behavior, and deficits in socioemotional skills and relationship building (Eyden et al., 2016 ; Mesquita & Figueiredo, 2025 ). The need for support for this population is evident to support healthy development. Interventions delivered during infancy may have a protective effect against the known negative child outcomes associated with the mother's diagnosis (Stepp et al., 2012 ). Despite the prevalence of perinatal BPD and its consequences for family members, surprisingly few therapies have been described to help these families (Florange & Herpertz., 2019 ). May et al's systematic review ( 2023 ) found only seven relevant publications, with only low quality evidence of improvement. Hellberg et al's scoping review ( 2024 ) similarly found few studies, and of these, there was little to provide clear direction for positive outcomes for relevant families with perinatal BPD. Recently, there has been increasing interest in interventions designed for mothers with BPD, with particular focus on emotional regulation and responsive parenting skills. In Germany, Rosenbach et al ( 2020 ) have advanced their dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) therapy for this patient population, described in a recent manual. Their approach supports early parenting strategies and aims to prevent negative impact on child development such as socioemotional, child attachment (Rosenbach et al, 2022 ). Herpertz et al ( 2025 ) reported encouraging shorter‐term improvements in parenting practices following a five session/12 h mentalisation‐based intervention, delivered in addition to standard clinical care for mothers with BPD. In Australia, a four session, psycho‐education program for parents with BPD has shown some promising improvements in reducing parenting stress (Grenyer et al, 2025 ). Another longer intervention with developing evidence is an adaptation of dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) (Linehan & Wilks, 2015 ) which incorporates a number of elements of mother‐infant therapy into DBT (MI‐DBT). In evaluations, MI‐DBT has shown improvement in mental health symptoms including depression, anxiety and BPD symptoms as well as parenting confidence and competence (Sved Williams et al, 2018 ; 2021 ). However, objective measures of parent‐infant relationship did not show significant improvement and it is clear that mothers who have undertaken MI‐DBT “want more” to support them in their parenting role (Giles et al., 2024 ). Attachment and Biobehavioral Catch‐up (ABC) (Dozier & Bernard, 2017 ) is a ten‐week annualized home‐visiting program which aims to improve carer‐infant relationships for carers from a range of vulnerable circumstances (e.g. Berlin et al., 2018 ).

### The Precision Hierarchy Hypothesis: A Unifying Framework to Explain Opposing Serial Effects
**כתב עת:** 📄 Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience (J Cogn Neurosci) *(IF: 4.4)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Ufer C, Blank H | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 6

Serial dependence, the phenomenon where current perception is biased toward the previous stimulus, has been interpreted as a mechanism for perceptual stability, smoothing variability in sensory input. However, analyses that include both the previous stimulus and previous response unmasked repulsive effects at the sensory level that were otherwise concealed. Moreover, attractive serial biases fail to scale with environmental correlations, challenging their primary role in maintaining stability. We propose the "precision hierarchy hypothesis," grounded in predictive processing, as a unifying framework for these seemingly contradictory findings. Repulsive effects naturally emerge when priors-shaped by recent trials-are highly precise, making small deviations in incoming input more probable under alternative priors. We further propose that prior precision decreases across hierarchical levels. A higher precision at lower sensory levels leads to repulsive effects, as even small deviations in incoming signal are in conflict with the prior. In contrast, attractive biases emerge at higher, more abstract levels, where deviations are integrated with broader conceptual priors. By dynamically shaping prior precision according to environmental correlations, perception remains adaptive, balancing the need for stability with the sensitivity to detect meaningful changes in the environment. This framework reconciles diverse findings on serial effects and provides testable predictions for future research.

### Independent regulation of cognitive stability and flexibility in both intra-task and inter-task contexts among children
**כתב עת:** 📄 Cognitive Psychology (Cogn Psychol) *(IF: 3.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Xu X et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 9

Stability and flexibility are essential components of cognitive control, critical for goal-directed behavior and adaptability, particularly in children. However, the relationship between them is debated; traditional unidimensional models suggest a trade-off relationship, while recent studies propose a two-dimensional perspective emphasizing their independence. Previous research mostly focused on adults, overlooking the rapid development of stability and flexibility in children. The current study recruited 38 adults and 73 healthy children aged 9-10 years to investigate the relationship between stability and flexibility in both intra-task (Experiments 1 and 2) and inter-task (Experiment 3) contexts. Experiments 1 and 2 utilized a rule-switching task with stimulus-response consistency manipulation: switch costs measured flexibility, while the consistency effect assessed stability. Both results of Experiments 1 and 2 supported a two-dimensional model for cognitive stability and flexibility in the intra-task context. Experiment 3 employed the color-word Stroop task to measure cognitive stability (congruency effect), and an inter-task correlational analysis was conducted with the switch costs (flexibility indicator) from Experiment 2. The findings revealed no significant correlation between the Stroop congruency effect and rule-switching costs, indicating independence between stability and flexibility in different task contexts, further supporting the two-dimensional model in the inter-task context. In conclusion, this study supports the independence of cognitive stability and flexibility during middle childhood, demonstrating their ability to coexist at high levels based on task demands. These insights deepen our understanding of cognitive control in children and provide a theoretical foundation for offering developmental guidance and developing effective cognitive training programs.

### Aversive learning retroactively modulates neutral episodic memories structured by prior knowledge of predictive sequences
**כתב עת:** 📄 Cognition *(IF: 3.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Baczkowski BM, Villringer A | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 9

Linking memories of temporally separated neutral and threatening experiences can support later recognition of early warning signals. Yet it remains unclear what cognitive processes might allow the seemingly inconsequential neutral events - encoded in safe contexts and thus prone to forgetting - to nevertheless be preserved in memory well enough to inform future threat predictions. Here, we tested whether Pavlovian threat conditioning retroactively strengthens long-term episodic memory for previously encoded neutral events by leveraging prior knowledge of their predictive relations. Young adults first learned to anticipate the order of semantic categories in two deterministic sequences (A → B → C and D → E → F) through repeated presentation of images depicting category exemplars. They then incidentally encoded trial-unique exemplars from each category during sequence viewing. In a next phase, exemplars from the third-position categories were repeated such that one (C) predicted mild electric shocks, whereas the other (F) was never reinforced, creating a category-level threat association through conditioning. After 24 h, recognition memory for exemplars of categories encoded before conditioning depended on sequence and position: when a sequence ended with a shock-predictive category, memory favored the starting category (A) over the following category (B) - an effect absent in the control categories (D and E). These results suggest that aversive learning can retroactively reshape episodic memories of neutral events when they are scaffolded by prior sequence knowledge, bridging temporal gaps and thereby potentially promoting early threat anticipation.

### When "they" is singular: The interplay between cognitive and social factors predicts pronoun misinterpretations in bilingual English speakers
**כתב עת:** 📄 Cognition *(IF: 3.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Pulido MF et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 6

Language change is shaped by cognitive biases as well as by sociolinguistic perceptions of linguistic innovation. Although the use of they as a singular pronoun to refer to a generic referent is not new in English (e.g., "An employee can file a complaint if they need to"), a recent innovation extends this usage to refer to known individuals of non-binary gender. This type of socially motivated language change allows speakers to position themselves relative to linguistic innovation. Using the self-paced reading paradigm, we examined the processing of non-binary singular they across unambiguous (one-referent) and temporarily ambiguous (two-referent) contexts. We report on a processing experiment with second-language English speakers from Spain who, by virtue of their bilingual experience, are skilled at language learning and regulating activation of competing representations through cognitive control. We investigate how variability in the adoption of the pronominal innovation is influenced by sociodemographic and cognitive factors. We find that seemingly at-chance group-level performance in resolving temporary ambiguities is the product of bimodal distributions in interpretation within the population. Lingering misinterpretations in some speakers are predicted by a combination of sociodemographic and cognitive factors. Critically, we find that even among participants willing to adopt non-binary language, accurate interpretation of the non-binary singular they is mediated by cognitive resources. The findings bear implications for accounts of language change and the adoption of non-binary pronouns in bilingual communities as a result of individual variability. We outline a proposal for a conceptual Integrated Model of Adaptation to Grammar In Evolution ("IMAGINE").

### Do smiling faces change children's minds? Testing evaluative conditioning in 3-6‑year‑olds
**כתב עת:** 📄 Journal of Experimental Child Psychology (J Exp Child Psychol) *(IF: 3.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Balas R et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 6

This study examined whether preschoolers form preferences for shapes paired with happy or sad faces (evaluative conditioning) and whether the effect varies with age or with the number of different faces paired with each shape. Sixty-one children aged 3-6 years viewed four neutral shapes during a brief conditioning movie. Each shape was assigned to one of four conditions, in which it was paired with a single happy face, a single sad face, five different happy faces, or five different sad faces. Shape preferences were measured before and after the movie, along with memory for the pairings. Children showed clear pre-existing preferences for some shapes, and the pattern differed by age. After accounting for these starting preferences, two additive advantages emerged (for positive over negative faces and for multiple over single faces) and their combination in the shape paired with multiple smiling faces was the only condition to produce a reliable shift from zero. The other three conditions did not produce reliable change, and children's exact age and memory for the pairings did not predict change. These findings suggest that evaluative conditioning in preschoolers is more selective than typically assumed, with the number of different faces paired with a shape emerging as a key factor. The results also highlight the importance of accounting for children's pre-existing preferences when measuring acquired evaluative learning.

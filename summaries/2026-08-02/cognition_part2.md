### Using the dynamics of children's reaching to examine the role of inhibitory control in social cognitive development
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Psychological Review (Psychol Rev) *(IF: 12.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Sobel DM | **תאריך:** 2026 Jul 27

Numerous studies in cognitive development have examined the relation between children's developing inhibitory control and their social cognition, including false belief, selective trust, and children's understanding of fairness norms. This research typically finds correlations between performance on inhibitory control measures and developmental milestones in social cognitive development, such as success on false belief measures, actively recognizing deceptive agents, and rejecting advantageous inequities. These studies, however, do not describe the role of inhibitory control as children are making these social cognitive inferences. We use reach trajectory tracking-a method commonly used with adults-to isolate the role of distinct inhibitory control mechanisms as children make such inferences. After describing this method, we present three case studies that describe how different inhibitory mechanisms are involved in understanding others' false beliefs, selective learning from human versus nonhuman stimuli, and making inferences about fair resource distributions. Taken together, these studies suggest that different inhibitory mechanisms relate to social evaluations about other people as opposed to evaluations of the information generated by others. Further, reach trajectory tracking offers a natural way of studying the role of inhibitory control in cognitive development, as children are making decisions. ### Slow Oscillations Modulate Overnight Brain Changes in Working Memory Function
**כתב עת:** 📄 Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience (J Cogn Neurosci) *(IF: 4.4)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Zhang J et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Jul 17

Working memory (WM), the transient storage and manipulation of information, is a cognitive function that can improve with training. Accumulating evidence suggests that sleep, particularly slow oscillations (SOs) that occur during nonrapid eye movement sleep, supports this improvement. Yet, how sleep and SOs in particular relate to neural processes supporting WM remains poorly understood. In this study, we investigated how WM-related neural activity evolves across nocturnal sleep and how these changes relate to neural processing during SOs. Participants performed a WM task during fMRI before and after sleep, with simultaneous EEG-fMRI capturing neural activity during the first 2.5 hr of sleep. Our results showed significant overnight changes in WM-related activity, characterized by reduced recruitment of the dorsal precuneus during WM encoding/maintenance phases and frontal regions during the retrieval phase of WM. In addition, sleep increased item-specific reinstatement in a sensory processing region during WM. This pattern of results suggests a combined influence of sleep on enhancing sensory reinstatement while reducing potential top-down control (i.e., reduced parietal and frontal activity). Critically, SO-related activity was directly linked to overnight activity changes: Stronger SO activation in the premotor cortex and ventromedial pFC predicted greater overnight reductions in WM-related activity, and multivoxel analyses in the ventral attention network revealed a parallel relationship. These findings suggest that SOs play a critical role in WM function by facilitating the reorganization of WM-related processing.

### Exploring Neural Mechanisms Underlying Error-related Impairments in Active Working Memory Suggests an Adaptive Shielding of Contents during Cognitive Control
**כתב עת:** 📄 Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience (J Cogn Neurosci) *(IF: 4.4)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Choo Y et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Jul 17

Goal-directed behavior relies on cognitive flexibility-the ability to rapidly adapt ongoing thoughts and behaviors while preserving task-relevant information. The performance monitoring system optimizes such behavior by detecting and evaluating errors, whereas the working memory (WM) system maintains relevant information and protects it from interference. We previously showed that motor errors impaired active WM maintenance (Error-Related Impairment of Active working Memory [ERIAM]; [Wessel, J. R., Jiang, J., & Stolley, J. J. Action errors impair active working memory maintenance. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 151, 1325-1340, 2022]). Here, we investigated the source of ERIAM by tracking the contralateral delay activity (CDA), a neural marker of visual WM maintenance, throughout the error-making process. Forty-two participants maintained visual information in WM while performing a flanker task during the delay period. Consistent with prior results, a significant ERIAM effect occurred. We observed the canonical CDA before flanker task onset, and this CDA declined after the flanker task began. Following the flanker response, however, we observed a sustained contralateral positivity (CDAp). Critically, CDA amplitudes did not differ between motor correct and error trials before the flanker task, ruling out a general performance deficit. The CDA was also unaffected immediately after flanker onset, ruling out a perceptual interference explanation. Neural differences emerged only after the flanker response, supporting a genuinely error-related origin of the ERIAM effect. Importantly, CDAp was larger following correct responses than errors, and greater CDAp modulation predicted a smaller ERIAM effect. ### Aha-related surprise tracks violated expectations at multiple levels in perceptual insight
**כתב עת:** 📄 Cognition *(IF: 3.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Gega P et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Jul 29

Insight is often accompanied by an Aha experience, but why some solutions feel especially insightful remains unclear. The insight-as-prediction-error (IPE) account proposes that this experience arises when a solution violates expectations formed before recognition. Here, we tested whether this account generalizes to perceptual insight and whether Aha-related surprise reflects violated expectations at multiple levels. Across two online experiments (Experiment 1: N = 50; Experiment 2: N = 43), participants briefly previewed Mooney images, rated expected solvability, attempted to identify the hidden object, and then rated the Aha dimensions surprise, suddenness, pleasure, and certainty. In both experiments, meta-cognitive prediction error (PE) about solvability reliably predicted greater surprise on correctly solved trials. Longer preview durations increased expected solvability (from 250 to 650 ms), suggesting that these expectations form rapidly from minimal perceptual input but are not simply accurate forecasts of later identification success. Critically, content-based PE-defined as mismatch between anticipated and actual solution content-explained additional variance in surprise beyond meta-cognitive PE and showed clearer selectivity for surprise than the other Aha dimensions. Together, these findings extend the IPE account to perceptual insight and support a multi-level account in which insight-related surprise is associated with trial-wise violations of expectations about both whether a solution will occur and what that solution will be.

### Spatial biases of covert attention with dynamic gaze position
**כתב עת:** 📄 Cognition *(IF: 3.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Cai Y et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Jul 25

Humans express multiple biases in the spatial distribution of attention. While overt attention tends to center on visual content, or show a slight bias to the left, it is still largely unknown whether covert attention (attention shifts without eye movements) also exhibits spatial biases similarly to those of overt attention. The current study assesses the spatial biases of covert attention with two experiments. In Experiment 1, participants overtly tracked a moving dot and covertly discriminated probes surrounding it. Discrimination performance was higher for probes presented at the screen center independent of gaze locations, indicating that covert attention expresses a spatiotopic center biases similar to that of overt attention. Moreover, when the gaze was on the left, performance was enhanced towards the screen center, relative to the gaze position, indicating that covert attention also exhibits a retinotopic bias towards the center when overt attention is leftward. Experiment 2 assessed the distribution of covert attention during free-viewing movies, indexing covert attention via pupillary light responses. The results replicated Experiment 1 by showing a similar retinotopic center bias of covert attention when overt attention was leftward but not rightward. Together, the results imply that covert attention bias depends on the allocation of overt attention which is often leftward and may serve to compensate for the hemispace unattended by overt attention.

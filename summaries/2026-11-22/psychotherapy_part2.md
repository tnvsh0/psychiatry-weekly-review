### Sustainability of Treatment Success 5 Years after Exposure-Based Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy for Anxiety Disorders
**כתב עת:** ⭐ Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics (Psychother Psychosom) *(IF: 15.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Pittig A et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 16

INTRODUCTION: Achieving sustainable success in the treatment of anxiety disorders remains a central objective in mental health care. Although research has demonstrated the short-term efficacy of psychotherapy, evidence regarding long-term sustainability is limited. This study examined treatment outcomes 5 years after prediction-error-based exposure therapy.

METHODS: For 355 patients (616 eligible; 58% follow-up rate), newly collected follow-up data on anxiety symptoms and psychosocial functioning were compared to pretreatment, posttreatment, and 6-month follow-up data from a multicenter clinical trial characterized by high treatment fidelity.

RESULTS: Improvements in anxiety symptoms and psychosocial functioning that were evident at posttreatment and 6-month follow-up were largely preserved after 5 years. No significant differences emerged between randomized groups of temporally intensified and non-intensified exposure. From 6 months to 5 years, overall remission rates remained stable, with the majority of patients exhibiting no reliable change in symptom severity. Reliable relapse occurred in 4.9% and reliable new remission in 6.5%. Most patients (63.4%) did not seek additional treatment. Among those who did, depression (64.2%) and anxiety (60.5%) were the most frequently cited reasons, although only a minority (6.0%) sought further treatment exclusively for anxiety. Additional treatment during the follow-up period was associated with higher symptom severity throughout assessments.

CONCLUSION: These findings highlight the sustainability and long-term public health benefits of exposure-based CBT for anxiety disorders. Most patients do not need additional treatments for mental disorders even 5 years after treatment. Nevertheless, further efforts are needed to optimize interventions for those patients who do not achieve remission or experience relapse.

### From evidence to systems: A roadmap for scaling evidence-based psychological interventions via task-sharing across global contexts
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Clinical Psychology Review (Clin Psychol Rev) *(IF: 12.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Singla DR et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 26

The care gap for psychological interventions for common mental health conditions remains a global challenge. The scaling up of psychological interventions, often through "task-sharing" (training non-mental health specialist providers in community or routine care settings), has emerged as a promising strategy to expand access globally. Yet few interventions have been scaled sustainably in real-world settings. Using the RE-AIM and WHO Health Systems frameworks, we conducted a multiple-case narrative review inclusive of five case studies to illustrate how evidence-based psychological interventions for common mental health problems have been scaled in diverse contexts (United Kingdom, Europe, South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa) and then, applying a political economy perspective, we synthesized the political, economic, and health system factors that shaped their trajectories. We found that scale is most successful when interventions: (1) leverage trusted workforces with political and institutional support for expanded roles; (2) reduce burdens on patients and providers through simplified and accessible service-delivery models; (3) align with high-priority and politically salient policy agendas; (4) are supported by robust monitoring and information systems that enable accountability and legitimacy; (5) are implemented through partnerships between government, technical, or funding actors; (6) remain adaptive to shifts in political will and contextual conditions; (7) are catalyzed by digital technologies that strengthen training, supervision, quality assurance, and routine outcome monitoring; and (8) are supported by sustained advocacy efforts. These lessons highlight that successfully scaling psychological interventions requires a systems and political framework in research design, implementation and dissemination. We conclude by outlining implications for policy and practice to embed interventions within existing service platforms and governance structures to support equitable, sustainable scale-up; and for implementation science, emphasizing the need for future evaluations to measure reach, adoption, and fidelity-to-function.

By recognizing the existence of conservative infantile amnesia, psychoanalysis introduced a new category of temporality: timelessness. The contents thus subjected to amnesia escape the influence of time. This unconscious psychic reality is established to counteract the regressive force emanating from the id, in order to fulfil a function: that of thwarting the tendencies to revert to a prior state, even to the inorganic, which are inherent in every drive, and to prevent the disappearance of libidinal cathexes into a 'traumatic timelessness'. Through the regression of sleep, dream work can use this tendency towards extinction to create new psychic content and regenerate narcissistic and object-related cathexis. The dream is the time machine, the fountain of youth available to human beings. The oscillation between experiences of disappearance and those of regeneration, between an endo-perception of discontinuity characterized by experiences of disappearance and eternity, lies at the origin of the notion of temporality, as well as of many devices designed to create the illusion of continuity. Extinction cannot be represented; it is perceived through the traumatic emotions of terror and anxiety. This is why time can only be embodied by the devices that measure it - that is to say, by a method consisting of retaining that which tends to disappear.

### Transformations in play. Dyadic work inspired by psychoanalytic infant observation
**כתב עת:** 📄 The International Journal of Psychoanalysis (Int J Psychoanal) *(IF: 1.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Wakelyn J | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct

The article explores the psychoanalytic foundations of Watch Me Play! (WMP), an applied approach first developed to promote understanding of the needs of babies and young children in care, now more widely used to support parent-child relationships and child development. Watch Me Play! is derived from psychoanalytic infant observation and underpinned by key concepts in child development research such as mind-mindedness, joint attention and contingent responsiveness. The relevance of the central psychoanalytic concepts of attention and time in the thinking of Freud, Bion and Birksted-Breen is illustrated in clinical examples from work with the group for whom the approach was first developed: infants and young children who have experienced abuse, neglect and disrupted attachments, together with their foster carers or adoptive parents.

### Effects of cognitive behavioral therapy on psychological and physical outcomes in adults with rheumatoid arthritis: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
**כתב עת:** 📄 Front Public Health | **סוג מחקר:** מטה-אנליזה | **מחברים:** Zhang Y et al. | **תאריך:** 2026

Background Pain and functional limitations associated with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) can substantially impair patients’ psychological and physical well-being. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) may alleviate psychological and physical symptoms in patients with RA by modifying maladaptive cognitions and behaviors and promoting adaptive coping with the disease. However, the effect of CBT in RA patients has not been systematically established. Methods PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science were systematically searched from database inception to July 9, 2026, for relevant studies. Study selection, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment were independently performed by two reviewers. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or adjudicated by a third reviewer. Meta-analyses were primarily implemented through random-effects models. Effect estimates were presented as standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Results Twelve RCTs involving 1,538 participants with RA were incorporated. CBT was associated with lower levels of depression (SMD = −0.35, 95% CI: −0.55 to −0.14, low-certainty evidence), anxiety (SMD = −0.20, 95% CI: −0.32 to −0.08, moderate-certainty evidence), and fatigue (SMD = −0.44, 95% CI: −0.66 to −0.22, low-certainty evidence). At the 6-month follow-up, the pooled SMDs were −0.42 (95% CI, −0.63 to −0.20) for depression and −0.22 (95% CI, −0.43 to −0.01) for anxiety, based on six RCTs with available follow-up data. For sleep, the pooled estimate favored CBT, although this finding was based on only two studies and was supported by very-low-certainty evidence. No statistically significant between-group differences were noted for pain, physical function, or quality-of-life (QoL). Substantial heterogeneity was observed for depression and fatigue, and the 95% prediction intervals crossed the line of no effect for both outcomes. Exploratory subgroup analyses by CBT delivery format revealed no significant differences between subgroups. Conclusion The pooled estimates suggested that CBT may be associated with lower levels of fatigue, anxiety, and depression in patients with RA. However, substantial heterogeneity, prediction intervals crossing the line of no effect, and low certainty of evidence limit the reliability of these outcomes. Systematic review registration https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420261470674 , CRD420261470674.

1 Introduction Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disorder marked by synovial inflammation that can result in joint pain, structural damage, and functional limitations. The Global Burden of Disease study indicates that RA is still an important public health concern, with its prevalence projected to continue increasing ( 1 ). Persistent joint pain, functional limitations, and joint deformities can substantially impair the psychological and physical well-being of RA patients, further contributing to the overall burden of the disease. Depression and anxiety are among the most prevalent psychological comorbidities in RA patients. Evidence suggests that approximately 16.8% of patients with RA have major depressive disorder, and their risk of developing new-onset depression is about 47% higher than that of individuals without RA ( 2 , 3 ). Anxiety is also common and is linked to pain, impaired physical function, greater disease activity, and poorer quality-of-life (QoL) ( 4 ). Fatigue is another common and burdensome symptom in patients with RA. Recent evidence indicates that substantial fatigue may persist despite treatment with biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic therapies and is closely related to pain, impaired physical function, and psychological factors ( 5 ). The European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recognizes fatigue as a common and particularly challenging symptom of inflammatory rheumatic diseases and recommends its routine assessment and management in clinical practice ( 6 ). Sleep disturbances are also common in patients with RA and are associated with poorer physical and psychological QoL, further contributing to the overall disease burden ( 7 ). The combined burden of psychological and physical symptoms can substantially impair health-related QoL, with greater disease activity generally associated with poorer QoL ( 8 ). These challenges highlight the need for feasible and efficient complementary interventions to overcome the multidimensional burden of RA. CBT is a structured psychological intervention that incorporates cognitive restructuring, behavioral strategies, and problem-solving techniques to help patients with chronic conditions cope with symptoms and stress and improve psychological well-being, daily functioning, and adjustment to illness ( 9–11 ). Previous evidence has shown that CBT can attenuate symptoms of depression and anxiety ( 12 ) and is a safe and effective treatment for insomnia ( 13 , 14 ). Few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have examined CBT in RA patients. However, differences in intervention format, duration, and outcome assessment have led to inconsistent findings across studies. Prothero et al. conducted a systematic review of reviews on psychological interventions for RA and reported small to moderate improvements across several biopsychosocial outcomes ( 15 ). However, considerable heterogeneity existed among the included interventions, and the potential influence of group versus individual delivery was not examined. In 2020, Shen et al. performed a meta-analysis focusing specifically on CBT for RA and found significant improvements in anxiety, depression, and fatigue ( 16 ). However, the analysis included only six RCTs, searched the literature up to December 2018, and did not perform subgroup analyses. Since then, five additional RCTs included in the present review have been published. These studies have expanded the evidence base in terms of sample size, CBT delivery formats, and outcome measures. They also provide an opportunity not only to update the overall evidence on CBT effectiveness but also to examine whether treatment effects differ across clinically relevant delivery formats, such as individual versus group CBT and online versus offline CBT. Therefore, an updated meta-analysis was needed. ### Mindfulness and psychological well-being in older adults: the effect of reminiscence therapy
**כתב עת:** 📄 BMC Geriatr | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Çiftci MÇ, Yildiz GN | **תאריך:** 2026 Oct 13

Background This study aimed to examine the effects of reminiscence therapy on psychological well-being and mindfulness among community-dwelling older adults. Methods This pre-test–post-test randomized controlled trial was conducted at XX Community Health Center between May and June 2025. The study was completed with 90 community-dwelling older adults who were randomly assigned to an experimental group ( n = 45) or a control group ( n = 45). Data were collected using the Descriptive Characteristics Form, the Psychological Well-Being Scale, and the Mindful Attention Awareness Scale. Participants in the experimental group received a structured 10-session reminiscence therapy program, whereas the control group received no intervention during the study period. Outcomes were assessed before and immediately after the intervention. A two-way mixed analysis of variance was used to examine the effects of group, time, and the group × time interaction. Results A significant group × time interaction was found for mindful awareness, indicating that changes in Mindful Attention Awareness Scale scores differed significantly between the experimental and control groups, F (1, 88) = 31.415, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.263. Mindful awareness scores increased in the experimental group from 56.71 ± 16.70 at pre-test to 64.27 ± 16.57 at post-test, whereas scores decreased in the control group from 58.00 ± 18.85 to 54.64 ± 17.87. A significant group × time interaction was also observed for psychological well-being, F (1, 88) = 15.196, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.147. Psychological well-being scores increased in the experimental group from 37.00 ± 9.35 to 40.80 ± 10.34, whereas they decreased in the control group from 40.53 ± 14.74 to 36.53 ± 10.91. Conclusions Reminiscence therapy was associated with significantly more favorable changes in mindful awareness and psychological well-being compared with the control condition. These findings suggest that reminiscence therapy may be a useful psychosocial intervention for supporting positive psychological functioning and mindful awareness among community-dwelling older adults. Further studies with larger and more diverse samples, active control conditions, and long-term follow-up assessments are needed to confirm the sustainability and generalizability of these findings. Clinical trial number ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06964399 .

Introduction Improved healthcare services and advances associated with socioeconomic development have contributed to increased life expectancy, resulting in a rapid increase in the proportion of older adults worldwide [ 1 ]. As the older population grows, greater attention is required to the physical, psychological, and social changes associated with aging and their potential effects on health and well-being [ 2 ]. In this context, non-pharmacological interventions have gained importance as approaches that can support healthy aging and address the psychological and social needs of older adults [ 3 , 4 ]. One such intervention is reminiscence therapy, which involves helping individuals recall, reflect on, and share past life events and meaningful experiences, either verbally or silently, often with the support of concrete cues [ 5 ]. The therapeutic purpose of reminiscence, however, may differ according to the characteristics and needs of the target population. While reminiscence-based interventions have also been used in individuals with dementia or cognitive impairment, the present study focuses on community-dwelling older adults without severe dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, related neurological disorders, or major psychiatric disorders. In this population, reminiscence is considered primarily as a psychosocial intervention that may support psychological well-being, life satisfaction, personal identity, self-esteem, autobiographical integration, and emotional adjustment. Consistent with this perspective, reminiscence may help older adults feel stronger, more valued, and more confident and may contribute to the development, maintenance, or reconstruction of personal identity [ 2 , 4 ]. Importantly, evidence obtained directly from healthy older adults supports this psychosocial perspective. Viguer et al. (2017) found that a 10-session reminiscence intervention in 160 healthy older adults reduced depressed mood and improved life satisfaction and psychological well-being; improvements in life satisfaction and some dimensions of psychological well-being were maintained at the three-month follow-up. These findings are particularly relevant to the present study because both the target population and the psychosocial outcomes closely correspond to those examined here [ 6 ]. Mindfulness refers to the capacity to attend to present-moment experiences with awareness and a non-judgmental, accepting attitude toward thoughts, emotions, and bodily sensations. In the present study, mindfulness is not conceptualized as the direct product of a formal mindfulness-based intervention. Rather, it is considered a potentially relevant psychological outcome of the reflective processes involved in reminiscence therapy. Reminiscence requires older adults to deliberately evoke, attend to, elaborate, and reinterpret autobiographical experiences. Through this process, individuals may become more aware of their emotional responses and personal meanings associated with past experiences, observe these experiences from a reflective perspective, and integrate them into their current understanding of the self. Such reflective engagement may be associated with greater awareness of emotional responses and personal meanings and may encourage individuals to adopt a more reflective and accepting orientation toward their experiences. Accordingly, although reminiscence therapy differs conceptually from mindfulness-based interventions, its emphasis on attentive reflection, emotional awareness, acceptance, and reinterpretation of autobiographical experiences provides a theoretical rationale for examining mindfulness as an outcome. Mindfulness was therefore included not because reminiscence therapy explicitly teaches mindfulness practices, but because changes in awareness and the way individuals relate to their internal experiences may accompany the reminiscence process [ 7 ]. Accordingly, mindfulness was examined as a theoretically relevant psychological outcome rather than as a direct or expected consequence of formal mindfulness training. In the present study, psychological well-being is conceptualized in accordance with the framework underlying the Psychological Well-Being Scale developed by Diener et al. (2010), rather than as a general construct encompassing all dimensions of subjective or psychological well-being [ 8 ]. Within this framework, psychological well-being refers to flourishing or positive psychological functioning, reflected in perceived purpose and meaning in life, supportive and rewarding relationships, engagement in daily activities, feelings of competence and self-respect, optimism, and the perception that one is functioning well [ 9 ]. This conceptualization is particularly relevant to healthy aging, as maintaining positive psychological functioning becomes increasingly important in the context of the physical, psychological, and social changes associated with later life [ 10 ]. Reminiscence therapy may support these aspects of psychological functioning by enabling older adults to recall, share, and reinterpret meaningful autobiographical experiences, thereby facilitating meaning-making, self-evaluation, emotional balance, and a sense of continuity in life. Accordingly, psychological well-being was selected as a primary outcome of the present study because changes resulting from the reminiscence process may be reflected in older adults’ perceived meaning, competence, positive relationships, and overall optimal psychological functioning.

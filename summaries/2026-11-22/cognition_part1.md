### Uncovering internal sensory evidence reveals the nature of confidence computation in multi-alternative perceptual decision making
**כתב עת:** 🔷 Psychological Review (Psychol Rev) *(IF: 12.0)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Xue K et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 16

A central goal of perceptual decision making is to determine the computations underlying choice and confidence in complex, multi-alternative tasks. However, revealing these computations requires knowledge of the internal sensory evidence upon which the computations operate. Unfortunately, unlike for 2-choice tasks, there is no established method in multi-alternative tasks to determine how stimuli are transformed into internal sensory evidence. Here we address this critical gap by developing a novel modeling paradigm that reveals how the brain transforms external stimuli into internal sensory evidence during multi-alternative decision making in the context of numerosity tasks. Across three experiments that feature 2-, 3-, and 5-choice conditions, we show that the internal sensory evidence in our numerosity task can be described with a simple, 2-parameter equation that fits data for up to 12 conditions. Critically, the ability to model the internal sensory evidence allowed us to test six theories of confidence computation. Our results support a model where confidence reflects the raw evidence difference between the top two alternatives. These results establish a new numerosity paradigm in which a 2-parameter equation can be used to determine the internal sensory evidence for a potentially unlimited number of multi-alternative conditions and reveal the confidence computation in multi-alternative tasks.

Introduction Humans possess the metacognitive ability to assess the accuracy of their own decisions through confidence ratings ( Fleming, 2024 ; Mamassian, 2020 ; Rahnev, 2021 ). Accurate metacognition is important in many domains, such as learning and the ability to engage in sequential decisions ( Aguilar-Lleyda et al., 2020 ; Hainguerlot et al., 2018 ; Schunk & Ertmer, 2000 ). A critical goal in the field is identifying the computational mechanisms behind confidence in complex, multi-alternative tasks ( Rahnev et al., 2022 ). However, revealing these computations requires knowledge of the internal sensory evidence upon which the computations operate. But unlike for 2-choice tasks, there is currently no well-established paradigm that allows modeling how external stimuli are converted into internal sensory evidence for multi-alternative tasks ( Miyoshi et al., 2024 ; Shekhar & Rahnev, 2024a ). Only a few papers have examined the computation underlying confidence in n-choice tasks via cognitive modeling. Li & Ma (2020) designed a cloud task where participants indicated the color of the cloud from which a black dot originated. Comay et al. (2023) used a different task where participants judged which of three shapes has the largest area. The two papers reached different results about the confidence computation, with Li & Ma favoring a model where confidence reflects the difference in probability between the top two options, whereas Comay et al. favored a model where confidence reflects the difference in probability between the top choice and the average of all other choices. More importantly, all models tested by Li & Ma (2020) and Comay et al. (2023) were probability-based models. Such models are known as “explicit-Bayes” models ( Xue & Rahnev, 2026 ): they necessitate a complex computation that considers the likelihood of all possible stimulus states given a certain percept and applies Bayes’ theorem to convert these likelihood functions into probabilities ( Locke et al., 2022 ). This contrasts fundamentally with evidence-based models, which give confidence based directly on the raw internal sensory evidence without requiring complex inference to derive probabilities ( Locke et al., 2022 ; Xue et al., 2024 ). More recently, Miyoshi & Sakamoto (2025) used an evidence accumulation framework and compared a model where confidence is based on a combination of early and late evidence with a model where confidence is based on the Softmax computation. Overall, no prior work has used cognitive modeling to compare the full range of viable confidence models, including probability- and evidence-based models, in the context of multi-alternative tasks. Some prior work has compared probability- and evidence-based models in the context of 2-choice tasks ( Adler & Ma, 2018 ; Aitchison et al., 2015 ; Locke et al., 2022 ; Xue et al., 2024 ). Aitchison et al. (2015) used a two-interval forced-choice contrast discrimination task and found that confidence appeared Bayesian (probability-based) when subjects reported their decision and confidence sequentially, but results were mixed in a simultaneous response design. Adler & Ma (2018) used two orientation categorization tasks that varied sensory reliability and found evidence against a fully Bayesian, probability-based account of confidence, but their preferred heuristic models do not fit neatly into a simple probability- versus evidence-based categorization. Locke et al. (2022) used a two-interval spatial discrimination task and found that most observers were better described by evidence-based models than by probability-based models, although individual differences existed. Xue et al. (2024) used an orientation discrimination task and informed participants about the difficulty level of each trial, finding that evidence-based models better fit the data than probability-based ones. Thus, the literature on 2-choice tasks tends to favor evidence-based models, but not only is this problem still open for 2-choice tasks, it is unclear how the 2-choice results would generalize to multi-alternative tasks. A recent paper used artificial neural networks to compare probability- and evidence-based models in multi-alternative tasks ( Shekhar et al., 2025 ). Participants completed an 8-choice handwritten digit discrimination task and their performance was compared to that of RTNet, a neural network model of human perceptual decisions ( Rafiei et al., 2024 ). Shekhar et al. then generated confidence ratings produced by RTNet using both probability- and evidence-based computations and compared these ratings to the ones obtained from the human participants. Shekhar et al. found that the best match was provided by a model where confidence reflects the difference in evidence for the top two choices. However, the use of artificial neural networks instead of cognitive modeling has several limitations. First, unlike cognitive models that can be tuned to human behavior, RTNet provides only an imperfect model of human internal activations ( Rafiei et al., 2024 ). Second, the approach involved no formal model fitting, as that is computationally difficult for neural networks, and instead simply equated RTNet’s accuracy to the average human accuracy. Finally, the qualitative signatures examined, including effects of task difficulty, speed-accuracy trade-off, and the folded-X pattern, are well-established behavioral benchmarks for confidence models in general but do not specifically probe the computational challenges unique to multi-alternative tasks, such as how additional weak options or changes in the number of choices affect confidence. Thus, while artificial neural networks allow modeling of very complex tasks (e.g., handwritten digit discrimination), they cannot yet replace cognitive modeling, which allows close fitting of individual participants and the use of tasks that probe more detailed behavioral signatures. Our study directly addresses these critical gaps by building a novel modeling paradigm that successfully establishes the transformation of stimuli into internal sensory evidence for multi-alternative numerosity tasks. This advancement enables the mapping of a potentially unlimited number of conditions in multi-alternative numerosity tasks using as few as two free parameters, while providing a broader template for analogous mappings in other perceptual domains. ### Event-general concepts guide semantic generalizations during verb learning cross-linguistically
**כתב עת:** 📄 Cognition *(IF: 3.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Lee SH, Papafragou A | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 17

Languages vary in how they map the conceptual components of events onto verbs. For example, some languages, such as English, often encode the manner of spontaneous motion in verbs (e.g., run), while others, such as Spanish, make greater use of verbs that encode the path of motion (e.g., enter). Such verb lexicalization biases are known to be malleable for spontaneous-motion verbs in English. In the present study, we probed the scope and malleability of such biases cross-linguistically. We found that both English-speaking and Spanish-speaking learners can learn new verb lexicalization biases from a few exposures and extend them to novel verb meanings within the same semantic class - in both previously documented and new domains (spontaneous motion - Experiment 1; caused motion - Experiment 2). Furthermore, speakers of both languages could extend verb biases across semantic classes (from spontaneous motion to caused motion - Experiment 1; and from caused motion to spontaneous motion - Experiment 2). These findings indicate that verb lexicalization biases are flexible higher-order semantic generalizations that draw from conceptual dimensions that cross-cut specific event types and support verb learning across languages.

### Exploring cognitive and emotional predictors of adaptive information search
**כתב עת:** 📄 Cognition *(IF: 3.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Abel L et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 16

Adaptive information search often requires balancing competing cognitive goals. In selecting an information search strategy, individuals may face a trade-off between the speed and efficiency of information acquisition. Evidence suggests that individual differences explain variability in how people trade-off deliberation time and accuracy when making decisions. However, less is known about how individuals might differ in how they balance search speed and efficiency in information-rich environments that require sequential information sampling and flexible goal coordination. In our exploratory study, N = 127 participants played a novel variant of the code-breaking game Mastermind in three conditions: in the speed condition, participants were incentivized for rapid game completion; in the efficiency condition for using few queries; and in the mixed condition for both. We assessed individual differences in numeracy, working memory, state and trait emotion, personality and thinking styles in a pretest. Our results suggest that both numeracy and working memory predict information search behaviour across these conditions. Higher numeracy and working memory scores were associated with improved performance and more effective information search strategies across conditions, particularly under time pressure. Emotional predictors showed smaller and condition-dependent effects. State emotional dominance was negatively associated with performance in the mixed condition, suggesting a modulating role of emotional dominance in the coordination of competing demands. State emotional valence was negatively associated with performance under the speed incentive, potentially reflecting a relationship between emotional valence and time-sensitive goal-directed behaviour. Our exploratory findings stress the relevance of considering individual differences in research on adaptive information search.

### Mind the cueing design: Order effects mask benefits of congruency pre-cues
**כתב עת:** 📄 Cognition *(IF: 3.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Nuño CO et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 20

Evidence that individuals proactively adjust cognitive control in response to pre-cues signaling upcoming distraction has been surprisingly sparse, raising questions about the limits of volitional control. The present study tested whether this pattern reflects genuine constraints in proactive control engagement or methodological limitations of standard pre-cueing designs. Across two pre-registered experiments, we examined the role of cueing order (i.e., whether participants started with informative or uninformative pre-cue blocks) in the commonly used between-block design and evaluated an alternative within-block design in which informative and uninformative pre-cues were randomly intermixed. In Experiment 1, the standard approach of collapsing cueing order conditions yielded null pre-cue benefits for the between-block comparison, mirroring what is often observed in the literature. However, when considering cueing order, benefits emerged when informative cues were encountered first (cued-first group) but not when they appeared second (uncued-first group). In contrast, the within-block comparison produced reliable pre-cue benefits regardless of cueing order. Experiment 2 replicated the within-block pre-cue benefit using a fully intermixed design without the cueing order manipulation. Together, these findings indicate that sparse evidence for pre-cue benefits may partly reflect design-related constraints rather than limits in proactive control. Why pre-cue benefits depend on cueing order remains an open question, but the present findings reveal that the intermixed cueing design offers a more sensitive and reliable method for detecting cue-based control adjustments-with important implications for prominent theories of cognitive control.

### Assessing advanced theory of mind in middle childhood: psychometric evaluation and responsiveness to training of a comprehensive AToM battery
**כתב עת:** 📄 Journal of Experimental Child Psychology (J Exp Child Psychol) *(IF: 3.5)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Warnke J et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 13

This study examined the psychometric properties and responsiveness to training of a comprehensive Advanced Theory of Mind (AToM) Battery in middle childhood. In Study 1, 168 eight- to nine-year-old children participated (M age  = 8.61 years, SD = 0.64; 88 girls, 79 boys, one preferred not to say). Children completed the AToM Battery, the Strange Stories task, and measures of language, inhibition, working memory, and nonverbal intelligence. Confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) largely supported the proposed three-factor structure. At the same time, the CFA indicated that several items, particularly within the Social Reasoning component, showed comparatively weak loadings. The AToM Battery showed moderate convergent validity with the Strange Stories task, r = .37, p < .001. Language ability significantly predicted overall AToM performance, R 2  = .16, with effects primarily driven by the Social Reasoning component, R 2  = .15. In Study 2, 126 children completed the battery at pretest, posttest, and follow-up after participating in either a conversation-based training or a control training. Linear mixed-effects models indicated general improvements over time across groups. Training-specific gains emerged most consistently for Recognition of Transgressions of Social Norms at posttest [t(247.20) = 3.45, p < .001] and follow-up [t(247.20) = 3.23, p = .001]. Stability correlations in the control group indicated moderate temporal stability over a three-month interval, r = .55, p < .001. Overall, the findings support the AToM Battery as a multidimensional and psychometrically useful measure of advanced social-cognitive reasoning, while highlighting the importance of considering language demands and subscale-specific responsiveness.

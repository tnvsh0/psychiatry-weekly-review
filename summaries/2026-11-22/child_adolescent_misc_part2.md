### Antipsychotic Use in Children and Adolescents with Autism Spectrum Disorder
**כתב עת:** 📄 Child Adolesc Psychiatr Clin N Am | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Register-Brown K, Powers J | **תאריך:** 2027 Jan

Antipsychotic medications may be effective for the management of irritability, aggression, and other mental health disorders in youth with autism spectrum disorder and other neurodevelopmental disorders including intellectual disability. While these medications may significantly improve safety outcomes and quality of life, autistic youth may be at greater risk of overprescribing, side effects, and insufficient monitoring. Best practice includes clarifying target symptoms/behaviors, using lowest effective doses, ensuring first-line behavioral and environmental interventions, collaboratively working with parents/guardians and other care team members, utilizing creative strategies to follow gold-standard monitoring guidelines, and deprescribing when able.

### Clozapine in Children and Adolescents: Indications, Efficacy, and Safety
**כתב עת:** 📄 Child Adolesc Psychiatr Clin N Am | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר סקירה | **מחברים:** Bhatt A et al. | **תאריך:** 2027 Jan

Clozapine is the most effective antipsychotic but it remains underutilized. This review synthesizes evidence for clozapine use in children and adolescents, including additional indications such as suicidality, aggression, autism, and catatonia. Children have differences in pharmacokinetics, dosing, and side effect profiles. Although side effects can include agranulocytosis, myocarditis, and metabolic effects, regular laboratory and vital sign monitoring and adjunctive treatments can mitigate these risks. Persistent barriers often delay initiation, but increased education and systems-level changes can reduce barriers to clozapine use. Recent regulatory changes and emerging evidence support earlier and more widespread use of clozapine in appropriately selected pediatric populations.

### Examining the Effectiveness of a Dual Executive Function Training Package: Protocol for a Randomized Controlled Trial
**כתב עת:** 📄 JMIR Res Protoc | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Poon K et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 19

Abstract Background Executive function (EF) refers to the cognitive processes that enable goal-directed regulation of thoughts, emotions, and behaviors. Two interrelated domains are commonly distinguished: “cool” EF, which involves abstract cognitive processes, and “hot” EF, which involves regulation in emotionally or motivationally charged contexts. Children with special educational needs (SEN) frequently show difficulties across both domains, yet most interventions target one in isolation. Objective The present study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a dual EF training package for 3 of the most prevalent SEN groups: attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), specific learning difficulties (SpLD), and autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Methods A total of 288 primary school students aged 6 to 12 years with a diagnosis of ADHD, ASD, or SpLD will be recruited. Participants will be randomly assigned to either an intervention or a waitlist control group, stratified by diagnosis. Each intervention group (n=48) will receive the dual EF training program. Participants in the waitlist control group will continue to receive usual educational and clinical support (standard care) during the study period and will be offered the dual EF program upon completion of the study. Preintervention and postintervention assessments will include neuropsychological tests of EF, teacher- and parent-reported behavioral ratings, and measures of disability impact, school satisfaction, and social participation. Changes in outcome measures will be examined using a mixed-design analysis, with diagnosis (ADHD, ASD, and SpLD) and group (experimental vs waitlist control) as between-participant factors and time (pretest vs posttest) as the within-participant factor. Baseline scores will be included as covariates where appropriate. The analysis will assess overall intervention effects and whether changes over time differ by intervention condition and diagnosis, with significant interactions followed by appropriate post hoc comparisons. Results The study was funded by Advance Children Medical and Education Foundation Limited from September 2025 to December 2026 and received ethical approval. Recruitment commenced in early 2026, and 30 participants with ADHD, 30 with ASD, and 45 with SpLD had been enrolled by June 2026. Baseline assessments were completed for all participants. Further data collection and data analysis are scheduled for completion by early 2027, with the results to be submitted for publication thereafter. Conclusions This study will evaluate the effectiveness of a theme-based dual EF program that integrates hot and cool EF within a structured curriculum. Findings will provide evidence of the potential of this approach to support EF and related outcomes in children with SEN and may inform future research and inclusive educational practices.

Introduction Executive functions (EFs) are a set of higher-order cognitive processes that enable children to regulate their thoughts, emotions, and behaviors in pursuit of goals. Core domains include working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility, often referred to as cool EFs because they are typically measured in abstract, decontextualized contexts [ 1 ]. Cool EFs are top-down cognitive processes that are logically based or mechanistic [ 2 ] and operate in affectively neutral contexts [ 3 ]. In contrast, hot EF refers to regulation in affectively or motivationally charged situations, encompassing delay discounting, affective/risky decision-making, and interpersonal and social behavior [ 3 ]. Both domains are crucial for children’s learning, adaptation, and participation, but they rely on partially distinct neural systems and show different developmental trajectories [ 4 ]. Importantly, recent models emphasize that hot and cool EF should not be viewed as isolated constructs but rather as interconnected components along a continuum [ 3 , 5 ]. In real-world learning and social contexts, cognitive and affective demands are often intertwined. For example, sustaining working memory while managing frustration or planning actions while negotiating peer dynamics highlights the need for interventions that address both hot and cool domains simultaneously. EF difficulties are particularly salient in the context of special educational needs (SEN). Children with SEN are disproportionately likely to present with impairments in both hot and cool EF, which underpin many of the learning and behavioral challenges they face in schools [ 6 ]. Among these, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and specific learning difficulties (SpLD) are the 3 most prevalent conditions worldwide, and they are consistently associated with executive dysfunction [ 7 , 8 ]. ADHD, ASD, and SpLD are all characterized by combined hot and cool EF deficits. In ADHD, cool EF weaknesses in working memory, inhibition, and sustained attention [ 6 , 9 ] are compounded by hot EF problems such as poor delay of gratification, impulsivity, and emotional dysregulation [ 10 ]. ASD presents with cool EF impairments in flexibility, planning, and working memory [ 11 , 12 ]. It also shows hot EF deficits in emotion regulation, reward processing, and theory of mind [ 13 , 14 ]. SpLD is often framed as academic skill deficits. However, it also involves cool EF challenges in working memory, processing speed, and attention [ 15 ], as well as hot EF challenges related to frustration tolerance and motivation [ 8 ]. Despite this dual profile, many existing interventions have focused disproportionately on one side of the EF spectrum, typically emphasizing cool EF while neglecting hot EF. To provide meaningful support for children with SEN, a more comprehensive approach is needed, one that systematically integrates both domains. Existing EF interventions provide partial solutions but leave important gaps. Cognitive training studies, such as Re et al [ 16 ], have demonstrated that children with ADHD symptoms can improve in attention, inhibition, and working memory following structured training. These domains reflect cool EF, and while the program successfully enhanced cognitive performance, the gains were largely confined to laboratory tasks, with modest evidence of generalization to socioemotional or classroom functioning. Hot EF, such as emotional regulation or social reasoning, was not directly addressed. A more ecological approach is seen in the cognitive-functional (Cog-Fun) intervention for ADHD [ 17 ]. Cog-Fun is an occupational therapy program that emphasizes metacognitive strategy use, planning, organization, and self-monitoring in daily life. Parents are also coached to support the application of strategies at home. While Cog-Fun is effective in improving organization, homework performance, and children’s self-directed behavior, its focus remains on cool EF, with only indirect influence on hot EF domains such as emotional regulation and motivation through parent involvement. More recent work has explicitly attempted to integrate hot EF. Shuai et al [ 18 ] developed an EF training package for children with ADHD that included not only core cool EF skills (working memory, inhibition, planning, flexibility, and verbal fluency) but also hot EF components such as emotional regulation. Their results showed improvements in both cognitive control and social understanding, underscoring the value of a dual-domain approach. However, the scope of hot EF was narrow, confined to theory of mind tasks and behavioral ratings of emotional control, and the program was delivered in a clinic-based format, limiting scalability and ecological validity in school contexts. ### Metaphorical perceptions of caregivers of children with autism spectrum disorder regarding the caregiving process
**כתב עת:** 📄 Eur J Pediatr | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Kaçan H, Durmuş M | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 20

This study aimed to investigate the metaphorical perceptions of caregivers of children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) regarding the caregiving process. The study was conducted with 46 participants responsible for the care of children with ASD. Data were collected between March and April 2026 using a metaphor form. The first part of the form included questions regarding sociodemographic characteristics, while the second part included the statement, "Caring for a child with autism spectrum disorder is like … because …" to identify participants' metaphorical perceptions of the caregiving process. Participants produced 46 metaphors, which were analyzed using content analysis and grouped into five themes: love, belonging, and hope; struggle and resilience; patience, acceptance, and adaptation; uncertainty, learning, and discovery; and intensive care and dependency. Regarding emotional valence, 28.3% of the metaphorical expressions were classified as positive, 37.0% as negative, and 34.7% as mixed. The theme of love, belonging, and hope had the highest frequency (28.3%). Descriptive cross-tabulations were also used to present the distribution of metaphor themes according to caregiving support, psychological support, and education about ASD. As no inferential statistical tests were performed, these distributions were interpreted descriptively and were not considered evidence of statistically significant differences, associations, or causal effects. Overall, the findings demonstrate that caregivers' metaphorical perceptions of caring for a child with ASD reflect a multidimensional experience encompassing emotional attachment and hope as well as struggle, adaptation, uncertainty, and dependency.

### Goal Management Training for Adolescents with ADHD: A Multi-Center Randomized Controlled Effectiveness Trial
**כתב עת:** 📄 Res Child Adolesc Psychopathol | **סוג מחקר:** RCT (מחקר אקראי מבוקר) | **מחברים:** Dyresen A et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 18

Non-pharmacological interventions targeting executive functions (EF), such as Goal Management Training (GMT), may augment treatment as usual (TAU) for adolescents with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). We examined whether GMT plus TAU improves EF, ADHD symptoms, and functional impairment more than TAU only. In this preregistered multi-center randomized controlled trial, 116 participants (M age = 14.69 years; 66% female) were randomized to GMT plus TAU or TAU only. GMT comprised seven weekly 2-hour group sessions, supplemented by parent and teacher sessions. TAU was standard outpatient mental health services. Outcomes were assessed at baseline and after 12 weeks. The primary outcome was the parent-reported Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function (BRIEF-2), Global Executive Composite (GEC). Secondary outcomes were teacher-, self-, and parent-reported BRIEF-2 subdomains; parent- and teacher-reported ADHD Rating Scale-IV scores; and parent-reported functional impairment measured with the Weiss Functional Impairment Rating Scale–Parent Report (WFIRS-P). GMT plus TAU showed no additional benefit over TAU only. Intention-to-treat analyses showed no significant between-group difference in GEC (adjusted b = − 2.12, 95% CI [− 7.64, 3.41], p = .449, d = − 0.14), parent-reported ADHD symptoms ( d = − 0.27, p = .323) or other secondary outcomes (all p > .10). Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov ( NCT05874791 ; registered April 14, 2023).

Introduction Clinical guidelines recommend a multimodal approach to the management of adolescent attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), typically combining psychoeducation, pharmacological treatment, and psychosocial interventions (Coghill et al., 2023 ; National Institute for Health and Care Excellence [NICE], 2018 ; Wolraich et al., 2019 ). Although pharmacological treatments demonstrate robust effects on core ADHD symptoms, the evidence for corresponding improvements in everyday functioning is less consistent (Cortese et al., 2018 ; Sibley et al., 2025 ). Reliance on medication as a standalone treatment may therefore restrict response domains, as pharmacotherapy alone often fails to yield improvements in executive function (EF) and real-world functioning domains more reliably addressed through non-pharmacological interventions (Sibley et al., 2025 ). Non-pharmacological and pharmacological interventions may thus be considered complementary as medication reduces core symptom burden, particularly in the short term, whereas psychosocial and training-based interventions target the EF difficulties essential to long-term everyday functioning (Sibley et al., 2025 ; Wolraich et al., 2019 ). EF refers to a set of top-down cognitive control processes, including working memory, planning, and self-regulation, that are orchestrated largely by the prefrontal cortex to enable goal-directed behavior (Diamond, 2013 ). EF difficulties are common among youth with ADHD and are associated with poorer academic performance (Dvorsky & Langberg, 2014 ), reduced quality of life (Barkley & Fischer, 2019 ), and elevated rates of comorbid psychopathology (Orm et al., 2023 ; Yang et al., 2022 ). Adolescence may represent a particularly opportune period for EF-focused interventions, as executive abilities continue to mature while academic, social, and organizational demands intensify (Ekman et al., 2022 ; Selemon, 2013 ). Several evidence-based psychosocial interventions for adolescents with ADHD target EF difficulties by combining direct skill-building with behavioral support (Rios-Davis et al., 2023 ; Sibley et al., 2023 ). This is particularly evident in programs addressing organization, time management, and planning (OTMP), which employ multi-component delivery to target the behavioral manifestations of EF difficulties (Rios-Davis et al., 2023 ; Sibley et al., 2016 ). Organizational Skills Training (OST), for instance, integrates skills sessions for youth with parent and teacher consultations to promote everyday use of these strategies (Bikic et al., 2017 ; Nissley-Tsiopinis et al., 2024 ). Such EF-focused programs yield moderate-to-large gains in organizational behaviors, whereas effects on core inattention and academic achievement are generally modest (Bikic et al., 2017 ; Nissley-Tsiopinis et al., 2024 ). Specialized CBT for adolescents that integrates metacognitive, emotion-regulation, and planning strategies also shows promise for EF-related outcomes in meta-analyses; yet individual trial results remain inconsistent, particularly for core ADHD symptoms (Haugan et al., 2022 ; Qiu et al., 2023 ). Similarly, computerized cognitive training targeting domain-specific difficulties (e.g., working memory) often produces robust near-transfer to trained tasks but limited far-transfer to everyday functioning, academic outcomes, or core ADHD symptoms, particularly in masked evaluations (Veloso et al., 2020 ; Westwood et al., 2023 ). These findings motivate interest in metacognitive approaches such as Goal Management Training (GMT), which includes strategies for monitoring and sustaining goal-directed behavior in real-world contexts (Lenartowicz et al., 2024 ; Stamenova & Levine, 2019 ). GMT, a group-based, manualized intervention grounded in models of goal neglect and supervisory attentional control, is primarily a cognitive rehabilitation intervention using metacognitive strategy training to address executive difficulties through goal management, attentional control, and self-monitoring (Duncan et al., 1996 ; Norman & Shallice, 1986 ; Robertson & Levine, 2013 ). The hypothesized GMT change mechanism is improved metacognitive control of goal-directed behavior by training participants to interrupt automatic responding, reactivate task goals, and monitor ongoing behavior against those goals (Robertson & Levine, 2013 ). The intervention uses the core “stop-and-think” strategy and mindfulness-based attention regulation to support these processes (Kabat-Zinn, 2005 ; Levine & Stamenova, 2018 ). Originally developed to target EF difficulties following acquired brain injury (Robertson, 1996 ), GMT has since been adapted for a range of neurological and psychiatric conditions (Haugen et al., 2021 ; In de Braek et al., 2017 ; Jensen et al., 2021 ; Stubberud et al., 2013 ). GMT has shown promise for improving everyday EF and emotional outcomes in adults with ADHD. One study reported that GMT added to psychoeducation and counseling significantly improved clinician-rated cognitive functioning (In de Braek et al., 2017 ). An exploratory study found enhancements in performance-based inhibitory control, with gains maintained at six-month follow-up, alongside self-reported improvements in productivity and ADHD symptoms (Jensen et al., 2021 ). In a randomized controlled trial (RCT), GMT did not yield additional gains in perceived EF relative to routine care but was associated with a greater reduction in anxiety symptoms (Hanssen et al., 2023 ). Despite these encouraging findings in adult populations, GMT for adolescents remains largely unexplored. A small pilot study ( n = 6) demonstrated good adherence, reductions in functional difficulties, and preliminary improvements in everyday EF (Sadeghi et al., 2024 ), underscoring the need for larger controlled studies in this age group.

### Changing characteristics, treatment patterns, and risk among children and young people with a first recorded common mental disorder symptom or diagnosis in primary care in England (2001-23): a retrospective, population-based cohort study
**כתב עת:** ⭐ The Lancet Psychiatry (Lancet Psychiatry) *(IF: 64.3)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Taxiarchi VP et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Dec

BACKGROUND: Presentations to primary care by children and young people (CYP) for common mental disorders (anxiety and depression) have risen steeply in the past two decades. In this study, we aimed to describe changes in the case mix of CYP with an incident record of a common mental disorder, treatment patterns after the incident record, and whether these trends were accompanied by secular changes in the demographics of those presenting, their treatment patterns, and whether the clinical risk profile of those presenting changed between 2001 and 2023 in England.

METHODS: In this retrospective, population-based cohort study, we used anonymised primary care health records data from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink Aurum (2001-23). We identified children and adolescents (aged 10-18 years) registered with English general practices with linked hospital admission and mortality records. We estimated trends in incidence of common mental disorders (using both symptom and diagnosis codes) in primary care and, among CYP with an incident record of a common mental disorder, changes in demographic and comorbidity profiles, referrals to Child And Adolescent Mental Health Services (CAMHS), and antidepressant prescribing. We compared CYP with incident common mental disorders to matched controls on hospital admission for self-harm, alcohol misuse, and drug misuse, and tested for change in these associations using common mental disorder-by-calendar-year interactions. People with lived experience were not involved in the design or conduct of this study.

FINDINGS: Between Jan 1, 2001, and Dec 31, 2023, 6 220 923 CYP were registered with CPRD-Aurum practices. 379 802 CYP had a first recorded common mental disorder symptom or diagnosis. 250 315 (65·9%) of 379 802 CYP were girls and 129 353 (34·1%) were boys, with a mean age of 15·2 years (range 10-18; SD 2·1), 304 544 (80·2%) were White, and 35 082 (19·2%) were from minoritised ethnic groups. From 2001, the incidence of common mental disorders increased by four times from 57 per 10 000 person-years to 240 in 2019, followed by a decrease to 163 in 2023. The age of CYP with an incident record of a common mental disorder became younger (mean age 15·5 years [SD 2·1] to 15·0 [SD 2·1]), the proportion of White CYP became increasingly overrepresented relative to the general population, and the prevalence of psychiatric or neurodevelopmental comorbidities almost tripled (5·1% in 2001, 13·8% in 2023). After an incident common mental disorder, CAMHS referrals increased from 6·8 to 17·8 per 100 person-years (rate ratio [RR] 2·64 [95% CI 2·48 to 2·79]); antidepressant prescribing halved (38·8 to 17·9 per 100 person-years; RR 0·46 [0·45-0·48]), while prescriptions per treated CYP increased from 4·1 to 6·2 (RR 1·50, 1·48- 1·52). Between 2001 and 2019, the association between common mental disorders and adverse outcomes in the 2 years after the index date remained broadly stable over time (ratio of hazard ratios [HRs] per calendar year 1·01 [95% CI 1·00-1·02] for hospital admission for self-harm; 1·00 [0·99-1·01] for alcohol misuse; 1·01 [0·99-1·02] for drug misuse).

INTERPRETATION: The increase in recorded common mental disorders among CYP was not accompanied by declining clinical risk as would be expected if young people were increasingly being recorded at lower levels of severity, whether through readier presentation to primary care, greater clinical recognition, or more liberal recording. These findings highlight the need for greater consistency, better recording of psychometric measures, and transparency in care pathways.

FUNDING: NIHR, Wellcome Trust, Place2Be.

### Cannabidiol for Severe Behavioral Problems in Youths With Intellectual Disability: A Randomized Clinical Trial
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Pediatrics (JAMA Pediatr) *(IF: 27.6)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Efron D et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 16

IMPORTANCE: There is a high level of interest in cannabidiol (CBD) as treatment for severe behavioral problems in children with intellectual disabilities. Given the lack of evidence, clinical trials are needed to inform practice.

OBJECTIVE: To investigate the efficacy of CBD to reduce severe behavioral problems in children and adolescents with intellectual disability.

DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS: This parallel design, double-blind placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial was conducted between May 11, 2021, and May 8, 2025. Participants were children and adolescents aged 6 to 18 years with an intellectual disability and severe behavioral problems from 3 children's hospitals in Australia: The Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne, Monash Children's Hospital in Melbourne, and The Children's Hospital Westmead in Sydney.

INTERVENTION: Participants were randomly assigned 1:1 to receive CBD isolate in oil (10 mg/kg/d) or placebo orally for 8 weeks, with a 4-week follow-up period.

MAIN OUTCOME AND MEASURE: The primary outcome was parent-reported score on the Aberrant Behavior Checklist, Irritability (ABC-I) subscale assessed at the end of the 8-week maintenance phase.

RESULTS: Of 131 participants randomized after exclusion of 1 participant randomized in error, 65 were in the CBD group and 66 were in the placebo group. Overall, mean (SD) age was 11.2 (3.1) years, and 98 participants (74.8%) were male. A total of 110 participants (84.0%) had comorbid autism. Primary outcome data were available for 61 participants in the CBD group and 63 in the placebo group. Mean (SD) ABC-I subscale scores at baseline were 32.2 (7.3) and 30.0 (6.3) in the CBD and placebo groups, respectively. Intention-to-treat analysis showed similar mean (SD) ABC-I subscale scores at the end of treatment: 21.7 (11.0) in the CBD group and 19.2 (10.7) in the placebo group (mean difference, 2.4; 95% CI, -1.5 to 6.3). A similar proportion of participants experienced at least 1 adverse event in the CBD group (41 [63.1%]) and the placebo group (40 [60.6%]).

CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE: In this randomized clinical trial, CBD was not effective in treating severe behavioral problems in youths with intellectual disability. The results suggest that CBD should not be used to treat severe behavioral problems in this population.

TRIAL REGISTRATION: ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04821856.

### School-Based Health Centers and Student Health Care Use, Need, and Health Outcomes
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Pediatrics (JAMA Pediatr) *(IF: 27.6)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Kranz AM et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 16

This cross-sectional study evaluates 10 years of survey data to determine the association of access to school-based health centers with health, unmet health care need, and health care receipt for high school students in Oregon.

Key Points Question Does having access to a school-based health center (SBHC) improve the physical and mental health outcomes of high school students? Findings This cross-sectional study using Oregon data found that SBHC access reduced suicidal thoughts, suicide attempts, and unmet emotional health care need among students in grade 11. No changes in physical health or unmet physical health care need were observed. Meaning SBHCs may help improve the mental health of high school students.

Importance School-based health centers (SBHCs) are health clinics located in schools that provide comprehensive primary medical care at low or no cost to students. SBHCs are well positioned to improve health outcomes and health care use by eliminating transportation and cost barriers and to reduce disparities by primarily serving communities with low household incomes and high health care needs. Objective To determine the association of SBHC access with health, unmet health care need, and health care receipt for high school students in Oregon. Design, Setting, and Participants This cross-sectional study used 10 years of student survey data during 2006 to 2023 linked to information on SBHC access in high schools in Oregon. Participants were students in 11th grade statewide who took the health survey. Using entropy-balanced staggered difference-in-differences regressions, we estimated the association of having an SBHC at a student’s school with their outcomes. Data analysis was performed from July to September 2026. Exposure School-level measure of whether an SBHC was present in a high school in Oregon. Main Outcomes and Measures Three measures of mental health and suicidality, 2 measures of physical health, 2 measures of unmet health care need, and 1 measure of medical care received. Results Among 87 871 students at 180 schools, 43 397 (49.7%) were female, 42 824 (49.0%) were male, and 1157 (1.3%) were another gender. A total of 14 933 students attended a school with an SBHC during the study period. SBHC access was associated with statistically significant improvements in student mental health, with decreases in suicidal thoughts (−2.7 percentage points [pp]; 95% CI, −4.5 to −0.7 pp) and suicide attempts (−1.4 pp; 95% CI, −2.3 to −0.5 pp) and decreases in reports of unmet emotional health care needs (−2.1 pp; 95% CI, −3.8 to −0.6 pp). No other statistically significant associations with SBHC access were observed. Heterogeneous treatment effects by gender, sexual orientation, race and ethnicity, and rurality were observed. Conclusions and Relevance This study found that SBHC access was associated with improved mental health among high school students. There are nearly 4000 SBHCs in the United States, and they are primarily in schools with a high share of students from low-income families. Expanding access to SBHCs may help reduce mental health disparities.

Introduction School-based health centers (SBHCs) are health centers located in schools that offer a range of physical, behavioral, and preventive health services at low or no cost. There are nearly 4000 SBHCs nationwide. SBHCs offer an opportunity to reduce disparities in health outcomes and access to care, as schools with SBHCs have a higher share of students from low-income households and Black and Hispanic students compared with schools without SBHCs. Much of the evidence about the impacts of SBHCs on health care use and health outcomes is based on studies examining correlations. Among more rigorous evaluations of SBHCs, there are mixed findings on the associations of receipt of health care and health outcomes, likely due to the variety of contexts and outcomes examined. Nationwide research suggests that SBHC openings reduce teen birth rates, but may not change high school dropout rates, and that the opening of an SBHC in a county increases the likelihood of an adolescent having a usual source of care, but does not change receipt of a mental health visit or improve overall health. Positive changes on students’ mental health following SBHC openings were observed in Tennessee and Oregon, with reductions in emergency department (ED) visits for mental health concerns and increased outpatient mental health visits among youth insured by Medicaid after the opening of an SBHC in a district in Tennessee and reductions in student-reported depression, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts among high school students after the expansion of mental health services in existing SBHCs in Oregon. However, a study of SBHCs offering mental health care in California found no changes in student-reported depression and suicidality. Nearly all of these studies looked at SBHC access at the school district or county level, which may understate the benefits that students attending schools with an on-site SBHC experience. The 2 studies that examined access to on-site SBHCs may have limited generalizability because one examined the expansion of services at existing SBHCs and the other examined a subtype of SBHCs that offer mental health care with or without primary care services. Our study extends previous work by examining SBHC access among students attending these schools and by examining a broad array of measures of physical and mental health. The objective of this study was to determine the changes in physical and mental health outcomes, unmet health care need, and receipt of care for high school students in Oregon after an SBHC opens. We used 10 years of survey data and a difference-in-differences study design to compare outcomes for students in grade 11 in schools with an SBHC to schools that did not have an SBHC during the study period. Both are statewide surveys that provide information about the health and health behaviors of students in grade 11. These data were obtained from the Oregon Health Authority with school identifiers, allowing us to link on information about SBHCs. Information on Oregon’s SBHCs, including school location and open (and close) date, was obtained from the Oregon Health Authority SBHC program. Data analysis was performed from July to September 2026. SBHCs are required to have a primary care clinician, such as a physician, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant, working on-site at least 10 hours per week and at least 2 days per week. They are also required to have a qualified mental health professional, with at least a master’s degree in a mental health field, available on-site or through referral who can provide mental health care, substance use treatment, and other services supporting student emotional well-being. ### Reduced Social Media Use and Change in Internalizing Symptoms Among High-Risk Young Adults
**כתב עת:** ⭐ JAMA Pediatrics (JAMA Pediatr) *(IF: 27.6)* | **סוג מחקר:** מאמר מחקרי | **מחברים:** Han DH et al. | **תאריך:** 2026 Nov 16

Key Points Question Among young adults with elevated internalizing problems and frequent social media use, is reduced social media use associated with subsequent improvement in internalizing symptoms? Findings In this cohort study of a nationally representative sample of 2358 US young adults (3788 observations) with elevated internalizing symptoms and frequent social media use at baseline, reductions in social media use frequency were associated with subsequent improvement in internalizing symptoms. Meaning The findings suggest that reducing social media use may represent a modifiable target for prevention and early intervention in young adults with elevated symptoms and frequent use.

This cohort study evaluates associations between reduced social media use and changes in internalizing problems among young adults in the US.

Importance National cohort analyses of the association between reduced social media use and subsequent improvement in internalizing problems among high-risk young adults—ie, those most likely to benefit from intervention—remain limited. Objective To assess whether reduced social media use is associated with improvements in internalizing problems among US young adults with frequent social media use and elevated internalizing problems. Design, Setting, and Participants This cohort study used data from the Population Assessment of Tobacco and Health Study (2013-2021). Repeated observations were organized into 4 consecutive, overlapping 3-wave intervals, each comprising 3 sequential survey waves ( t , t + 1, and t + 2); for example, interval 1 included waves 1-3, and interval 4 included waves 4-6. Young adults aged 18 to 24 years with elevated internalizing problems (all 4 symptoms) and frequent social media use (several times a day) at wave t were included. Data were analyzed from February to August 2026. Exposures Self-reported social media use frequency at wave t + 1 (nonfrequent [≤once daily] vs frequent [several times daily]). Main Outcomes and Measures Past-year internalizing problems (nonelevated [≤3 symptoms] vs elevated [4 symptoms]) at wave t + 2 via the Global Appraisal of Individual Needs–Short Screener (depression, anxiety, psychological distress, sleep difficulties). Results A total of 3788 person-interval observations (2561 [64.7%] female; aged 18-24 years at baseline) were included. Approximately 14% (n = 540) transitioned to nonfrequent use at t + 1. Reduced use was associated with greater likelihood of nonelevated internalizing symptoms at t + 2 (0.57 vs 0.49; prevalence difference, 0.07; 95% CI, 0.02-0.13; prevalence ratio, 1.15; 95% CI, 1.04-1.27). Findings were generally consistent across sensitivity analyses, including alternative exposure definitions (weekly or less vs several times daily social media use: prevalence ratio, 1.17; 95% CI, 1.04-1.33), alternative missing-data handling approaches (prevalence ratio, 1.14; 95% CI, 1.04-1.25), design-based analyses (prevalence ratio, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.06-1.28), concurrent models (prevalence ratio, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.01-1.26), and alternative outcome definitions using internalizing symptom counts, with non-frequent use associated with fewer symptoms (adjusted incidence rate ratio, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.85-0.96). Conclusions and Relevance In this cohort study, reduced social media use was associated with subsequent improvement in internalizing symptoms among US young adults with elevated symptoms and frequent use. These findings identify reduced social media use as a potentially modifiable target for prevention and early intervention in this high-risk population.
